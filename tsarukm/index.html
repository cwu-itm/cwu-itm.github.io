Systems Thinking and Model-Based Design in Complex Digital Systems

Digital systems are becoming more complex every year. Websites, mobile apps, online stores, banking systems, and social media platforms can include many different parts. These parts must work together to create a useful experience for the user. Because of this complexity, designers cannot focus only on one screen, button, or feature. They need to understand how all parts of the system connect. This is where systems thinking becomes important. Systems thinking helps designers look at the whole system instead of looking at each part separately. It also helps them understand how users interact with the system. A model-based approach supports this process because models can make a complex system easier to understand, test, and improve.

What Is Systems Thinking?

In my opinion, systems thinking means looking at something as a whole and understanding how its different parts work together. Instead of studying one part by itself, a person looks at the relationships between the parts. A change in one area can affect many other areas. Because of this, the goal is not only to make each individual part work well. The goal is to make the entire system work well.

The International Council on Systems Engineering describes systems thinking as looking at the big picture and understanding how parts of a system are connected. It explains that the relationships between different parts can be just as important as the parts themselves (INCOSE, n.d.).

A digital shopping website is a simple example. It may include a home page, search system, product pages, shopping cart, payment system, customer account, and order tracking. These parts may seem separate, but they depend on each other. For example, if the shopping cart does not communicate correctly with the payment system, the customer may not be able to complete an order. A systems thinker would not only ask whether the shopping cart works. They would also ask how the cart connects with payment, inventory, user accounts, and other parts of the system.

Systems thinking is also important because digital systems include people. Users have expectations about how websites and applications should behave. Jeff Johnson explains that interface design should be based on knowledge of human perception, memory, learning, and decision-making. Understanding these mental processes helps designers make better design decisions instead of simply following design rules without understanding them (Johnson, 2020).

For example, a designer may technically be able to place a large amount of information on one screen. However, that does not mean users can easily understand it. Human attention and memory have limits. Therefore, the designer needs to think about both the technical system and the person using it. In this way, good systems thinking includes technology, design, users, and the relationships between them.

Systems Thinking Compared with Linear Thinking

Systems thinking is different from traditional linear thinking because it looks at the whole situation instead of focusing on one direct cause and effect. Linear thinking usually follows a simple path, such as A causes B, and B causes C. This approach can work well for simple problems. For example, if a button on a website does not work because it has the wrong link, the designer can correct the link and solve the problem. However, complex digital systems often have problems that do not have one clear cause. For example, if customers leave an online store before completing their purchase, several factors could be responsible. The checkout process may have too many steps, the website may load slowly, users may not trust the payment page, or the shipping cost may appear too late. Systems thinking looks at all these factors and considers how they may work together. Instead of only asking, “What is broken?” the designer asks, “What parts of the system may be creating this result?”

This idea also connects with Jon Yablonski’s discussion of complexity in Laws of UX. Yablonski explains Tesler’s Law, also called the law of conservation of complexity. This law states that every system has some complexity that cannot simply disappear. The complexity must be handled either by the system or by the user (Yablonski, 2024). This means that removing features does not always make a digital system easier to use. Designers need to decide where the complexity should be handled. In many situations, the system should complete more of the difficult work so that the user has an easier experience. At the same time, designers should not simplify an interface so much that important information is lost (Yablonski, 2024). This shows an important difference between linear and systems thinking. Linear thinking often searches for one problem and one solution, while systems thinking considers how different decisions can affect the entire user experience.

Why a Model-Based Approach Is Useful

A model-based approach is useful when designing complex digital systems because a model provides a simpler way to understand a large system. A model can show important parts of a system, their relationships, user actions, data, and processes without requiring the team to build the complete system first. Models can include user flows, diagrams, wireframes, and prototypes. For example, a banking application may include login security, bank accounts, money transfers, bill payments, notifications, personal information, and customer support. If each feature is designed separately, the final system may have problems when the features are connected. A model can show how users move between these areas and how information moves through the system. This can help the team notice problems earlier. INCOSE explains that systems engineering uses a holistic approach to make sure that different parts of a complex system work together successfully (INCOSE, n.d.). Models support this approach because they make important connections easier to see and understand.

A model-based approach also improves communication and helps designers focus on the user. Complex digital projects may include UX designers, developers, project managers, business leaders, and other stakeholders. Each person may have a different understanding of the system. A shared model gives the team a common picture of how the system should work. Models can also help teams test ideas before spending large amounts of time and money on development. This is especially important when considering how people interact with technology. Johnson explains that designers should understand the psychological reasons behind design guidelines so they can make better design decisions (Johnson, 2020). Yablonski also explains that successful digital products should work with the way people perceive and process information instead of forcing users to adjust to the technology (Yablonski, 2024). Therefore, models should represent not only technical parts of a system but also user needs, behaviors, and expectations.

Conclusion

Systems thinking is important in the design of complex digital systems because it helps designers understand the system as a whole. Unlike linear thinking, which often focuses on one cause and one solution, systems thinking considers the relationships between many different parts. A problem in one area may affect several other areas of the system. Designers also need to consider the people who use the system. Human attention, memory, expectations, and behavior can affect whether a digital product is easy or difficult to use. Looking at all these factors together can help designers make better decisions and create systems that work more effectively for users.

A model-based approach works well with systems thinking because it makes complex relationships easier to understand. Models allow teams to organize information, study connections, communicate ideas, and test possible solutions before building the final product. Johnson shows why human thinking and perception should influence interface design, while Yablonski explains how psychology and complexity influence the user experience. INCOSE provides the larger systems perspective by showing the importance of understanding relationships between different parts of a system. Together, these ideas show why complex digital products should not be designed as separate features. They should be designed as connected systems in which technology, information, and people work together.

Module 1.2 Blog Post

Understanding the Systems Development Life Cycle and Its Importance in Digital Systems

Introduction

Digital systems are a very important part of our everyday lives. We use websites, mobile applications, online banking, shopping systems, and many other technologies almost every day. However, creating these systems requires much more than just writing code. Developers need to understand what users need, plan the system, design it, test it, release it, and continue improving it. This process can be organized through the Systems Development Life Cycle. In my opinion, having a structured life cycle makes the development process easier to understand and manage. There are different life-cycle models, including linear, iterative, and incremental models. Each of them has its own characteristics, advantages, and disadvantages. I think understanding these models is important because a digital system should not only work when it is first created but should also be able to improve and change in the future.

What Is the Systems Development Life Cycle?

From my understanding, the Systems Development Life Cycle is a structured process that helps teams plan, create, test, implement, and maintain digital systems. In simple words, it is like a guide that developers can follow from the beginning of a project until the system is released and maintained. Instead of immediately starting to build something, developers first need to understand the problem and what users actually need. According to IBM (2025), the software development life cycle commonly includes stages such as planning, analysis, design, development, testing, deployment, and maintenance. Each stage has its own purpose. For example, planning establishes the goals, while analysis helps identify requirements. After that, the system can be designed, developed, tested, and eventually released.

I think one of the most important things about the SDLC is that the process does not necessarily end when the product is released. A digital system may need maintenance, security updates, bug fixes, and new features in the future. Users can also change the way they interact with technology. Here we can see that developers have to think about more than just creating the first version of a product. They should also consider what can happen to the system later. That is why I think the SDLC is useful for both creating and maintaining digital systems.

Linear Life-Cycle Model

The linear life-cycle model, which is also commonly called the waterfall model, follows a clear order. Usually, one stage is completed before the next stage begins. For example, the team completes planning and requirements before moving to design. After design comes development, testing, and deployment. One advantage of this approach is that it is easy to understand because the project has a clear structure. Team members can see what needs to be completed and what comes next. Atlassian (n.d.) explains that the waterfall approach works best when requirements are clearly understood and are not expected to change significantly. For example, I think this model could work well for a simple employee attendance system where the organization already knows exactly what the system needs to do.

However, in my opinion, the clear structure can also become one of the biggest disadvantages of the linear model. If users decide that they want something different after development has already started, going back and changing earlier work can take more time and money. Some problems may also be discovered later because users may not interact with the finished system until later stages. Here we can see that having a very organized process does not always mean that it is the most flexible process. That is why I can assume that the linear model is a good option for projects with stable requirements, but it may not be the best choice for digital products that need frequent changes.

Iterative Life-Cycle Model

The iterative life-cycle model is more flexible because developers do not try to create a perfect final product immediately. Instead, they create a version of the system and then improve it through repeated cycles. During these cycles, developers can design, test, collect feedback, and make changes. For example, a company could create an early version of a mobile shopping application and allow users to test it. Users might say that the navigation is confusing or that certain buttons are difficult to find. Developers could then use this feedback to improve the next version. I think this is helpful because developers can actually see how people use the system instead of only assuming how they will use it.

In my opinion, one of the biggest advantages of the iterative model is that problems can be discovered earlier and improvements can be made throughout development. However, there are also disadvantages. Repeatedly changing and testing a product can require additional time and resources. The project may also become difficult to manage if the team continues making changes without having a clear final goal. Here we can see that flexibility is useful, but it still needs to be controlled. That is why I think iterative development is especially useful when the developers do not fully understand all user needs at the beginning of a project.

Incremental Life-Cycle Model

The incremental life-cycle model focuses on creating a system in smaller functional parts. Instead of waiting until the entire product is completed, developers can release different features step by step. For example, an online shopping website could first include a product catalog and customer accounts. The next increment could introduce a shopping cart and online payments. Later, developers could add order tracking, customer reviews, and personalized recommendations. In my opinion, this approach can make a large project feel more manageable because the development team does not need to complete everything at the same time. Users can also begin using important parts of the system earlier.

However, incremental development can also create challenges. All of the separate parts eventually have to work together as one complete system. If the original system is poorly designed, adding new features can become more difficult. Developers also need to carefully decide which features should be developed first. Here we can see an important difference between iterative and incremental development. Iterative development focuses more on improving something through repeated versions, while incremental development focuses on adding new parts to the system. That is why I can assume that incremental development can be a good option for larger projects where the organization wants to provide useful features to users without waiting for the entire system to be finished.

Importance of Life-Cycle Thinking in Digital Systems

I think thinking about the entire system life cycle is very important because digital systems do not stay the same forever. Technology changes, businesses grow, security risks appear, and users develop new expectations. A system that works perfectly today might not work as well several years from now. Because of this, developers should consider future changes while they are designing a system. In my opinion, this is also connected to sustainability. If a digital system is designed so that it can be maintained and updated, an organization may not have to completely replace it every time something changes. This can save time, money, and other resources. Security is another good example because digital systems need regular updates to protect information from new threats.

User experience is also connected to the system life cycle. Yablonski (2024) discusses how understanding human psychology can help designers create digital products that are easier for people to use. I think this idea is important throughout the whole life of a system, not only during its original design. For example, when developers add new features to an application, they should consider whether those features make the application easier or more confusing for users. Here we can see that adding more features does not automatically make a digital system better. That is why I think developers should continue testing and thinking about user needs as the system evolves. From this, I can assume that life-cycle thinking helps digital systems remain useful, secure, understandable, and sustainable for a longer period of time.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the Systems Development Life Cycle provides an organized way to plan, design, develop, test, release, and maintain digital systems. Linear, iterative, and incremental models provide different ways of managing this process. The linear model is organized and predictable, but it can make changes more difficult. The iterative model allows developers to improve a product through repeated testing and feedback, but it may require more time and resources. The incremental model allows developers to build and release a system in smaller parts, but all of those parts need to work together correctly. In my opinion, there is no single life-cycle model that will work perfectly for every project. The best choice depends on the requirements, complexity, users, and goals of the system.

Overall, I think the most important idea is that developers should not think only about creating a system and releasing it. They should also think about what will happen to that system in the future. Digital products need maintenance, security updates, improvements, and changes as technology develops. Here we can see why thinking about the complete system life cycle is so important. It helps developers prepare for future needs instead of only solving today’s problems. That is why I can assume that understanding the system life cycle can lead to digital systems that are more useful, sustainable, adaptable, and easier to maintain over time.
