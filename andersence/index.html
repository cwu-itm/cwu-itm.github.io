Systems-Thinking

Introduction

A system is defined as a set of things working together as parts of a mechanism or an interconnecting network. There are a number of components within a system, and none of them operate in isolation. These components interact with each other, so altering part of the system can easily influence the other parts. Systems thinking involves taking a step back and evaluating a system as a whole, rather than focusing on its individual aspects.

This idea is further supported by Southern New Hampshire University, which defines systems thinking as a holistic approach where one focuses on the influence parts of a system have on others, and how their interactions affect the system at large (Morganelli, 2024). Thus, individual parts within a system are often best understood when we look at the relationships they have with one another, as opposed to looking at each one under a microscope (Holt, 2021).

We can look at modern cars as an example of this, as they rely on a vast number of mechanical parts as well as software and communication networks to operate effectively and safely. A problem with one part can prevent other parts from working as intended. Viewing the car through a systemic lens allows us to understand how each part depends on others. Holt (2021) reminds us of how complex modern vehicles can be, highlighting the interactions with outside systems, such as satellites, cloud services, and other complex infrastructure. So, the boundaries of the system aren’t always obvious because the car might depend on outside technology. If we only consider the individual parts within the car itself, we’d be leaving out crucial relationships that allow the entire system to function properly.

Systems v. Linear Thinking

The way that we typically aim to problem solve is through linear paths, assuming one problem has a direct cause and solution. The relationships between different events in the system are looked at as moving in one path; one event happens, which triggers another and another. Approaching things this way can be effective when dealing with simple problems but isn’t always optimal when we try to apply it to complex systems. In comparison, systems thinking looks at how these parts can influence each other in both directions (National Institute of Standards and Technology [NIST], 2022). From a systems thinking standpoint, one would acknowledge that an issue could have numerous causes and that simply trying to fix a malfunctioning part can cause undesired effects elsewhere within that system.

If we take the example of a company noticing a high number of customers abandoning their online shopping carts during checkout, a linear approach would look at the most plausible cause. Maybe they observe that the checkout page is running particularly slowly. Linear thinking would aim to solve the issue by improving speed. Systems thinking would still take speed into account but would also focus on other factors that could be contributing to the issue, like navigation issues, device compatibility, etc., while also considering how changing the checkout process would affect other parts within the system.

The checkout page could be slow, but increasing its speed wouldn’t solve the problem if the customers are leaving for other reasons, like payment malfunctions or bad performance on mobile devices. Even though the page’s speed would improve, there could be issues left unsolved if the customers are abandoning their online shopping carts for any of those other reasons mentioned. The linear solution can sometimes be correct, but the distinction between the two ways of thinking is that systems thinking allows us to have a deeper understanding of the issue before deciding that there’s one cause and solution. This is essentially why systems thinking is so important; we must think about how a decision could impact the whole system before making changes.

Model-Based Approach

Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE) uses models that represent systems to help us develop a better understanding of them. The models can include visual representations of different system components and how they relate to one another (Holt, 2021). This approach is especially useful today as digital systems are becoming increasingly complex. Software, hardware, databases, etc., all intertwine, and as the number of components and connections increases, it can become quite the task to keep track of these interactions. Models allow us to organize the large amounts of information needed to understand the system, as well as assist designers in understanding the possible outcomes involved in making a change to its specific parts.

One of the greatest benefits of a model-based approach is the ability to keep important system information connected and organized. A lot of traditional approaches involve separate documents, spreadsheets, etc. containing important information, and as the system and project become more complex, it becomes harder to maintain consistency. According to SEBoK, MBSE aims to bring system information together in a singular model which allows different stakeholders to access project information in a shared source (Singam & Carter, 2026). As a result, there is less room for miscommunication and other potential errors or inconsistencies in the design process, which is beneficial for all parties. Models allow designers to see issues early on and avoid mistakes that could be costly and time-consuming.

Another advantage of model-based approaches is improving communication between different stakeholders. There can be many different roles involved in working on a system, and people can have different perceptions on what should be prioritized. Holt (2021) covers this, stating that stakeholders often interpret the same system differently based on their perspectives. Shared models give everyone a representation of the whole system and can help overcome these differences in perspectives by outlining how individual priorities fit with the larger project. Decisions made by one group can directly impact the work and concerns of other groups, so taking this approach can be very useful in ensuring things flow correctly.

Reflection

Systems thinking allows us to look beyond a singular issue. The focus becomes how all the parts work together and how they may be affecting each other. Although linear thinking can be useful when addressing simple problems, it isn’t a great strategy in systems where multiple causes and effects could be involved. One of the biggest takeaways for me is that oftentimes the problem that seems the most obvious isn’t the only concern. As Holt (2021) explains, complexity is often found in the relationships between system elements. Making a change before understanding the relationships could just lead to additional problems. McConnell explains that we should first zoom in to the individual parts of a problem to understand it and then zoom out to evaluate its connections to the larger problem (Project Management Institute [PMI], 2023).

Using a model-based approach is a great way to put systems thinking into practice by providing a visual understanding of important system elements and how they interact, giving all parties a clearer picture of what is actually going on. When people work together to ensure that important components are working properly in isolation as well as in relation to others, a better product can be produced, and risks can become easier to manage. This is important for digital systems as they continue to increase in complexity. The more complex the system, the higher the likelihood of problems spiraling when parts of the system are not working properly. Seeing and understanding the relationship between individual aspects within the system before making decisions can ultimately make the design of the system better. Overall, this has made me look at problem-solving in a different light; a solution is only a solution if the original problem is properly understood.

Thinking in Life Cycles

Introduction

For systems engineers, creating something that works well is only one part of the process. In addition to creating a functional system, that system needs to be tested and maintained, and over time may need to be replaced. The purpose of the systems development life cycle is to organize all the steps and understand how a system changes.

In Systems Engineering Demystified, Holt explains that there are six main stages within a system’s lifecycle. These stages are conception, development, production, utilization, support, and retirement (Holt, 2023). These stages outline the entire process, from thinking of an idea, to developing, using, maintaining, and eventually putting the product to rest.

Note that there are several ways to approach system development. There are other life cycle models that influence how each of these stages are handled. Some models have a specific order, while others are more fluid. The three main approaches include linear, iterative, and incremental life cycle models. There are pros and cons to each approach, and some are better suited to certain projects than others.

Linear Life Cycle Model

This life cycle model is a relatively straightforward way to approach systems development. It follows a sequence, requiring each stage to be completed before moving onto the next one. Consider the Waterfall Model, one of the most established examples of a linear life cycle model. The development process is divided into separate phases that can include determining requirements, designing, building, testing, and so on. It is referred to as a waterfall because of the way that the process flows in a downward direction, like water flowing over the waterfall. Every phase builds upon completed work within the previous one. Like other linear life cycle models, going back to previous phases can be difficult (Holt, 2023). Consequently, using this model requires developers to plan thoroughly and understand the project’s scope before beginning the process of development.

Although the somewhat rigid structure within linear models can seem a little restrictive, there are many advantages it can provide. Having a clear structure and process makes the linear life cycle model easy to manage and organize in comparison to others. Each phase has specific goals which help developers establish deadlines, evaluate the progress being made, and know what to expect as the project continues. This approach is particularly effective when used on smaller projects that have a clear scope and requirements that will more than likely stay the same.

While this approach can work well for smaller projects, it can create problems when dealing with larger or more complex systems. GeeksforGeeks (2026) explains that when using the Waterfall Model, testing typically occurs rather late in the development process. So, a potential disadvantage to linear development is that problems are sometimes discovered when most of the work has already been completed. This becomes problematic when developers have already put a lot of work into the project, causing frustration, delays, and extra costs. Certain projects involve more uncertainty and require flexibility throughout the development process, making the linear model less suitable.

Iterative Life Cycle Model

Instead of following a sequential process that goes from beginning to end, the iterative model goes through the stages of development several times, which lets developers make changes and improve the system throughout the process. The repeated cycles are referred to as iterations. Each iteration aims to produce a better version of the system than the previous version (Holt, 2023).

Iterative development is similar to the process of writing an essay for school. You create a rough draft of the paper, go over it, and find out what needs to be changed or adjusted. You then make those changes and read through it again. This cycle repeats itself until you have a final draft that you are happy with before you consider it ready for submission. Iterative approaches give developers room to go back and fix mistakes, along with making other improvements as the project continues.

The flexibility of this model is one of the biggest advantages of using it. The system gets reviewed and improved throughout development. This means that problems can be addressed along the way, and feedback can be obtained by the people who would use the system. Holt (2023) also notes that sometimes developers can return to an older version of a system when a new version runs into serious problems.

This all sounds ideal, but sometimes this level of flexibility can cause problems too. If various stakeholders request changes too frequently, it can become more difficult for developers to stay on schedule, and the scope of the project can become unclear. They may have a hard time deciding when the project is actually finished. Additionally, frequently having new releases can make developers feel pressured to continue pushing out new things when they haven’t had enough time to properly test and improve the version that was already out (Holt, 2023). In other words, despite this being a useful model when systems need to be adjusted, it doesn’t eliminate the need for proper planning.

Incremental Life Cycle Model

The Incremental Life Cycle Model is like the Iterative Model in that it allows systems to be changed and developed over time. The distinction is that the incremental model is centered around building and adding things to a system, rather than improving what is already there. Each addition to the system is referred to as an increment. According to Holt (2023), with this approach, system needs are identified in the beginning stages of the process. The actual development and production of deliverables come after and are completed in portions. With that, some parts of the system can be used before everything else is finished.

A practical way to understand this is if you think about building a website to sell products from your company. At first, the website may only have basic features, like an inventory catalogue and a checkout process. This would be the first increment. Afterwards, other features can be added like wishlists, reviews, etc. Essentially, with this model a company could release a website that is basic but works, and add things over time, instead of waiting until every element is ready. Thus, an advantage of this model is that a system can be used sooner than it might with other models. In the example I gave, this is particularly beneficial as it would allow the company to start making online sales sooner.

Another advantage is that breaking things up this way can make larger projects easier to navigate. Stakeholders can also choose what features they want to prioritize. Overall, this is a helpful method particularly for complex projects or those that could take a while to complete. There are some disadvantages, however, when it comes to certain projects. Sometimes, for a system to function safely and effectively, all of its parts need to work together. In that case, releasing only one part of the system wouldn’t make sense (Holt, 2023). Also, developers often need to plan ahead when thinking about how the parts within the system will fit together as new things are added. Adcock (2026) explains that life cycle processes need to be selected and applied situationally; they depend on the needs of a system.

Although the ability to develop and release parts individually can be advantageous, we can’t forget that these parts all still need to work together within the larger system. Proper planning is important to avoid problems when you will be combining different parts of the system.

Design, Sustainability, Evolution

When developing a system an important consideration is how well it will function over time, not just how well it works in the present. Even though a system can work exactly as intended at first, there is no guarantee that it will have the same level of functionality as time goes on. This is where design comes into play. The choices made during the design phase directly impact how well the system performs over the years, especially when we think about how easy these decisions make it to update and maintain the system. Chiu and Chu (2012) explain that thinking about sustainability in the design phase is of utmost importance. In other words, the best practice is to think about the long-term impacts of design choices and to not jump the gun when it comes to producing a present working version.

Take the example of a company that is working on a new computer system. If they focus on designing the system so that its parts can be upgraded or replaced individually as problems arise, they may get good use of the system for years. If the system is built in a way that makes it hard to do so, the company may waste resources and create additional expenses. For example, they may have to completely replace the system instead of being able to go in and repair its necessary parts to get things running smoothly. This is the sustainability aspect of things. If a system is designed holistically, considering the future, developers have the opportunity to lower potential waste and costs.

Something else developers should keep in mind is that technology changes, and so do people’s needs. A system/product that worked great just a few years ago could be outdated today. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST, 2009) explains the importance of this when considering cybersecurity. Security threats can pop up and be addressed during development but often emerge after a system or product has already been released. This necessitates updates and maintenance throughout the system’s life course.

The examples provided highlight the importance of long-term thinking when creating a system. The decisions that get made during the design process directly impact the longevity of a system. The life cycle model used in the project plays a big role here. Iterative development makes it easier to introduce changes to the system throughout its time. Similarly, Incremental approaches allow new features to be added as needs change. Both models can help improve a system’s performance and longevity, while also saving resources, lowering waste, and saving money. All in all, planning ahead can make a system useful for a longer time and avoid problems later on.

Final Thoughts

It is important that systems engineers understand the different life cycle models, because there is not a one-size-fits-all. Every approach has advantages and disadvantages, so determining the current and future needs of a system is critical. Regardless of which model gets used, I believe the design process is one of the most important phases of a system’s life cycle. The way a system is designed directly corresponds to how sustainable it is and how long it can remain effective. When thinking about these things, developers can develop systems that withstand the test of time by planning for updates and other maintenance that may be needed in the future. Doing so increases satisfaction across the board for all stakeholders.
