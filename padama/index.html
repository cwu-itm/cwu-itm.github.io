My thoughts about System Thinking

To me, systems thinking means looking at the whole thing instead of just one part. A system has different parts that work together. If something changes in one part, it can sometimes affect another part too. So, you have to think about how everything connects.

Systems and their blocks

In Chapter 1 of Systems Engineering Demystified, it talks about how systems can have many different parts and how those parts are connected. You can't always understand a system by only looking at one part. You have to look at how the parts work together. An example of this would be a car. A car has an engine, wheels, brakes, computers, sensors, and other parts. All of these things have to work together for the car to actually work (Holt & Perry, 2023).

Systems Thinking in IT

The MIT article talks about how having a common understanding of systems thinking can help people from different technology fields work together. I think this makes sense because IT projects usually involve different people working on different parts of a system. Systems thinking can help everyone understand how their part connects to the bigger system (MIT System Design and Management,2024).

Model-Based Systems Enigeering

Chapter 2 talks about Model-Based Systems Engineering, or MBSE. Basically, MBSE uses a model to help show how a system works. The model doesn't have to show every single detail. It just needs to show the important information. The chapter also talks about how the relationships between parts are important, which connects back to systems thinking (Holt & Perry, 2023).

  • Systems have multiple connected parts.
  • Changes to one part can affect other parts.
  • Relationships between parts are important.
  • Systems thinking can help IT teams work together more effectively.

Why Systems Thinking Matters

Richard Buchanan also talks about systems thinking and how the different parts of a system work together toward a common purpose. This connects to the textbook because the textbook also explains that the relationships between parts are important. I think this is useful in IT because technology systems have many connected parts, and changing one part can affect the rest of the system (Buchanan, 2019).

All in all, I think that systems thinking is basically about understanding how everything works together. This is useful in IT and cybersecurity because there are usually a lot of different parts involved. You can't just look at one computer or one piece of software and ignore everything else. You need to think about how the different parts connect and how a change in one area could affect another area.

Module 1.2 Blog Post

Week 2

When people think about creating a new computer system or application, they might mainly think about the programming and design involved. However, developing a system involves much more than just building it. There needs to be planning, testing, implementation, maintenance, and eventually a plan for replacing or retiring the system. These steps are important because a system needs to work properly, meet user needs, and continue working as technology changes. This is where the systems development life cycle comes in.

The systems development life cycle, also known as the SDLC, is the process used to plan, create, test, release, and maintain a system. It provides a general structure for organizing development and making sure important steps are not skipped. IBM (n.d.) explains that the SDLC helps development teams organize the work needed to create software and improve its quality. Systems engineering also considers how different parts of a system work together and how decisions affect the system throughout its life. Understanding the life cycle is important because it helps teams make better decisions, manage risks, and create systems that can continue meeting people's needs.

What Is the Systems Development Life Cycle?

In my own words, the systems development life cycle is the process a system goes through from the first idea to the point when it is no longer needed. It gives developers and other team members a plan for what needs to happen during each stage. Without a clear process, a team could rush into building something without fully understanding what users need. This could lead to mistakes, wasted time, security problems, or a product that does not work as expected.

Although the exact stages can vary depending on the project, the life cycle often includes planning, requirements gathering, design, development, testing, implementation, maintenance, and retirement. During planning, the team identifies the problem that needs to be solved and considers the available time, money, and resources. During requirements gathering, the team determines what the system needs to do. Design explains how the system will work, while development involves building or configuring it. Testing checks whether it works correctly before it is released.

After implementation, the work is not necessarily finished. A system may need updates, bug fixes, security patches, and other improvements. Eventually, it might need to be replaced because it is outdated or no longer meets the needs of its users. NASA (2025) explains that systems engineering covers the development, operation, and retirement of systems. This shows that a system's life cycle includes much more than the initial development stage.

Another important part of the life cycle is communication. Developers, managers, customers, security specialists, and users may all have different expectations. The team needs to understand these expectations and make decisions that balance performance, cost, time, and risk. A system might work technically but still fail if it is too difficult to use or does not solve the original problem. Planning for the entire life cycle helps teams consider these issues before they become expensive problems.

The Linear Model

The linear model, which is commonly associated with the waterfall model, follows a series of stages in a mostly fixed order. The team usually completes planning and requirements before moving to design, development, testing, and implementation. Each stage provides information needed for the next stage. The idea is to establish a clear plan and follow it through the project.

One advantage of the linear model is that it is easy to understand and organize. Everyone can see which stage the project is in and what work needs to be completed. It also creates documentation that can help teams understand the system and track decisions. This can be useful for projects with clear requirements that are not expected to change much. For example, a company replacing an existing system with a similar one might already know most of the features and requirements it needs.

However, the linear model can be difficult to use when requirements change. If a team discovers a major problem during testing, it might need to return to earlier stages and change parts of the design or development work. Those changes can take additional time and money. Users may also have to wait a long time before seeing a working version of the system, which means the team might not discover that something is wrong until late in the project.

For example, imagine a college is developing a new student registration system. If the team decides all the features at the beginning and follows a linear model, it might not discover that students have trouble navigating the registration process until testing. Fixing the problem could require changes to the original design. The linear model can still be useful, but it works best when the team understands the requirements and can keep them relatively stable.

The Iterative Model

The iterative model uses repeated cycles of development, testing, and improvement. Instead of expecting the first version to be perfect, the team creates an early version and uses what it learns to make changes. Each cycle gives the team another chance to identify problems, improve the design, and get closer to the expected result.

One advantage of the iterative model is that it allows teams to learn as they work. They can test ideas early, collect feedback from users, and make changes before too much time is spent on one design. This can be especially helpful when the project involves new technology or when the team does not know every requirement at the beginning. NASA (2025) describes systems engineering processes as being applied iteratively to correct problems and improve a system's development. This supports the idea that repeating processes can help teams find and address issues throughout a project.

The iterative model also has some disadvantages. Repeated testing and changes can increase costs and make it harder to predict exactly when the project will be finished. The team needs to manage feedback carefully so that the project does not keep changing without a clear goal. It is also important to document changes and make sure that improvements in one area do not create problems in another area.

An example would be developing a mobile banking application. The team might create an early version with basic account information and then ask users to test it. Based on their feedback, the team could improve the navigation, make important features easier to find, or fix confusing screens. More testing could reveal additional issues. The application improves through repeated cycles instead of relying on one final test at the end.

The Incremental Model

The incremental model develops a system in smaller parts, or increments. Instead of waiting until every feature is finished, the team builds and releases portions of the system over time. Each increment adds new features or capabilities until the system is complete. The parts need to work together as one system, so the team must plan how they will connect and share information.

A major advantage of the incremental model is that users can start using important features earlier. The team can focus on the most necessary functions first and add other features later. This can make a large project easier to manage because the work is divided into smaller pieces. It also gives the team opportunities to test each increment and collect feedback before continuing.

One disadvantage is that the team must carefully plan how all the increments will fit together. If different parts are developed without considering their connections, integration problems can occur. For example, two features might store information differently or fail to communicate with each other. The team also needs to make sure that early versions are secure and reliable, even if they do not contain every planned feature.

A college student portal is a good example of incremental development. The first increment could allow students to log in and view their class schedules. A later increment could add registration, followed by tuition payments, financial aid information, and degree progress. Students would gain access to useful features before the entire portal was finished. However, the development team would need to make sure that these features work together and protect student information.

Comparing the Three Models

The three models organize development differently, and each can be useful depending on the situation. The linear model focuses on completing stages in order. The iterative model focuses on improving a system through repeated cycles. The incremental model focuses on delivering the system in smaller, usable parts.

  • Linear: Works well when requirements are clear and stable. It provides structure and predictable stages, but late changes can be difficult and expensive.
  • Iterative: Works well when a system needs feedback and repeated improvement. It supports learning and change, but repeated cycles require time and careful management.
  • Incremental: Works well when a system can be divided into useful features or components. It allows earlier delivery, but the team must plan integration between the parts.

These models are not automatically better or worse than one another. The best choice depends on the system, its requirements, the people using it, the budget, and the risks involved. Some projects also combine approaches. A team could plan the overall system carefully, release it in increments, and use iterative cycles to improve each increment. The important thing is to choose an approach that supports the project's goals rather than following a model just because it is familiar.

Why Thinking About the Entire Life Cycle Matters

Thinking about the entire life cycle matters because decisions made early can affect the system for years. A team that only focuses on building a product might ignore maintenance costs, security, future upgrades, or what happens when the system becomes outdated. These issues can create problems later, even if the system initially works well.

NASA (2019) explains that the design decisions made early in a project can have a major effect on total life-cycle costs. Finding problems later can be more expensive because the team may need to redesign, rebuild, and test parts of the system that have already been completed. This is one reason why teams should evaluate requirements, test designs, and identify risks as early as possible. Early planning does not prevent every problem, but it can reduce the chances of expensive changes later.

Life-cycle thinking is also important for sustainability. Digital systems require electricity, hardware, storage, and other resources. Computers and servers eventually become outdated, and replacing equipment can create electronic waste. Developers and organizations can make more sustainable choices by considering energy use, repairability, hardware lifespan, and responsible disposal. A system that is designed to be maintained and upgraded may not need to be replaced as quickly as one that is difficult to change.

Security is another reason to consider the entire life cycle, especially in information technology and cybersecurity. Security cannot be treated as something that only happens during final testing. Teams should consider possible threats when gathering requirements and designing the system. They should also test security controls before release and continue applying updates and monitoring the system during operation. If a system is no longer supported, it can become more vulnerable because new problems may not be fixed. Planning for retirement and replacement helps organizations avoid continuing to depend on outdated technology.

Life-cycle thinking also helps systems evolve. Technology changes, organizations grow, and user expectations are different over time. A system that works well today might not meet future needs. By planning for maintenance, upgrades, and possible changes, teams can make systems easier to improve. This is especially important for digital systems because software, hardware, networks, and users are connected. A change in one part may affect other parts, so the team needs to consider the bigger picture before making changes.

Verification and Validation

Verification and validation are two important activities that help ensure a system is successful. They sound similar, but they answer different questions. Verification asks whether the system meets its specified requirements. Validation asks whether the system actually meets the needs and expectations of the people who will use it.

For example, a new student registration system might pass verification because it correctly saves course selections and follows its technical requirements. However, students might still find the registration process confusing. Validation would help determine whether the system is useful and works as expected for students. NASA (2019) explains this difference by connecting verification to requirements and validation to the system's intended purpose and operating environment.

Both activities should happen throughout development rather than only at the end. Testing early versions can reveal problems before they become harder to fix. Feedback from users can also show whether the team is building the right system, not just building a system that technically works. This is especially useful in iterative and incremental development, where teams can use test results to guide later improvements.

Conclusion

The systems development life cycle provides a way to organize the work involved in creating, operating, maintaining, and retiring a system. The linear, iterative, and incremental models each offer different benefits and challenges. The linear model provides a structured process, the iterative model supports repeated improvement, and the incremental model allows features to be delivered in smaller parts. The best model depends on the project's needs and how likely its requirements are to change.

Overall, I think the biggest lesson is that developing a system does not end when it is released. Systems need to be tested, maintained, secured, and improved as technology and user needs change. Planning for the full life cycle can help reduce costs, improve sustainability, and prevent problems from being ignored until they become serious. In cybersecurity and IT management, this is especially important because a system needs to remain useful and secure throughout its life, not just work on the day it launches.
