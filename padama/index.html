My thoughts about System Thinking

To me, systems thinking means looking at the whole thing instead of just one part. A system has different parts that work together. If something changes in one part, it can sometimes affect another part too. So, you have to think about how everything connects.

Systems and their blocks

In Chapter 1 of Systems Engineering Demystified, it talks about how systems can have many different parts and how those parts are connected. You can't always understand a system by only looking at one part. You have to look at how the parts work together. An example of this would be a car. A car has an engine, wheels, brakes, computers, sensors, and other parts. All of these things have to work together for the car to actually work (Holt & Perry, 2023).

The MIT article talks about how having a common understanding of systems thinking can help people from different technology fields work together. Systems thinking can help everyone understand how their part connects to the bigger system (MIT System Design and Management,2024).

Chapter 2 talks about Model-Based Systems Engineering, or MBSE. Basically, MBSE uses a model to help show how a system works. The model doesn't have to show every single detail. It just needs to show the important information. The chapter also talks about how the relationships between parts are important, which connects back to systems thinking (Holt & Perry, 2023).

  • Systems have multiple connected parts.
  • Changes to one part can affect other parts.
  • Relationships between parts are important.
  • Systems thinking can help IT teams work together more effectively.

Richard Buchanan also talks about systems thinking and how the different parts of a system work together toward a common purpose. This connects to the textbook because the textbook also explains that the relationships between parts are important. I think this is useful in IT because technology systems have many connected parts, and changing one part can affect the rest of the system (Buchanan, 2019).

All in all, I think that systems thinking is basically about understanding how everything works together. This is useful in IT and cybersecurity because there are usually a lot of different parts involved. You can't just look at one computer or one piece of software and ignore everything else. Week 2

Life Cycles

When people think about creating a new computer system or application, they might mainly think about the programming and design involved. However, developing a system involves much more than just building it. There needs to be planning, testing, implementation, maintenance, and eventually a plan for replacing or retiring the system. These steps are important because a system needs to work properly, meet user needs, and continue working as technology changes. This is where the systems development life cycle comes in.

The systems development life cycle, also known as the SDLC, is the process used to plan, create, test, release, and maintain a system. It provides a general structure for organizing development and making sure important steps are not skipped. IBM (n.d.) explains that the SDLC helps development teams organize the work needed to create software and improve its quality. Systems engineering also considers how different parts of a system work together and how decisions affect the system throughout its life. Understanding the life cycle is important because it helps teams make better decisions, manage risks, and create systems that can continue meeting people's needs.

Systems Development Life Cycle

In my own words, the systems development life cycle is the process a system goes through from the first idea to the point when it is no longer needed. It gives developers and other team members a plan for what needs to happen during each stage. Without a clear process, a team could rush into building something without fully understanding what users need. This could lead to mistakes, wasted time, security problems, or a product that does not work as expected.

Although the exact stages can vary depending on the project, the life cycle often includes planning, requirements gathering, design, development, testing, implementation, maintenance, and retirement. During planning, the team identifies the problem that needs to be solved and considers the available time, money, and resources. During requirements gathering, the team determines what the system needs to do. Design explains how the system will work, while development involves building or configuring it. Testing checks whether it works correctly before it is released.

After implementation, the work is not necessarily finished. A system may need updates, bug fixes, security patches, and other improvements. Eventually, it might need to be replaced because it is outdated or no longer meets the needs of its users. NASA (2025) explains that systems engineering covers the development, operation, and retirement of systems. This shows that a system's life cycle includes much more than the initial development stage.

Another important part of the life cycle is communication. Developers, managers, customers, security specialists, and users may all have different expectations. The team needs to understand these expectations and make decisions that balance performance, cost, time, and risk. A system might work technically but still fail if it is too difficult to use or does not solve the original problem. Planning for the entire life cycle helps teams consider these issues before they become expensive problems.

The Linear Model

The linear model, which is commonly associated with the waterfall model, follows a series of stages in a mostly fixed order. The team usually completes planning and requirements before moving to design, development, testing, and implementation. Each stage provides information needed for the next stage. The idea is to establish a clear plan and follow it through the project.

One advantage of the linear model is that it is easy to understand and organize. Everyone can see which stage the project is in and what work needs to be completed. It also creates documentation that can help teams understand the system and track decisions. This can be useful for projects with clear requirements that are not expected to change much. For example, a company replacing an existing system with a similar one might already know most of the features and requirements it needs.

However, the linear model can be difficult to use when requirements change. If a team discovers a major problem during testing, it might need to return to earlier stages and change parts of the design or development work. Those changes can take additional time and money. Users may also have to wait a long time before seeing a working version of the system, which means the team might not discover that something is wrong until late in the project.

For example, imagine a college is developing a new student registration system. If the team decides all the features at the beginning and follows a linear model, it might not discover that students have trouble navigating the registration process until testing. Fixing the problem could require changes to the original design. The linear model can still be useful, but it works best when the team understands the requirements and can keep them relatively stable.

The Iterative Model

The iterative model uses repeated cycles of development, testing, and improvement. Instead of expecting the first version to be perfect, the team creates an early version and uses what it learns to make changes. Each cycle gives the team another chance to identify problems, improve the design, and get closer to the expected result.

One advantage of the iterative model is that it allows teams to learn as they work. They can test ideas early, collect feedback from users, and make changes before too much time is spent on one design. This can be especially helpful when the project involves new technology or when the team does not know every requirement at the beginning. NASA (2025) describes systems engineering processes as being applied iteratively to correct problems and improve a system's development. This supports the idea that repeating processes can help teams find and address issues throughout a project.

The iterative model also has some disadvantages. Repeated testing and changes can increase costs and make it harder to predict exactly when the project will be finished. The team needs to manage feedback carefully so that the project does not keep changing without a clear goal. It is also important to document changes and make sure that improvements in one area do not create problems in another area.

An example would be developing a mobile banking application. The team might create an early version with basic account information and then ask users to test it. Based on their feedback, the team could improve the navigation, make important features easier to find, or fix confusing screens. More testing could reveal additional issues. The application improves through repeated cycles instead of relying on one final test at the end.

The Incremental Model

The incremental model develops a system in smaller parts, or increments. Instead of waiting until every feature is finished, the team builds and releases portions of the system over time. Each increment adds new features or capabilities until the system is complete. The parts need to work together as one system, so the team must plan how they will connect and share information.

A major advantage of the incremental model is that users can start using important features earlier. The team can focus on the most necessary functions first and add other features later. This can make a large project easier to manage because the work is divided into smaller pieces. It also gives the team opportunities to test each increment and collect feedback before continuing.

One disadvantage is that the team must carefully plan how all the increments will fit together. If different parts are developed without considering their connections, integration problems can occur. For example, two features might store information differently or fail to communicate with each other. The team also needs to make sure that early versions are secure and reliable, even if they do not contain every planned feature.

A college student portal is a good example of incremental development. The first increment could allow students to log in and view their class schedules. A later increment could add registration, followed by tuition payments, financial aid information, and degree progress. Students would gain access to useful features before the entire portal was finished. However, the development team would need to make sure that these features work together and protect student information.

Comparison Time

The three models organize development differently, and each can be useful depending on the situation. The linear model focuses on completing stages in order. The iterative model focuses on improving a system through repeated cycles. The incremental model focuses on delivering the system in smaller, usable parts.

  • Linear: Works well when requirements are clear and stable. It provides structure and predictable stages, but late changes can be difficult and expensive.
  • Iterative: Works well when a system needs feedback and repeated improvement. It supports learning and change, but repeated cycles require time and careful management.
  • Incremental: Works well when a system can be divided into useful features or components. It allows earlier delivery, but the team must plan integration between the parts.

These models are not automatically better or worse than one another. The best choice depends on the system, its requirements, the people using it, the budget, and the risks involved. Some projects also combine approaches. A team could plan the overall system carefully, release it in increments, and use iterative cycles to improve each increment. The important thing is to choose an approach that supports the project's goals rather than following a model just because it is familiar.

Why does it matter

Thinking about the entire life cycle matters because decisions made early can affect the system for years. A team that only focuses on building a product might ignore maintenance costs, security, future upgrades, or what happens when the system becomes outdated. These issues can create problems later, even if the system initially works well.

NASA (2019) explains that the design decisions made early in a project can have a major effect on total life-cycle costs. Finding problems later can be more expensive because the team may need to redesign, rebuild, and test parts of the system that have already been completed. This is one reason why teams should evaluate requirements, test designs, and identify risks as early as possible. Early planning does not prevent every problem, but it can reduce the chances of expensive changes later.

Life-cycle thinking is also important for sustainability. Digital systems require electricity, hardware, storage, and other resources. Computers and servers eventually become outdated, and replacing equipment can create electronic waste. Developers and organizations can make more sustainable choices by considering energy use, repairability, hardware lifespan, and responsible disposal. A system that is designed to be maintained and upgraded may not need to be replaced as quickly as one that is difficult to change.

Security is another reason to consider the entire life cycle, especially in information technology and cybersecurity. Security cannot be treated as something that only happens during final testing. Teams should consider possible threats when gathering requirements and designing the system. They should also test security controls before release and continue applying updates and monitoring the system during operation. If a system is no longer supported, it can become more vulnerable because new problems may not be fixed. Planning for retirement and replacement helps organizations avoid continuing to depend on outdated technology.

Life-cycle thinking also helps systems evolve. Technology changes, organizations grow, and user expectations are different over time. A system that works well today might not meet future needs. By planning for maintenance, upgrades, and possible changes, teams can make systems easier to improve. This is especially important for digital systems because software, hardware, networks, and users are connected. A change in one part may affect other parts, so the team needs to consider the bigger picture before making changes.

Checking to see if everything works

Verification and validation are two important activities that help ensure a system is successful. They sound similar, but they answer different questions. Verification asks whether the system meets its specified requirements. Validation asks whether the system actually meets the needs and expectations of the people who will use it.

A new student registration system might pass verification because it correctly saves course selections and follows its technical requirements. However, students might still find the registration process confusing. Validation would help determine whether the system is useful and works as expected for students. NASA (2019) explains this difference by connecting verification to requirements and validation to the system's intended purpose and operating environment.

Both activities should happen throughout development rather than only at the end. Testing early versions can reveal problems before they become harder to fix. Feedback from users can also show whether the team is building the right system, not just building a system that technically works. This is especially useful in iterative and incremental development, where teams can use test results to guide later improvements.

Summary

The systems development life cycle provides a way to organize the work involved in creating, operating, maintaining, and retiring a system. The linear, iterative, and incremental models each offer different benefits and challenges. The linear model provides a structured process, the iterative model supports repeated improvement, and the incremental model allows features to be delivered in smaller parts. The best model depends on the project's needs and how likely its requirements are to change.

Overall, I think the biggest lesson is that developing a system does not end when it is released. Systems need to be tested, maintained, secured, and improved as technology and user needs change. Planning for the full life cycle can help reduce costs, improve sustainability, and prevent problems from being ignored until they become serious. In cybersecurity and IT management, this is especially important because a system needs to remain useful and secure throughout its life, not just work on the day it launches.

Designing for People

Introduction to User-Centered Design

Have you ever used a website and had no idea where to click or where to find something? It can get pretty annoying, especially when you are just trying to finish something quickly. This is why human-centered design is important. It is about making websites, apps, and other systems easier for people to use instead of just making them look good.

A good example is Canvas. As students, we use it to check grades, find assignments, and turn in our work. If an assignment is hard to find or the submit button is confusing, it makes a simple task more stressful than it needs to be. Good design should make these things easier, not harder.

What is User-Centered Design?

Human-centered design basically means thinking about the people who will actually use something. Instead of guessing what people want, designers should listen to them, understand their problems, and try out different ways to solve those problems.

The Field Guide to Human-Centered Design states that designers should try to understand people, come up with ideas, and test those ideas. The guide breaks the process into three main phases: Inspiration, Ideation, and Implementation (IDEO.org).

For example, if students keep having trouble finding assignments in Canvas, the people designing it could ask students what is confusing. They could then try a different layout and see if students find it easier to use. This way, they are fixing a real problem instead of just guessing what might work.

ISO 9241-210 and IDEO's Approach

ISO 9241-210 is a standard that explains how to design systems with users in mind. It includes steps like figuring out who will use the system, understanding what they need, coming up with design ideas, and testing those ideas. If something does not work well, the design can be changed and tested again (International Organization for Standardization [ISO]).

IDEO's approach has a similar idea, but it focuses more on practical things like understanding people, working together, making prototypes, and learning from mistakes. Basically, ISO gives designers a more structured process, while IDEO shares ways to actually work through the design process.

Both approaches show why it is important to get feedback from real users. For example, someone designing a new Canvas assignment page could make a simple version and ask students to try it. If students still cannot find the due date or submit their work, the designer knows there is still something to fix.

How People View and Use Web Applications

One thing to remember is that people do not always notice everything on a screen. We usually look for whatever we need at that moment. If you are trying to submit homework, you are probably looking for the submit button instead of reading every single thing on the page. Johnson (2020) explains that our past experiences and expectations can affect how we understand what we see.

This is why keeping things consistent matters. If the Canvas menu stays in the same place on every page, students will get used to it. But if buttons keep moving around or have confusing labels, people might have trouble finding what they need.

Another issue is getting used to seeing the same messages over and over. If a website keeps showing unnecessary alerts, people might eventually stop paying attention to them. Important messages should stand out and actually be useful instead of overwhelming users (Johnson, 2020).

Visual Hierarchy and Why We Should Care

Visual hierarchy is just a way of organizing information so people can tell what is important first. Designers can do this by using headings, different font sizes, spacing, and colors to help guide people's attention.

Take a Canvas assignment page as an example. It would be helpful if the assignment name and due date were easy to spot, the instructions were organized, and the submit button was easy to find. Students should not have to search all over the page just to figure out what they are supposed to do.

If everything on a page looks equally important, it can be hard to know where to look first. The 10 Usability Heuristics for User Interface Design talks about ideas like keeping designs consistent, giving users clear feedback, and helping prevent mistakes (Nielsen, 1994). For example, Canvas should clearly tell you when your assignment has been submitted so you are not left wondering whether it actually went through.

The Laws of UX

There are also four UX laws that help explain why some websites are easier to use than others. These ideas connect to how people think and make decisions when using technology (Yablonski, 2024).

Jakob's Law

Jakob's Law is about how people expect websites to work like other websites they have used before. We get used to seeing menus, buttons, and links in certain places. When a website uses familiar layouts, it is easier to figure out. For example, students should not have to learn a completely new way to navigate every Canvas page.

Fitt's Law

Fitts's Law is about how easy it is to click or tap something based on its size and distance. Basically, bigger buttons are usually easier to select than tiny ones. If the submit button is too small or too close to another button, someone might accidentally click the wrong thing. Making important buttons easy to select can help prevent these problems.

Hick's Law

Hick's Law explains that having too many choices can make it take longer to decide what to do. Imagine opening a page and seeing a bunch of buttons that all seem important. You might not know which one to choose. Designers can help by organizing choices and making the main action easier to spot. That does not mean removing every extra option, just making the choices easier to understand.

Miller's Law

Miller's Law is often connected to the idea that people can keep around seven items, plus or minus two, in their working memory. However, this should not be treated as a strict rule that every menu can only have seven options. A more useful idea is to group related information together, which is called chunking (Yablonski, 2024).

For example, Canvas can organize assignments, grades, announcements, and course materials into separate sections. That way, students can focus on one group of information at a time instead of trying to look through everything at once.

Why Testing the Usability matters

Even if a website looks good to its designers, that does not automatically mean it will be easy for everyone else to use. This is where usability testing comes in. Designers let people try out a system and watch for problems so they can figure out what needs to be improved.

The Nielson Norman Group explains how testing can help find probelms that people go through when using a product (Moran, 2019).

For example, a designer could ask a few students to find an assignment and submit a file in Canvas. If several students struggle to find the submit button, that is a sign that the page might need to change. The designer could try a new layout and see if it works better. This also connects to ISO 9241-210 because testing helps designers check whether their design actually meets users' needs.

Conclusion

At the end of the day, human-centered design is about making things easier for the people who use them. A website can have lots of features, but those features do not help much if people cannot figure out how to use them. ISO 9241-210 and IDEO's Field Guide both show why understanding users and getting their feedback are important.

Canvas is a good example because students use it for important school tasks all the time. Clear instructions, familiar layouts, organized information, and easy-to-find buttons can make a big difference. Designers will not get everything right the first time, and that is okay. By listening to users, testing ideas, and making changes, they can create systems that are less confusing and more useful.

Usability

Introduction

We use technology pretty much every day. Whether it is checking Canvas, shopping online, watching videos, or using apps, technology is a big part of our lives. But just because a website or app has a lot of features does not mean it is easy to use. Sometimes a simple task can turn into a whole process because the website is confusing or things are hard to find.

A good digital system should make things easier, not harder. People should be able to open a website and understand what to do without having to figure everything out. Things like memory, attention, familiar layouts, and feedback all play a part in how easy a system is to use. If designers understand how people think and use technology, they can make websites and apps that are less confusing and less frustrating.

How Memory and Mental Models Affect Usability

Memory has a lot to do with how we use technology. When using a website, we have to remember what we are doing, where we clicked, and what steps we already finished. The problem is that people cannot remember everything at once. Johnson (2020) explains that working memory is limited, which means people can easily forget information when they start focusing on something else.

This is why websites should not expect users to remember every little thing. For example, if someone is filling out a form, the website should make it clear what information has already been entered. Progress bars and confirmation messages are also helpful because they show people where they are and what they have finished. It is much easier when the website keeps track of things instead of making the user do all the remembering.

Mental models are another part of usability. Basically, a mental model is what someone expects a website or app to do based on things they have used before. For example, most people know that a shopping cart icon means they can look at the things they want to buy. A magnifying glass usually means search. People already understand these symbols, so they do not have to spend time figuring out what they mean.

Johnson (2020) explains that recognizing something is easier than trying to remember it. For example, seeing a list of choices is easier than having to remember a bunch of commands. This is why websites should show users their options instead of expecting them to remember everything. It makes the whole experience easier, especially for people who are not very familiar with the website.

Consistency

Nobody wants to use a website where every page looks completely different. Consistency makes things easier because people get used to where everything is. If the menu, buttons, and other features stay in the same places, users do not have to keep searching for them.

Johnson (2020) explains that keeping things consistent helps people learn how a system works. Think about using a website where the back button is in one place on one page and somewhere completely different on another. That would get annoying pretty quickly. When things stay in the same place, people can get comfortable using the website without having to think about every action.

The World Wide Web Consortium (2023) also explains that websites should keep their navigation consistent across different pages. This means menus and navigation options should stay in familiar places. It might seem like a small detail, but it can make a big difference. People should be able to find what they need without having to search around every time they open a new page.

Feedback and Error Prevention

Feedback is basically how a website lets you know that something happened. For example, when you click a button, submit an assignment, or upload a file, the website should let you know if it worked. Otherwise, you are just sitting there wondering if anything actually happened.

A good example is submitting an assignment on Canvas. After submitting it, you want to see some kind of confirmation that it actually went through. If nothing shows up, you might start worrying that it did not submit and end up doing it again. Even a simple confirmation message can make things less stressful.

Nielsen Norman Group explains that users should be able to see what is happening when they interact with a system. This could be a loading symbol, a progress bar, or a message saying something was saved. These little things help users understand what the website is doing instead of making them guess.

The wording of messages matters too. Imagine getting a message that says, "Your session has expired. Please reauthenticate." Some people might not even know what reauthenticate means. Saying "Your session expired. Please log in again" is much easier to understand. Johnson (2020) explains that complicated words and confusing text can make it harder for people to understand what a system is telling them.

Tesler's Law and Cognitive Load

Tesler's Law is about how every system has some level of complexity that cannot completely go away. Basically, some things are just complicated. But designers can decide whether the user has to deal with all that complexity or whether the system can handle some of it for them.

For example, when shopping online, a website might save your address and payment information so you do not have to type everything in again. Email websites can also suggest someone's email address when you start typing their name. These features save time and make things easier.

Yablonski (2024) explains that Tesler's Law is about deciding where complexity should go. Designers cannot always remove complicated parts of a system, but they can make things easier for users. Instead of making people deal with every little step, the system can handle some of the work in the background.

This also connects to cognitive load, which is basically how much thinking a person has to do to finish something. If a website has too many buttons, pop-ups, instructions, and choices, it can get overwhelming. People might spend more time trying to understand the website than actually doing what they came there to do. A cleaner layout can help people focus on what matters.

How Bad Design Makes Life Difficult

Bad design can make even the easiest tasks annoying. One example is a website that has too many menus, advertisements, and pop-ups. When there is too much going on, it becomes harder to find what you are looking for. Instead of getting something done quickly, you end up clicking around trying to find the right page.

Johnson (2020) explains that people have limited attention. Most of the time, people are focused on whatever they are trying to accomplish. They are not looking at every single detail on the screen. This is why important buttons, instructions, and messages should be easy to notice. If something important is hidden, users might completely miss it.

Another example is when a website uses confusing instructions. If someone is just trying to submit an assignment, they should not have to read a huge paragraph to figure out which button to click. Instructions should be short and use words that people actually understand. Johnson (2020) also explains that things like tiny text, poor color contrast, and messy layouts can make reading more difficult.

I have also noticed this when using school websites. Sometimes it takes a while to find assignments, grades, or other course information because everything is spread across different pages. It gets frustrating when you have to keep clicking around just to find one thing. Having clear sections and keeping the same layout would make the experience a lot easier.

How Digital Systems Can Be Improved

There are a lot of simple ways to make websites and apps easier to use. First, keep the layout organized and do not put too much information on one page. Important buttons should be easy to find, and instructions should be simple. It also helps to use familiar icons and labels so people know what things do without having to guess.

Another important thing is testing the website with actual users. Sometimes a design might make sense to the person who created it, but that does not mean everyone else will understand it. Watching people use a website can help designers figure out what is confusing and what needs to change.

Designers should also remember that not everyone uses technology the same way. Some people use websites all the time, while others might be using them for the first time. A good system should be understandable for both. It should not make people feel like they need instructions just to complete a basic task.

Conclusion

All in all, a good digital system should make life easier. People should be able to use a website or app without getting confused or frustrated over simple things. Memory, mental models, consistency, and feedback all affect how people use technology. Johnson (2020) explains that people have limits when it comes to memory and attention, so websites should help users instead of making them remember everything.

Tesler's Law also shows that some complexity cannot be avoided, but designers can make it easier for people to deal with. Keeping things consistent, using clear instructions, and showing feedback are all small changes that can make a big difference. At the end of the day, people just want technology that works and does not make simple tasks harder than they need to be.

Visual and Interaction Design

Introduction

When we use an app or website, we usually aren't thinking about all the little design choices behind it. We just want to get something done, like check an assignment, order food, find information, or make a payment. But the way an app or website is set up can actually make a big difference in how easy it is to use. Things like where buttons are placed, what colors are used, and how much information is on the screen can change the whole experience.

Personally, I think a good app or website should be pretty easy to figure out. You shouldn't have to sit there wondering where to click or what you're supposed to do next. Everything should be clear enough that you can get what you need done without too much trouble. When a website is poorly designed, even something simple can become annoying.

There are a few UX laws that help explain why certain designs work better than others. The Aesthetic–Usability Effect is about how a nice-looking app can make people think it is easier to use. The Von Restorff Effect explains why things that look different tend to catch our attention. Postel's Law is about making apps and websites flexible enough to work with different people and devices. All of these ideas show that design isn't just about making something look pretty. It's also about making it easier for people to use.

Layout and Controls and how they affect our websites

The way a website is designed can really change how people look at and what they click on. Like, if an app puts its main buttton somewhere easy to spot, users are more likely to use it. However, if the main button is really small or hidden or has any other visual limitations, users might not even notice it.

Having everything in a nice and clean layout also plays a huge hand. Headings, spacing, pictures, and sections help break up information so people can find what they're looking for. Most people don't sit there and read every single word on a webpage. They usually skim through it until they find what they need. Nielsen (2006) explains that people often look at webpages in an F-shaped pattern, meaning they pay more attention to the top and left side of the page. This is why putting important information in easy-to-see places matters.

Buttons and other controls are important too. Things like menus, search bars, checkboxes, and links should make it pretty obvious what happens when you use them. Let's say, a button that says "Submit Assignment" is clear because you know exactly what it does. But if a button has a confusing name, you might have to guess what happens when you click it.

Keeping things almost same is another important component of good design. If a button looks completely different every time you see it, you're going to have to keep figuring out how it works. Johnson (2020) explains that people are better at doing things they've already learned than dealing with something new. This is why using familiar layouts and controls makes things easier. Once people get used to how a website works, they don't have to think about every little step.

The Aesthetic-Usability Effect

The Aesthetic–Usability Effect basically means that people tend to think something is easier to use when it looks nice. For example, a website with good colors, readable text, and a clean layout might feel easier to use than one that looks messy or outdated.

Yablonski (2024) shows that people can form opinions about a website really quickly just by looking at it. This means people might decide whether they like a website before they even try using it. If it looks organized, they might feel more comfortable using it. If it looks confusing or cluttered, they might already expect it to be difficult.

I'm pretty sure that almost everyone has experienced this. When I open an app and everything looks organized, I can figure out what to do in the first glance. But when there are too many colors, pop-ups, and buttons everywhere, it gets overwhelming. Even if the app technically works, the way it looks can make the experience more annoying.

Obviously, just because it looks nice and easy enough, that doesn't mean that it actually works well. A website could have the simplest layout or the flashiest, but still have confusing menus or buttons that are hard to find. That's why designers can't just focus on making things look pretty. They also need to make sure people can actually use the website without running into problems.

Let's take a shopping app for this example. The shopping app might have really nice product pictures and a clean home page, but if the checkout process is confusing, people might give up before buying anything. The design might get their attention at first, but the actual experience is what matters in the end.

The best design is when something looks good and is easy to use. I don't see a point in having a beautiful app if people can't figure out how to do anything in it.

The Von Restorff Effect

The Von Restorff Effect is basically about how something that looks different from everything around it is more likely to catch our attention. For example, imagine a website with a bunch of gray buttons and one bright button that says "Continue." Most people are probably going to notice the bright button first because it stands out.

Yablonski (2024) explains that designers can make things stand out by changing their color, size, shape, or position. It doesn't always have to be a different color. Something can also stand out just by being bigger or having more space around it.

I think this is useful because we're not paying attention to every single thing on a screen. If every button and message is trying to get our attention, it's hard to know what actually matters. Designers need to make the important stuff stand out without making the whole page look like a mess.

A good example is a food ordering app. There might be restaurant pictures, menus, prices, and reviews all on the screen, but the "Add to Cart" button should be easy to find. Making that button stand out helps people know what to do next without having to search around for it.

There is such a thing as using too much contrast, though. If every button is bright, every message is bold, and every section has a different color, then nothing really stands out anymore. It just makes the page look busy. Designers should save the extra attention-grabbing stuff for things that actually matter

Accessibility is important here too. Not everyone sees colors the same way, so designers shouldn't use color as the only way to show that something is important. They can also use words, icons, or different shapes. The World Wide Web Consortium (W3C, 2023) explains that text needs enough contrast against its background so people can actually read it. This helps make websites easier to use for people with different visual needs.

Postel's Law and Flexible Design

Postel's Law is another UX law that focuses on making technology easier for different people to use. It's usually explained as "Be conservative in what you do, be liberal in what you accept from others." Basically, a website or app should work properly without being too picky about how people use it.

Yablonski (2024) explains that people use different devices and ways of interacting with technology. Some people use a computer and mouse, while others use their phones, touchscreens, voice controls, or assistive technology. A good website should be able to work with these different ways of doing things instead of expecting everyone to use it the exact same way.

One example is filling out an online form. Some forms are way too picky about how you enter information. For example, a form might reject your phone number just because you added spaces or dashes. That's annoying because the website should be able to understand the number either way. A better form would accept different formats and fix the information automatically.

Another example is a website that works on both computers and phones. Nobody wants to open a website on their phone and have to zoom in just to read something or click a tiny button. The important features should still be easy to find no matter what device someone is using.

I think Postel's Law is important because technology should make things easier for people, not the other way around. People make mistakes, use different devices, and sometimes enter information differently. A good website should be able to handle that without making everything more difficult.

This also works with Johnson's (2020) explanation that unfamiliar tasks take more effort than things people already know how to do. When a website is flexible and gives clear feedback, people don't have to spend as much time trying to figure out what the system wants from them. It makes the whole experience less frustrating.

How the Way We Think Affects the Design

The way we think and process information also has a lot to do with how easy a website is to use. In Designing with the Mind in Mind, Johnson (2020) talks about how people rely on things they've already seen and learned. When someone uses the same type of app or website over and over, they start recognizing certain patterns and don't have to think as much about every action.

For instance, most people know that a shopping cart icon means the items they want to buy. Nobody needs a whole explanation every time they see it. We've seen it so many times that we already know what it means. Using familiar icons and layouts helps people understand new websites without having to learn everything from scratch.

Johnson (2020) also explains that people can only focus on so much at once. When a website throws too much information at someone, it can make it harder to figure out what to do. That's why keeping things organized and getting rid of unnecessary steps is so important.

Another idea is that people think in all different ways. Sometimes we make quick decisions without really thinking about them, especially when we're doing something familiar. Other times, we have to slow down and think more carefully because something is new or confusing. A good website should make familiar tasks easy and give people enough help when they need to do something new.

This doesn't mean every website has to be super simple or get rid of every option. Some tasks are just complicated. The important thing is to organize everything so people aren't dealing with too much at once. A checkout page could separate delivery information, payment information, and the final order confirmation instead of putting everything on one crowded page.

My personal friend Canvas

One website I use pretty much all the time is Canvas for my college classes. I use it to check assignments, read instructions, submit my work, and look at my grades. Overall, it's useful because everything for my classes is in one place. But it can also get frustrating when every course is organized differently or when I have to click through several pages just to find something.

Like, when I open a course, I usually just want to find the assignments, due dates, and instructions. If the instructor keeps everything organized, it's pretty easy to find what I need. But if information is spread across different pages or hidden under headings that don't make much sense, it takes longer. The information might be there, but that doesn't really help if I can't find it.

This also goes great with the idea of consistency from Designing with the Mind in Mind. Johnson (2020) explains that people have an easier time with actions and layouts they're already familiar with. When courses use clear labels and organize things in a similar way, students don't have to keep figuring out where everything is.

The buttons and feedback matter too. When I submit an assignment, I want to know that it actually went through. A clear confirmation message makes it easier to know that everything worked. Without that feedback, I might sit there wondering if my file uploaded or if I need to submit it again.

I also think that Canvas connects to Postel's Law because students might use it on a computer, tablet, or phone. The important features should still work no matter what device someone is using. If something only works properly on a computer, that's going to make things harder for people who use their phones instead.

All in all, Canvas is a good example of how a website can have all the features people need but still be frustrating if those features are hard to find. It's not enough to just have the tools. They also need to be easy to understand and use.

Why Good Interaction Design Matters

After looking at these UX laws, I think they all have one main thing in common: they are about making technology easier for people to use. The Aesthetic–Usability Effect shows that how something looks can affect how people feel about it. The Von Restorff Effect explains why certain things need to stand out. Postel's Law reminds us that websites should be flexible enough to work for different people.

These laws also show that even small design changes can make a big difference. Moving a button, using a clearer label, adding more space, or making a form easier to fill out can improve someone's experience. At the same time, adding too many colors, options, or steps can make things harder than they need to be.

I also think designers need to remember that not everyone thinks the same way they do. Something that seems obvious to the person who created a website might be confusing to someone using it for the first time. That's why testing websites with actual users is important. Watching people try to use something can show what is confusing and what needs to be fixed.

Another thing that matters is feedback. When someone clicks a button, uploads a file, or submits a form, the website should let them know that something happened. Otherwise, people might not know if their action worked. A simple confirmation message can save people from having to repeat the same action or worry that something went wrong.

Conclusion

Visual and interaction design have a bigger effect on us than we probably realize. Things like colors, buttons, layouts, and messages can change how easy or difficult it is to use an app or website. A good interface should help people get things done without making them confused or overwhelmed.

The three UX laws I talked about show different ways designers can make websites better. The Aesthetic–Usability Effect explains why appearance matters, the Von Restorff Effect shows how certain things can stand out, and Postel's Law explains why websites should be flexible. The concepts from Designing with the Mind in Mind also show why familiar layouts, consistency, and clear information are important.

To sum it all up, good design isn't just about making an app look nice. It should actually make people's lives easier. If someone can open a website, figure out what to do, and finish their work without quitting due to frustration, then the design is working as expected.

Usability and User Experience

Introduction

When people use an app or website, they usually just want to get things done. They don't want to sit around waiting for a page to load or spend forever trying to figure out where a button is. They want things to work without making their lives harder. Even small things like a button responding or a message saying something was saved can make a big difference. It might not seem like a big deal, but these little things can change how someone feels about using a website or app.

I think good UX is about making technology easier for people to use. It isn't just about making something look pretty. Designers also need to think about how people actually use technology and what makes them frustrated. There are a few UX laws that explain this, like the Doherty Threshold, Peak-End Rule, and Fitts' Law. These laws explain why things like fast feedback, good buttons, and a good ending to an interaction matter.

Another thing designers need to think about is whether they are actually helping people or just trying to get them to spend more time on an app. Some apps use notifications, rewards, and autoplay to keep people coming back. These features can be useful, but they can also be annoying or manipulative. Good design should help people instead of making them feel like they have to keep using something.

Why Timing and Feedback Matter

One thing that annoys me when using technology is clicking something and having absolutely nothing happen. I start wondering if I clicked the wrong thing or if the website is just broken. Sometimes I end up clicking the same button again because I think the first click didn't work. This can cause problems, especially when submitting assignments or making purchases.

This is why feedback is important. When someone clicks a button, the website should let them know that it received the click. It doesn't have to finish the entire task right away, but it should at least show that something is happening. A loading circle, a message, or even a button changing color can help. Without feedback, people are basically left guessing.

The Doherty Threshold explains that people work better when a computer responds within about 400 milliseconds. Basically, people don't want to wait around every time they click something. Faster responses help people stay focused and keep doing what they were doing (Yablonski, 2024).

For example, when I submit an assignment through Canvas, I want to know that my work actually went through. If I click submit and nothing changes, I might start wondering if I need to click it again. A simple message saying that my assignment was submitted would solve that problem. It is a small thing, but it makes the website feel more reliable.

The course reading also explains that people notice delays pretty quickly. A response around 0.1 seconds usually feels almost instant. If something takes longer, the website should show that it is working. For example, a progress bar can show how much of a download or upload is finished (Johnson, 2020).

I think progress bars are helpful because at least you know something is going on. They don't magically make the website faster, but they make waiting less confusing. If my computer is installing an update, I would rather see a progress bar than stare at a blank screen and wonder if my computer just died.

Motion can also help with feedback. A little animation when you click a button or add something to a shopping cart can show that the action worked. But there is a point where too much animation becomes annoying. If everything is moving, flashing, or popping up, it can make it harder to focus. Animations should have a reason for being there instead of just being added because they look cool.

The Peak-End Rule

Another interesting UX law is the Peak-End Rule. Basically, people tend to remember the most intense part of an experience and how it ended. They don't always remember every little thing that happened in between. So even if most of something went well, one really bad moment can change how you remember it (Yablonski, 2024).

Think about ordering food through DoorDash. You pick a restaurant, choose your food, pay, and everything seems pretty easy. But then your order gets delayed and the app doesn't give you any updates. Now you're sitting there wondering where your food is. Even if ordering was easy, that frustrating wait might be the part you remember the most.

On the other hand, if the app gives you updates, explains what's happening, and gives you an easy way to get help, the experience might end on a better note. You might still be annoyed that your food was late, but at least you weren't completely left in the dark.

I think this is something websites should pay attention to. It isn't enough to make the first few steps easy. The last part matters too. When someone finishes buying something, submitting an assignment, or making an account, they should know that everything worked. A confirmation message or a simple thank-you page can make the ending feel much better.

A good example is Spotify Wrapped. At the end of the year, Spotify shows people what they listened to the most. It takes information people already have and turns it into something fun to look at and share. It gives people a memorable moment instead of just showing them a bunch of boring numbers.

But, designers shouldn't use this idea to cover up problems. Adding a fun animation at the end doesn't fix a website that was confusing the entire time. The whole experience should be good, not just the last few seconds.

Making Buttons Easier to Use

Another UX law is Fitts' Law. This law is about how easy it is for someone to click or tap something. Basically, bigger buttons are easier to hit than tiny buttons. It also helps when the button is closer to where the person is already clicking or using their finger. This is especially important on phones because people are tapping the screen instead of using a mouse (Johnson, 2020).

Think about trying to press a really tiny button on your phone. You might accidentally hit something next to it instead. That can get annoying pretty quickly. Designers can make this easier by making buttons bigger and leaving enough space between them. It sounds pretty basic, but it can prevent people from clicking the wrong thing.

This also connects to preventing mistakes. Chapter 15 explains that people make different types of errors. A mistake happens when someone makes a decision but gets it wrong. A slip happens when someone does something by accident, even though they meant to do something else (Johnson, 2020).

For example, if I accidentally click the wrong button because two buttons are right next to each other, that is a slip. If I buy the wrong item because I misunderstood its description, that is a mistake. Both can be frustrating, but they happen for different reasons.

There are a few easy ways designers can help prevent these problems. They can make buttons easier to see, keep different options separated, and explain what will happen before someone clicks something important. They can also give users a way to undo things if they make a mistake.

I think the Undo button in Microsoft Word is a good example. Everyone makes typing mistakes or accidentally deletes something. Instead of having to figure out how to fix everything manually, you can just click Undo. It makes using the program easier because you know you can go back if something goes wrong.

Another example is deleting files. If someone is about to permanently delete something, the computer should probably ask if they are sure. That extra message gives the person another chance to stop before doing something they might regret. It is better than deleting something important with one accidental click.

Motion Should Support People

Motion is another thing that can change how people feel when using an app or website. Animations can make things easier to understand. For example, a loading circle tells you that a page is still working. A menu sliding open shows that something changed on the screen. These little things can make an app feel more responsive.

The problem is when there is too much motion. Some websites have flashing ads, videos that start playing by themselves, and notifications popping up all the time. It can feel like everything is fighting for your attention. Instead of helping you finish what you are doing, the website just becomes distracting.

Chapter 12 from Laws of UX explains how companies use psychology to influence what people do. One example is variable rewards. This is when people keep checking an app because they never know when they might get a new like, comment, or notification. Another example is infinite scrolling, where new posts keep showing up instead of the page having a normal stopping point.

I think most people have probably opened an app just to check one thing and then ended up spending way more time on it than they planned. Social media makes this pretty easy because there is always something new to look at. Autoplay does something similar because the next video starts without you even having to press anything.

These features aren't always bad. Sometimes autoplay is useful if someone wants to watch a bunch of videos. But people should still have control over it. There should be an easy way to turn it off instead of forcing everyone to use it. Designers should also think about people who don't like lots of movement on their screens. Giving users an option to reduce animations or turn off notifications can make things more comfortable.

Ethical Design and Dark Patterns

One thing I took away from the ethical design reading is that just because a company can get people to do something doesn't mean it should. Designers know how to get people's attention and encourage them to click things. That knowledge can be used to make websites easier to use, but it can also be used to trick people.

This is where dark patterns come in. Dark patterns are design choices that try to trick people into doing something they might not actually want to do. For example, a website might make signing up for a subscription really easy but make canceling it confusing. Another website might hide extra charges until the very end of checkout.

Research by Mathur and other researchers looked at around 11,000 shopping websites and found many examples of dark patterns. This shows that these problems are not just happening on one or two websites. They are used across different online shopping experiences (Mathur et al., 2019).

The Federal Trade Commission also explains how companies use dark patterns to get people to buy things, sign up for services, or share personal information. Sometimes the design makes it hard for people to understand what they are agreeing to (Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, 2022).

I think this is pretty unfair to users. If someone wants to cancel a subscription, they should be able to find the cancel button without searching through a bunch of pages. If a company wants people to sign up for something, it should be clear about the price and what they are signing up for. People shouldn't have to fight with a website just to make their own decisions.

A lot of people don't bother changing the settings that are already selected for them. This can be a problem with privacy. If an app automatically shares more information than someone expects, that isn't really giving them much control. Privacy settings should be easy to find and understand.

Slow and Steady Wins the Race

Technology is usually supposed to make everything faster and easier. Most of the time, that is a good thing. But I don't think every single action needs to happen instantly. Sometimes having an extra step can actually help people.

For example, if someone is deleting an important file, it makes sense to ask them if they are sure. The same goes for making a big purchase or changing important account settings. That extra step can stop someone from making a mistake they might not be able to fix.

The ethical design reading explains that adding friction can sometimes be a good thing. It can protect someone's privacy, help prevent mistakes, and give people time to think before making an important decision. I agree with this because making everything faster isn't always better.

There is a difference between helpful steps and annoying steps, though. Asking someone to confirm before deleting a file makes sense. Making someone click through ten different pages just to cancel a subscription is not helpful. Designers should make normal tasks easy while still protecting people when something important is happening.

Conclusion

All in all, I think good UX comes down to understanding that real people are using the technology. People get distracted, make mistakes, and get annoyed when something doesn't work. The Doherty Threshold shows why quick feedback matters. The Peak-End Rule explains why the best or worst parts of an experience and the ending can stick with people. Fitts' Law shows why even something as simple as button size can make a difference.

The readings also show that designers should help people avoid mistakes and make it easy to fix things when they happen. Something as simple as an Undo button, a loading message, or a confirmation screen can make a website less stressful to use.

The biggest thing, though, is that designers need to think about what is fair to the people using their products. Notifications, autoplay, and recommendations can be helpful, but they shouldn't be used just to keep people stuck on an app. Companies should care about whether people are actually getting something useful out of the experience instead of only caring about how long they stay online.

At the end of the day, technology should work for people, not the other way around. A good app should be easy to use, explain what is happening, and let people make their own choices. It doesn't need to be perfect, but it should at least make people's lives easier instead of making things more complicated.
