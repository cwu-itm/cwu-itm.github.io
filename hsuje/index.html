Module 1.1 Blog Post

Seeing the Hidden Framework of Systems Thinking

Think about a busy airport. You drop off your bags, pass through security, and board your plane exactly on time. This doesn’t happen just because one pilot did a good job. It works because thousands of parts, people, and rules cooperate together. If a flight is delayed for whatever reason, that problem does not stay local. That one delay can cause other flights to be delayed or canceled entirely. Flight crews can miss their shifts. Passengers could miss their connecting flights. When a breakdown like this happens, it is rarely because one single part broke. It usually happens because a connection somewhere in the network failed and caused a ripple effect across the entire system.

Beyond a Collection of Parts

A system is more than a collection of parts. Think of a system as a single whole made of different parts and relationships that work together to achieve a shared goal (Arnold & Wade, 2015). If you only had handlebars and pedals, you could not call that a bicycle. It only becomes a working bicycle once all the parts are connected and working together. The same applies to human organizations. A hospital is not just a building with beds and doctors. It is a highly connected system of staff, records, rules, and protocols all working to keep people healthy. Whether those parts are humans, machines, processes, or mental skills, they constantly affect one another. It is how they work together that determines what the whole system actually does (Arnold & Wade, 2015).

As the modern world becomes more advanced, our economic, social, and technological systems become more intertwined and dependent on one another (Arnold & Wade, 2015). Because these systems rely on each other, a change or development in one area will cause unpredictable ripple effects globally (Arnold & Wade, 2015). Since these ripple effects impact all areas of life, decision makers in every field need to understand these complex connections (Arnold & Wade, 2015).

To understand these complex connections, systems thinking is needed. It is a mindset focused on how parts connect and interact, rather than studying parts in isolation (Holt, 2021). In the real world, no single part operates in a vacuum. It is just as important to understand the relationships between parts as it is knowing how the individual pieces work. This supports the idea that solving a problem requires looking at the entire system instead of trying to fix individual parts (Holt, 2021).

Most of us learned to solve problems in a straight line. Break the big problem into smaller pieces, fix each piece, and put them back together. This linear approach is fine for simple jobs, but it breaks down when the pieces are deeply connected. When things move in loops instead of a straight line, fixing one piece can accidentally break another.

The Three Evils of Engineering

System failures are usually caused by three specific problems which are also known as the three evils of engineering (Holt, 2021). The first evil is unmanaged complexity which is when something becomes so big and complicated that it’s hard to track how all the pieces fit and connect (Holt, 2021). The second evil is poor communication between teams (Holt, 2021). The third evil is a lack of shared understanding about what the final result should be (Holt, 2021).

These three evils form a continuous and destructive loop that feeds into itself (Holt, 2021). Unmanaged complexity can cause poor communication. Poor communication can lead to a lack of understanding. That lack of understanding can make the original problem even more complex (Holt, 2021).

Think about a security update for a phone or a computer network. A network can become incredibly tangled and hard to map. Because it is so complex, the security team cannot clearly explain the system flaws to the programming team. The programmers misunderstand the goal and write a security patch that does not quite fit the system. When they install that patch, it creates a new software conflict. This conflict makes the original computer network even more complicated than it was before, and the cycle loops again.

Systems thinking serves as a common language that allows specialists from different fields to communicate and work together effectively (Arnold & Wade, 2015). Although experts agree with the need to teach this mindset to students, it remains largely missing from education today (Arnold & Wade, 2015). Systems thinking does not come naturally. It is a learned skill that requires practice and intentional learning (Arnold & Wade, 2015).

The Eight Skills of Systems Thinking

Learning to think this way involves eight connected skills (Arnold & Wade, 2015). The first three skills are about seeing the hidden framework of the world. A person must first recognize how separate parts connect (Arnold & Wade, 2015). Next, they must identify feedback loops where one action loops back to cause a reaction (Arnold & Wade, 2015). A thermostat perfectly demonstrates this because it turns on the heat and the resulting warm air feeds back to turn the machine off. Recognizing these connections and loops helps people understand the overall structure of a system (Arnold & Wade, 2015).

The next three skills involve watching how things change over time (Arnold & Wade, 2015). To map out a system, people sort information into stocks, flows, and variables (Arnold & Wade, 2015). A stock is a collection of resources like water sitting in the tub. The flow is the rate at which things enter or leave like water pouring from the faucet. A variable is an outside factor that controls the flow like the knob controlling the water pressure. Tracking these moving resources helps people understand unpredictable relationships (Arnold & Wade, 2015). In a complex network, a tiny turn of a faucet can cause a massive flood elsewhere. Because of these unpredictable reactions, a person must understand dynamic behavior and accept that things move and change constantly (Arnold & Wade, 2015).

Keeping all this moving information straight is difficult. To handle it, the final two skills are used to reduce complexity (Arnold & Wade, 2015). A person applies these skills by making a simplified mental model that drops useless details and by practicing how to zoom in and out to see how small parts affect the massive whole (Arnold & Wade, 2015). Teams end up creating accidental complexity by either using confusing tools or having bad habits that make the job harder for no reason (Holt, 2021). A good team should manage the essential complexity of a project and eliminate the accidental kind (Holt, 2021).

When people fail to use these thinking skills, big projects fall victim to the destructive loop of the three evils discussed earlier. At this point, people must recognize the difference between the complexity we have to deal with and the complexity we can just get rid of (Holt, 2021). Securing a digital account against modern hackers is naturally complex because it requires heavy encryption and verification protocols. That inherent challenge is essential complexity, which is the nature of the job and necessary (Holt, 2021). However, if an IT department uses poorly designed password rules that force users to change their logins every thirty days while locking accounts after a single typo, they create accidental complexity (Holt, 2021). That is an unnecessary problem caused by bad software habits that makes the system harder to use for no reason. Teams must manage the essential complexity of a project while eliminating the accidental kind (Holt, 2021).

The Digital Model Solution

For decades, the engineering industry tried to manage this complexity using paper documents. Teams wrote thousands of separate text files and spreadsheets to keep track of project requirements (Holt, 2021). When a design changed, a person had to find and update every single related document by hand. Information was constantly lost and mistakes were common. Disconnected paper documents make project complexity and poor communication much worse.

To fix this, the industry created model-based systems engineering (MBSE). This new way of working replaces scattered documents with one central digital model (Holt, 2021). A digital model is a visual database built inside a computer program. Instead of writing long paragraphs about how parts connect, engineers draw shapes and lines on a screen to show relationships (Holt, 2021). All project data is contained in this file.

This model becomes the single source for the entire project (Holt, 2021). Every person looks at the same file. If the engine team changes a wire, the system automatically updates the electronics team diagrams instantly. Model-based systems engineering puts the systems thinking mindset into practice. Systems thinking is used to make sense of a messy problem. The digital model then captures that simplified mental picture and stores it safely so the entire team can work from the exact same design (Holt, 2021).

This combination is important today because everyday items are no longer just simple machines. Fifty years ago, a car engine was mostly metal parts with a few electrical wires (Holt, 2021). Today, a modern car is a computer on wheels packed with millions of lines of software code. It connects to the internet, talks to satellites, and links to smart city traffic lights (Holt, 2021). It also has to comply with strict environmental laws, safety standards, and privacy rules (Holt, 2021). Digital models help teams predict how these complex behaviors will act in the real world before they ever build the physical car.

The Future of System Design

Professionals report major advantages when using model-based systems engineering. Industry surveys show that engineering teams view digital models favorably because they improve project understandability and collaboration (Campo et al., 2022). Workers state the tools help reduce time spent, lower costs, and improve analytical capabilities (Campo et al., 2022). Shared visual diagrams help improve communications and make sharing information easier (Campo et al., 2022).

While the advantages are promising, transitioning to a model-based approach does come with challenges. Moving from paper to digital models requires a major upfront financial investment in tools and training. Research shows that many of the claimed benefits are currently based on expectations rather than hard quantitative metrics (Henderson & Salado, 2020). A massive review found that many of the benefit claims lack actual proof, with most relying purely on author opinion (Henderson & Salado, 2020). If teams rely blindly on the software without applying basic common sense, they risk creating even more accidental complexity.

A digital tool is only as effective as the human mind guiding it. Systems thinking remains a foundational skill that anyone can practice simply by asking what the parts are, how they connect, and what shared goal they are trying to achieve. When teams first develop this mindset, digital modeling becomes an incredibly powerful way to put that thinking into practice. By keeping complex connections organized and providing a single source of truth, this approach helps modern organizations conquer the three evils of engineering, tame essential complexity, and build the innovative systems of the future.

A Guide to the Systems Development Life Cycle

The iterative life cycle model makes several passes through the stages instead of a single pass (Holt, 2021). Each pass is called an iteration (Holt, 2021). A complex project becomes easier to manage when it is broken into these shorter cycles (Holt, 2021). Each iteration starts at the concept stage and moves through production and into utilization (Holt, 2021). Every pass produces a more complete version of the working system (Holt, 2021). One major advantage is the ability to undo a failed release. If a new version fails the team can easily return to an older working version (Holt, 2021). This approach helps when requirements are unclear at the beginning of a project. The team can build and test assumptions repeatedly until stakeholders are satisfied with the result (Walden et al., 2015). The features that distinguish the iterative approach are velocity and adaptability (Walden et al., 2015). The project receives continuous feedback that ensures the team is going in a direction that satisfies the highest needs first (Walden et al., 2015).

The Six Generic Stages

There are six generic stages for a system (Walden et al., 2015). Conception is the first stage where engineers figure out what stakeholders need (Walden et al., 2015). Teams use this time to define the problem space and explore different technologies (Walden et al., 2015). Development follows to find potential solutions and choose the best design (Walden et al., 2015). Engineers define the specific system requirements and create the architecture descriptions (Walden et al., 2015). Production takes that chosen design and physically builds the system (Walden et al., 2015). The manufacturing team inspects and verifies the product during this period (Walden et al., 2015). Utilization is the period when people actually use the system in the real world (Walden et al., 2015). The system operates to satisfy the needs of the users (Walden et al., 2015). Support happens alongside utilization to keep the system running (Walden et al., 2015). This stage provides sustained capability through maintenance and upgrades (Walden et al., 2015). Retirement is the final stage where the system is taken out of service and disposed of safely (Walden et al., 2015). The team stores data and archives records during this shutdown phase (Walden et al., 2015).

Balancing System Integrity

Each stage has a specific goal with a clear beginning and end. Dividing the work into stages helps a team manage complex projects. A decision gate sits at the end of each stage to review the progress (Walden et al., 2015). The project can move forward and execute the next stage (Walden et al., 2015). The team can also stay in the current stage to fix problems or respond to action items (Walden et al., 2015). The project can return to an earlier stage or hold all project activity (Walden et al., 2015). The final option is to terminate the project entirely (Walden et al., 2015). Every system life cycle consists of three distinct aspects (Walden et al., 2015). These include the business case and the funding budget and the technical product (Walden et al., 2015). System integrity requires that these three aspects remain in balance (Walden et al., 2015). Decision gates must give equal emphasis to all three areas to keep a project healthy (Walden et al., 2015).

The Linear Life Cycle Model

When engineers build a system they must choose a specific model to organize their work. The linear life cycle model carries out the stages one time in a simple sequence (Holt, 2021). A classic example is the waterfall model (Holt, 2021). Each stage is finished completely before the next one begins. There is typically no way to go back to an earlier stage (Holt, 2021). This model works well when the project needs are clear and the technology is understood (Holt, 2021). Linear methods are highly predictable and stable (Walden et al., 2015). This stability makes the linear model a good choice for medical equipment and other products with strict safety rules (Walden et al., 2015). These projects must follow documented plans closely to maintain high assurance levels (Walden et al., 2015). Since the steps are firmly set the progress can be easily tracked and budgets can be estimated accurately.

Drawbacks appear when a project grows large or needs change. The final product is delivered in a single release at the very end of the timeline (Holt, 2021). Users cannot see or test the system until it is completely finished (Holt, 2021). Unforeseen changes can cause predictability to drop rapidly (Walden et al., 2015). Teams may spend large amounts of money just updating their planning documents to reflect new realities (Walden et al., 2015). This model is not well suited to massive systems whose needs can shift during a long development phase (Holt, 2021). If a competitor releases a new technology halfway through the timeline the team cannot adapt their design without starting over. The lack of flexibility forces teams to commit to early decisions even if those decisions become outdated before the launch date.

The Iterative Life Cycle Model

The iterative life cycle model makes several passes through the stages instead of a single pass (Holt, 2021). Each pass is called an iteration (Holt, 2021). A complex project becomes easier to manage when it is broken into these shorter cycles (Holt, 2021). Each iteration starts at the concept stage and moves through production and into utilization (Holt, 2021). Every pass produces a more complete version of the working system (Holt, 2021). One major advantage is the ability to undo a failed release. If a new version fails the team can easily return to an older working version (Holt, 2021). This approach helps when requirements are unclear at the beginning of a project. The team can build and test assumptions repeatedly until stakeholders are satisfied with the result (Walden et al., 2015).

The iterative approach does have specific drawbacks. Teams might rush to release an iteration on time rather than waiting to ensure it works perfectly (Holt, 2021). Stakeholders can also change their minds frequently after seeing early versions of the product. A strict process is required to manage these shifting needs and prevent the project scope from expanding out of control (Holt, 2021). A stakeholder who changes direction too often can leave the entire development effort unstable and chaotic (Walden et al., 2015). Teams must exercise strict discipline to ensure constant revisions do not destroy the project budget. The constant loop of feedback requires strong communication to keep everyone aligned on the final goal.

The Incremental Life Cycle Model

The incremental life cycle model takes a different approach to the early work. The concept stage is executed once to cover all the needs for the entire project (Holt, 2021). Later development and production stages only tackle specific subsets of those needs (Holt, 2021). This produces a partial solution that does not comprise the whole system (Holt, 2021). This partial system can be deployed into the real world immediately (Holt, 2021). The main advantage is that the system is delivered in parts. Users benefit from working features long before the final project ends (Holt, 2021). This is highly beneficial for very long projects where stakeholders require early system functionality (Holt, 2021). Delivering pieces of the system early provides value and allows the team to gather real world performance data.

A major drawback is that not every system can be divided into distinct pieces (Holt, 2021). This evolutionary approach is not suitable for highly integrated physical products (Holt, 2021). Stakeholders must review the risks carefully before adding the next increment to ensure all the pieces fit together safely (Walden et al., 2015). The project should only proceed if the risks are acceptable (Walden et al., 2015). Producing partial solutions that do not comprise the whole system is a difficult engineering challenge. If the early subsets of requirements are integrated poorly the system architecture will become fragmented as later increments are attached. The team requires intense foresight during the initial concept stage to ensure the final pieces will eventually form a cohesive whole.

Managing Complexity with Digital Models

Developing complex technical systems requires a highly structured approach (Lipšinić et al., 2025). Systems theory provides a way to study how individual components interact to create the overall behavior of a system (Lipšinić et al., 2025). Engineers decompose a massive system into smaller subsystems based on functionality or organizational structures (Lipšinić et al., 2025). Model based systems engineering is highly suited for developing these complex architectures in the early stages (Lipšinić et al., 2025). This approach uses digital tools to facilitate analysis and verification during development (Lipšinić et al., 2025). Continuous iterations through multiple system variants are a normal part of development (Lipšinić et al., 2025). Digital models support this constant growth and allow engineers to reuse data across the entire life cycle (Lipšinić et al., 2025).

The Impact of Early Decisions on Digital Systems

No single model is perfect for every project. Teams are free to choose the development model that suits them and are not restricted to sequential methods (Walden et al., 2015). Thinking about the full life cycle is valuable whichever path is chosen (Walden et al., 2015). This long term view is highly beneficial for the design and sustainability of systems. Early decisions shape the entire future of a product. Qualities like sustainability and safety depend heavily on choices made early in development (Lipšinić et al., 2025). These qualities often only become clear when the system is actually being used in a live environment (Lipšinić et al., 2025). Changes made late in the life cycle can raise financial costs significantly (Lipšinić et al., 2025). For a digital system it is much easier to adjust software design during the concept stage than after thousands of people rely on the live application.

Preparing for Continuous Change

A system continues to change long after it is built. A system in active use will differ from what was originally specified and built (Lipšinić et al., 2025). Repairs and upgrades alter the properties of the system over time (Lipšinić et al., 2025). Digital tools receive frequent software updates that move them away from their original design. A life cycle view expects this continuous drift and helps engineers plan for it. Technology ages over time as well. Tools and platforms experience a technology life cycle that moves through research and development into maturity and eventual decline. Systems lasting many years must plan for the day when some of their technologies become outdated. Digital systems sit on top of frameworks that follow this exact same pattern.

Looking Beyond the Launch Date

The later stages of a system require serious attention. Most studies focus entirely on the design stage. Far fewer researchers look at manufacture and utilization and maintenance and retirement (Lipšinić et al., 2025). Retirement planning must begin in the early concept stage (Walden et al., 2015). Qualities like maintainability must be considered at the start because adding them during production is too late (Walden et al., 2015). Poor work in the beginning leads directly to poor cost and schedule projections. The Mars Science Laboratory rover provides a clear example of this failure (Walden et al., 2015). Incomplete systems engineering in the early stages resulted in poor trades among technical alternatives (Walden et al., 2015). This caused the project to miss its scheduled launch window (Walden et al., 2015). The rover experienced a two year delay and a massive cost growth over the approved development budget (Walden et al., 2015).

Viewing systems through a complete life cycle allows engineers to plan for continuous change and avoid these massive financial failures. The systems development life cycle gives a team a map of a system’s whole life. It guides the project from the first idea to the final shutdown. The linear model offers predictability but leaves little room for change. The iterative model offers room to learn but needs steady management of changing needs. The incremental model delivers pieces early but only works when a system can be safely divided. Thinking in terms of the life cycle helps designers plan for change and aging technology instead of treating the first release as the end of the story.

Module 2.1 Blog Post

User Error: Designing for People

Picture the entrance to a building with a pair of doors. Each door has a handle on the outside. A person grabs the handle and pulls. The door does not move, so they pull harder. Then they spot a small sticker that says to push. Most people feel embarrassed in that moment, but they should not. People expect a door handle to mean pull because past interactions build an expectation of how doors work (Yablonski, 2024). Psychologists call this internal picture a mental model. It helps us navigate new tools based on past experience. The shape of the handle also acts as an affordance, which is a visual cue that signals to your brain what action to perform. The door is not broken. Its design simply gave the wrong visual cue, and the sticker is a fix added after the fact.

A door is a low-stakes example. A similar design failure in medical equipment or emergency alert systems can cause real harm (Thomas & McDonagh, 2013). Technology cannot operate in a vacuum. Systems must be built around human psychology and limited memory because humans are the ones making the system work. Many errors that get blamed on users can actually be traced to bad design (Yablonski, 2024). Systems are commonly designed around what the technology can do or what is easiest to build. They ignore how humans naturally think and behave, forcing people to adapt to rigid machines that set them up to fail.

Human-centered design reverses this approach (IDEO.org, 2015). This is a design philosophy that places the needs, capabilities, and limitations of users over what the machine or system requires. Instead of forcing people to adapt to confusing technology, the technology is made to adapt to the people using it. Designing for people means placing real human behavior at the center of system development.

Perception and Attention

People are part of every system, and when a design does not fit the person, the whole system breaks down. Designers build better systems by acknowledging human cognitive limits. These are the natural boundaries of human thinking, memory, and visual processing. Human vision does not capture an exact, unfiltered picture of the world. Perception is an active process where the brain constantly filters what we see based on our past experiences and current goals (Johnson, 2020). Shoppers in a grocery store do not read every single product label on the shelf. They use selective attention to scan aisles quickly for visual clues. This is the brain's ability to focus on specific details while ignoring background noise. They also follow information scent (Johnson, 2020). These are visual cues, like aisle category signs or bold headings, that point users toward the information they want.

Working memory can only hold a small amount of information at once before getting overloaded. When a screen or environment feels messy and crowded, people experience cognitive load (Johnson, 2020). This means the design makes users work harder than necessary to complete a basic task. Good design organizes information in a way that makes sense so users can make choices without extra effort. When designers ignore how people perceive and process information, they build systems that are confusing and hard to use.

Empathy and Early-Stage Design

Understanding these cognitive limits requires empathy (IDEO.org, 2015). In design, empathy is not about feeling what someone else feels. It is about looking at a system through the user's eyes to understand what they want, need, and expect. Designers naturally approach problems with their own biases and assumptions (Thomas & McDonagh, 2013). Empathy forces them to observe how different people interact with the world in natural environments. Without empathy, designers tend to project their own personal assumptions onto the product. Empathy shifts the focus away from just building technical features. It helps designers figure out what really drives users, since products need to fulfill emotional needs just as much as practical ones (Thomas & McDonagh, 2013). If a product makes the user frustrated or conflicts with their values, they will use it less or not at all.

The choices designers make at the beginning determine the quality of the system. Good decisions make a system easy to use, but bad ones cause problems that are much harder to fix. Sometimes teams design products around what is convenient for developers rather than what works for real people. This disconnect is known as the gulf of execution (Johnson, 2020). This gap is the difference between what a user wants to do and the extra steps a system forces them to take. Adding extra features or mixing different layout styles makes the system complicated and harder to learn. If controls are inconsistent, using the product never becomes second nature. Users end up wasting mental energy figuring out what to click next.

Before designing visual layouts and dashboards, teams should create a conceptual model. This is a big-picture blueprint that outlines how the system works from the user's perspective while ignoring technical details (Johnson, 2020). The best way to build this blueprint is by observing real people in their daily routines (Thomas & McDonagh, 2013). What people say and what people do are often two different things, so watching them reveals their true habits. Trying to fix design flaws later costs a lot of time and money. Instead, testing simple models early with real users allows teams to identify problems while changes are still cheap and easy to make (Ripalda et al., 2020).

Structured Design Frameworks and Visual Hierarchy

Human-centered design often relies on two complementary frameworks. The ISO 9241-210 standard offers an international process model for software and systems engineering (Franz & Kauer-Franz, 2024). This framework follows a repeatable six-step process that acts as a tool for managing risk and protecting investments. Teams plan their approach, define who will use the system and how, outline specific user requirements, build prototypes or simple models, evaluate those designs with real users, and repeat the cycle until all user needs are met (Franz & Kauer-Franz, 2024). This prevents organizations from wasting time and money on unusable systems. By requiring teams to meet clear usability benchmarks, it ensures the system succeeds in the real world.

Another helpful framework is the IDEO Field Guide to Human-Centered Design. It offers a hands-on approach that focuses on creative problem-solving built around real human needs (IDEO.org, 2015). It guides teams through three distinct phases. The Inspiration phase focuses on observing real people to learn about their daily needs and challenges. The Ideation phase focuses on brainstorming a wide range of ideas and testing early models. The Implementation phase focuses on bringing the product to life and making sure it works. This approach requires teams to accept uncertainty, build trust in their creative skills, and learn directly from mistakes. Throughout this process, teams test their ideas against three key standards (IDEO.org, 2015). They look at desirability, which is what people need. They check feasibility, which is what can actually be built. They also ensure viability, which is what an organization can sustain over time.

While both frameworks put the user first, they solve problems in different ways. The ISO 9241-210 standard relies on structured engineering guidelines to reduce technical risk and eliminate guesswork (Franz & Kauer-Franz, 2024). In contrast, the IDEO Field Guide offers a flexible hands-on method built around empathy, creative thinking, and fast experimentation (IDEO.org, 2015). The ISO process gives developers a reliable path to ensure a system works, while IDEO gives teams the flexibility to discover what users truly care about. Combining both approaches allows teams to balance strict, formal planning with creative innovation.

Designers use natural rules of vision to guide user focus without adding confusing instructions. Through the principle of proximity, the brain automatically groups items that are close to each other (Ripalda et al., 2020). This is why mobile layouts place related buttons together so users instantly know they share a similar purpose. Through the principle of similarity, the mind assumes that objects sharing the same color or shape perform identical tasks (Ripalda et al., 2020). By keeping all clickable links the exact same color, users do not have to guess what is interactive. The figure-ground principle explains how the brain separates the main subject from the background (Yablonski, 2024). This explains why a screen dims behind a pop-up window, forcing the user to focus entirely on a single message.

These rules establish visual hierarchy, which is the intentional arrangement of a layout to show which parts are most important (Yablonski, 2024). By shifting the size, position, and color of elements, designers make key details stand out. If actions look too similar, the brain is forced to work extra hard to process every choice equally. Using bold, colored buttons for main actions and faint links for minor choices keeps layouts intuitive and prevents mental fatigue (Yablonski, 2024).

Applying Psychological Rules to Interface Design

Once designers establish a clear visual hierarchy, they apply foundational psychological laws to fine-tune daily user interactions. These rules translate human perception and memory limits into concrete interface mechanics. For example, Jakob’s Law builds directly on existing mental models by stating that users spend most of their time on other digital products, meaning they expect new systems to work like the ones they already know (Yablonski, 2024). Instead of forcing people to relearn basic navigation, designers leverage familiar conventions so users can accomplish their goals without extra effort.

To manage working memory limits and prevent cognitive overload, designers rely on Hick’s Law and Miller’s Law (Yablonski, 2024). Hick’s Law explains that the time required to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices. Grouping related items and hiding secondary features behind progressive menus reduces decision friction and prevents choice paralysis. Similarly, Miller’s Law highlights that human short-term memory has natural capacity limits. Rather than presenting overwhelming lists of unformatted data, designers use chunking to organize content into small, scannable clusters that the brain can easily process (Yablonski, 2024).

Finally, Fitts’s Law connects visual hierarchy to physical movement by showing that the time required to hit a target depends on its distance and size (Yablonski, 2024). Making primary buttons larger and placing interactive elements close to a user's natural cursor or thumb path reduces physical effort and minimizes costly misclicks. Together, these psychological laws ensure that digital systems respect human capabilities at every level of interaction.

Conclusion

Building successful technology requires far more than writing functional software code. It demands a deep commitment to human-centered design principles that align system interfaces with natural human psychology. By pairing the structured risk management of ISO 9241-210 with the creative empathy of the IDEO Field Guide, development teams create products that balance engineering rigor with genuine human needs (Franz & Kauer-Franz, 2024). Grounding visual layouts in Gestalt perception and the Laws of UX eliminates unnecessary cognitive friction, allowing users to navigate interfaces effortlessly. Ultimately, designing for people prevents costly system failures and ensures that technology serves as an empowering tool rather than a source of frustration.

Module 2.2 Blog Post

The Cognitive Shield: Designing for Human Memory

People use apps and websites every day to shop, work, and learn. However, confusing technology causes a shifting of blame. Users often fault themselves for mistakes instead of the poor design (Johnson, 2020). Good design goes far beyond making a screen look nice. It focuses on how a tool actually works and how it supports human thought (Johnson, 2020). Software works best when it adapts to people rather than forcing users to learn complicated systems (Yablonski, 2024). Designers build programs around natural human habits. They present clear options that people recognize instantly (Yablonski, 2024). This eliminates the need to memorize commands (Yablonski, 2024). Complex tasks can happen invisibly in the background. This technology protects users from mental fatigue (Yablonski, 2024). Good design acts as a shield against frustration. It keeps users focused entirely on their actual goals.

Understanding Cognitive Demands and Memory

Human working memory has strict physical limits. Early psychological studies estimated that people could hold about seven items in their mind at once (Johnson, 2020). Modern researchers later revised this number. They accounted for how people naturally group information together. Science now shows that working memory can only handle about four distinct pieces of information at a time (Johnson, 2020). Think of it like juggling. An average person can toss one or two items back and forth without much trouble. The person will likely drop everything if someone throws a third or fourth item into the mix. The information held in working memory disappears almost immediately when a person gets distracted (Johnson, 2020). An interface might force someone to memorize a long confirmation code. It might make them remember what they typed on a previous screen. This drains their mental energy. This mental drain leads directly to user errors (Johnson, 2020).

Researchers call this mental effort cognitive load. Cognitive Load Theory splits this effort into two main categories (Darejeh et al., 2026). Intrinsic load is the natural difficulty of the task itself (Darejeh et al., 2026). Extraneous load is the unnecessary mental effort caused by confusing layouts and bad design (Darejeh et al., 2026). Building a financial spreadsheet has a high intrinsic load. The math and data relationships are naturally complicated (Darejeh et al., 2026). The spreadsheet software might use messy menus and hide important tools. This adds a massive extraneous load. The user is forced to waste brain power fighting the interface instead of focusing on their financial data. Good interfaces minimize this extraneous load to save working memory for the actual task (Darejeh et al., 2026).

Designers must also think about human habits. People spend the majority of their time using other apps and websites (Yablonski, 2024). They build mental models of how software should behave based on this extensive experience. This concept is known as Jakob's Law (Yablonski, 2024). A new app might follow standard design rules. Users navigate it using automatic habits that save mental energy (Johnson, 2020). An app might break these familiar patterns instead. This forces the user to stop and think about every single click. This conscious problem solving burns through working memory and exhausts the user quickly (Johnson, 2020).

Recognition Over Recall in Digital Environments

The human brain evolved to be incredibly good at recognizing patterns. Recognizing a visual object requires little mental effort. The sensory input matches a pattern already stored in the brain (Johnson, 2020). This creates a fast interaction style where users see and choose what they want (Johnson, 2020). Recall is a completely different process. It requires the brain to dig up information from memory without any visual clues on the screen (Johnson, 2020). This interaction style forces users to remember and type exact information. The process is slow and highly prone to mistakes (Johnson, 2020). Modern graphical interfaces work well because they use visible menus and buttons. These visible options let users recognize what they want. They do not force people to memorize exact text commands (Johnson, 2020).

Recent academic research proves how well recognition based design works. A 2025 study tested a medical symptom checker. The goal was to see how design choices affected human perception (Alhasan & Alnanih, 2025). The researchers split people into two groups. The experimental group used an updated version of the tool. This version applied Gestalt theory to group related items together (Alhasan & Alnanih, 2025). It also used Feature Integration Theory to highlight important details like confidence scores (Alhasan & Alnanih, 2025). The control group used a basic version with a uniform layout. It lacked any clear visual structure (Alhasan & Alnanih, 2025).

The results of this study were clear. People using the improved interface reached a 90 percent good performance rate on recognition tasks (Alhasan & Alnanih, 2025). The control group only managed a 20 percent success rate on the exact same tasks (Alhasan & Alnanih, 2025). The experimental group gave the system a much higher rating for being easy to understand. They rated it 4.8 out of 5. The control group averaged just 3.1 (Alhasan & Alnanih, 2025). The designers organized information to support natural human perception. This made it much easier for people to interpret complex medical data accurately.

Shifting the Burden with Tesler's Law

Developers must take on the heavy lifting to protect the user's working memory. This concept is known as Tesler's Law (Yablonski, 2024). Every digital process has a certain amount of baseline difficulty. No one can remove this difficulty entirely (Yablonski, 2024). The hard work has to go somewhere. Imagine ordering a pizza online. The store needs your exact delivery address and payment details to complete the sale. Gathering that information is a natural complication. The developers decide who handles that hard work (Yablonski, 2024). A development team might not write the code to automate this process. The burden then falls on the person using the screen (Yablonski, 2024). The user has to manually type their full address and credit card number every time. This penalizes the user and makes their daily tasks much harder (Yablonski, 2024).

Everyday digital tools show Tesler's Law in action. Think about writing an email. A modern email application will automatically fill in your sender information. It will suggest the recipient's address based on people you talk to often (Yablonski, 2024). The complicated process of routing a digital message across the world still exists. The software absorbs the friction. You only have to click a single button to confirm your choice (Yablonski, 2024).

Online shopping uses the same concept. Payment services like Apple Pay let you buy items by looking at your phone to verify your identity (Yablonski, 2024). This removes the need to manually type a long billing address and a credit card number. Physical retail stores like Amazon Go take this idea to the absolute limit. They use advanced cameras and sensors. You can walk in and grab an item and walk out without ever standing in a checkout line (Yablonski, 2024). Companies spent massive amounts of engineering effort in all these examples. Their goal was to remove steps from the user's daily routine.

There is a risk in taking this simplification too far. Hiding too much information makes a system abstract and confusing (Yablonski, 2024). Good systems support the way people think by following an action cycle. This loop involves forming a goal and taking an action. The user then evaluates what the system does in response (Johnson, 2020). The backend code should handle the difficult execution steps. The screen must still provide clear and immediate feedback. This helps the person know exactly what the software is doing (Johnson, 2020). People feel lost and out of control without this feedback.

Interface Consistency and Visual Ergonomics

Keeping an interface consistent is the best way to shift operational effort away from the user. An app might use the exact same interaction rules on every screen. People do not have to learn new habits as they navigate (Johnson, 2020). Inconsistent interfaces make things harder every time a new feature is added (Johnson, 2020). Consistent interfaces grow smoothly without adding extra mental strain. Standardized design stops people from falling back into slow problem solving mode.

The impact of this visual consistency is easy to measure in high stress work environments. A 2026 systematic review looked at visual ergonomics. It studied how these designs helped reduce human error in industrial settings (Vizcarra et al., 2026). The researchers found that adding clear visual hierarchy dropped operational errors by 30 to 70 percent. They also found success using modular layouts and color coding and real time feedback (Vizcarra et al., 2026). These exact same ergonomic changes improved the time it took to complete a task by 20 to 60 percent (Vizcarra et al., 2026).

The data from this review paints a clear picture. Systems that fully applied these visual ergonomic rules showed frequent errors in only 16.7 percent of cases (Vizcarra et al., 2026). Systems that ignored these rules had a 100 percent frequent error rate (Vizcarra et al., 2026). Consistent visual structures act as a direct safety net that prevents human failure.

Usability testers use objective metrics to track these improvements. Another 2026 study analyzed 87 different experiments on how to measure cognitive load (Darejeh et al., 2026). The researchers discovered the most common ways to test usability. These included performance measures like error rates and task completion times (Darejeh et al., 2026). These objective numbers perfectly match what users say on subjective surveys (Darejeh et al., 2026). People make fewer mistakes and finish tasks faster. This proves that a consistent visual design has successfully lowered their mental burden (Darejeh et al., 2026).

Real World Applications of Good and Bad Design

Looking at specific examples shows how these cognitive rules succeed or fail in everyday life. A famous example of bad design comes from a bank wire transfer screen. The digital system showed users two buttons labeled OK and Cancel (Johnson, 2020). Clicking the OK button actually confirmed that the user wanted to cancel the money transfer (Johnson, 2020). Clicking the Cancel button stopped the cancellation request entirely (Johnson, 2020).

This confusing text provided a misleading visual scent. It forced users to stop and solve a frustrating logic puzzle. They could not rely on instant recognition (Johnson, 2020). A much better solution uses explicit labels. A hotel reservation system might use buttons that say Yes Cancel Reservation and No (Johnson, 2020). These explicit labels match the user's mental goal perfectly. They eliminate all the friction.

Early search engines failed to respect the limits of working memory. These early websites would erase the original search words from the input box at the top of the screen (Johnson, 2020). This happened immediately after showing the search results. This forced the user to memorize their exact search parameters while reading through the list of results. This heavy memory demand often left people feeling disoriented (Johnson, 2020). Modern search engines leave your query in the box. This provides a constant visual record that relieves the brain's memory burden.

A common failure today is the use of unlabeled mystery icons in web apps. Toolbars often rely only on abstract shapes without any text labels. These shapes violate the core rule of recognition (Johnson, 2020). They force people to recall the meaning of the icon from memory. Users have to click randomly to see what happens if they cannot remember (Johnson, 2020). Adding clear text labels or immediate hover descriptions fixes this issue. It restores the fast see and choose dynamic and keeps the mental effort low.

A software program might grow too large over time. Progressive disclosure is the best way to handle the resulting clutter (Yablonski, 2024). This design technique hides advanced settings inside secondary menus. It only displays the primary actions on the main screen (Yablonski, 2024). This respects Tesler's Law by keeping the default view clean. It still provides all the features that advanced users need (Yablonski, 2024).

Building for Human Limits

Digital usability does not happen by accident. It is the direct result of building systems that respect natural human limits (Johnson, 2020). Working memory is highly limited. Digital screens must prioritize easy visual recognition over difficult memory recall because of this limit (Johnson, 2020). Designers keep interfaces strictly consistent and provide clear feedback. They build strong visual hierarchies. These choices stop people from feeling mentally exhausted. Development teams need to embrace Tesler's Law. They must absorb the baseline complexity in their software code. They cannot push that burden onto the user (Yablonski, 2024). The best digital tools empower human thinking instead of draining it. The main rule for usable design is clear. Developers must shift the hard work to the code and design for easy recognition. The machine must hold the memory burden.
