Seeing the Whole System: Why Systems Thinking and MBSE Matter in Digital Systems

Seeing the Whole System

When I think about a complex digital system, my first instinct is usually to break it into smaller pieces. This makes the problem easier to understand because I can focus on one part at a time. However, systems thinking shows that understanding the individual parts is not enough. A system can have components that work perfectly on their own and still fail when they interact with other components. This is why looking at the relationships between parts is just as important as looking at the parts themselves.

Systems thinking is a way of looking at a system as a whole while also considering its environment, boundaries, interfaces, and interactions. The International Council on Systems Engineering (INCOSE, 2018) explains that systems thinking considers how decisions and actions in one part of a system can affect other parts. This is important because improving one component does not always improve the overall system.

This idea changed the way I think about digital systems. It is easy to think about technology in a linear way: a user enters information, a program processes it, and the system produces an output. That approach can work for simple problems, but real-world systems are rarely that simple. A small change in one area can create unexpected effects somewhere else.

Looking Beyond Individual Components

For example, consider an online shopping system. It may include a website, mobile application, database, payment system, inventory system, shipping service, authentication process, cybersecurity controls, and customer support. Each component has a specific purpose, but the customer experiences them as one system. If the payment system works but the inventory information is incorrect, the overall experience can still fail. Similarly, a website may be fast and easy to use, but if authentication or payment processing does not work properly, the system has not achieved its purpose.

This also connects to the idea of system complexity discussed by Holt (2023). One challenge of systems engineering is that complexity can make it difficult for people to understand how everything fits together. Communication can also become difficult when different people focus on different parts of a system. A developer, business manager, customer, and security professional may all look at the same system from different perspectives. None of these perspectives is necessarily wrong, but they may emphasize different goals and concerns.

Systems thinking encourages us to consider these different perspectives together. Instead of asking only, “Does this component work?” I think a better question is, “How does this component affect the rest of the system?” That question can reveal problems that might otherwise remain hidden.

Thinking About the Entire Life Cycle

Another important part of systems thinking is considering the system throughout its life cycle. A system is not simply designed and then finished. It may be developed, tested, deployed, operated, maintained, modified, and eventually replaced or retired. A decision that seems reasonable during development could create problems later during maintenance or operation. Thinking about the entire life cycle helps system designers consider those future effects instead of focusing only on the current stage.

Why Interfaces Matter

Interfaces are another area where systems thinking becomes especially important. Two applications may each work correctly when tested independently but fail when they exchange information. For example, one application might send information in a format that the other application cannot interpret. The problem is not necessarily inside either application. It exists in the relationship between them. This is one reason why focusing only on individual components can provide an incomplete picture.

Before learning about systems thinking, I might have considered these issues as separate technical problems. Now I see that many of them are connected. A system's success depends not only on the quality of its individual components but also on how those components work together and support the needs of users and other stakeholders.

Moving Toward Model-Based Systems Engineering

This way of thinking leads naturally to Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE). If modern systems are too complex to understand through isolated pieces of information, engineers need a better way to represent the relationships among those pieces. MBSE provides a way to do that by using models as an important part of understanding and developing a system.

In the next part, I will look more closely at how MBSE supports systems thinking and why modeling can be useful when developing complex digital systems.

From Systems Thinking to MBSE

Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE) is more than simply creating diagrams. What I find most useful about MBSE is the idea of making a model a central representation of the system and its relationships. Instead of depending entirely on separate documents, spreadsheets, and diagrams, a model can help organize information about the system in a connected way.

Traditional document-based approaches can become difficult to manage as a system becomes more complex. Information may be stored in many different documents, and a change in one requirement may require changes in several other places. If those changes are missed, different documents can contain conflicting information. MBSE helps address this problem by providing a more connected way to represent system information (Holt, 2023).

Why Models Matter

However, a model should not be viewed as a complete copy of reality. A real system can contain an enormous amount of information, so a model is an abstraction that focuses on information important for understanding and designing the system. The value of the model comes from helping people see important relationships without having to examine every detail at once.

This seems especially useful for modern digital systems. A digital system may involve software, hardware, networks, databases, users, business processes, security controls, and external services. Looking at these elements separately can make the system seem manageable, but understanding how they interact can become much more difficult. A model can provide a structured way to represent those interactions.

Communication and Traceability

MBSE can also improve communication. Different stakeholders may use different terminology or focus on different concerns. A shared model can provide a common representation of the system that people from different areas can discuss. This does not eliminate disagreements, but it can make those disagreements easier to identify and discuss.

Another benefit is traceability. For example, a system requirement can be connected to design decisions and the parts of the system that are expected to satisfy that requirement. If the requirement changes, the team can better understand which parts of the design may also need to change. This becomes increasingly important as systems grow larger and more interconnected.

NASA's systems engineering guidance also emphasizes the importance of a multidisciplinary and systematic approach to developing complex systems (National Aeronautics and Space Administration [NASA], 2017). This supports the broader idea behind systems thinking: system-level decisions cannot always be made by looking at one technical area in isolation.

A Different Way of Thinking About Systems

At the same time, I do not think systems thinking means that we should stop using simple, linear thinking. Linear thinking is still useful when solving a small and clearly defined problem. The challenge is knowing when that approach is no longer enough. When many components, stakeholders, interfaces, and life-cycle stages are connected, looking at relationships becomes much more important.

The biggest lesson I take from systems thinking and MBSE is that complexity does not disappear simply because we divide a system into smaller pieces. Breaking a system apart can help us understand it, but we also have to put the pieces back into context and understand how they interact.

This has changed the questions I would ask when evaluating a digital system. Instead of asking only whether the system technically works, I would also ask whether it meets user needs, how it interacts with other systems, what happens when requirements change, whether different stakeholders understand it, and how it will be maintained throughout its life cycle.

What I Take Away From Systems Thinking and MBSE

For me, systems thinking is the mindset, while MBSE is one practical way to apply that mindset. Systems thinking encourages us to see the whole and understand relationships. MBSE provides tools and models that can help represent those relationships in a structured way. Together, they offer a better approach to dealing with the complexity of modern systems.

The more I learn about digital systems, the more I realize that successful engineering is not simply about making individual components work. It is about making the entire system work together toward a meaningful purpose. That is why systems thinking and MBSE are valuable: they help us move from thinking only about individual parts to understanding the system as a whole.
