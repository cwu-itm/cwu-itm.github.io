Seeing the Whole System: Why Systems Thinking and MBSE Matter in Digital Systems

Seeing the Whole System

When I think about a complex digital system, my first instinct is usually to break it into smaller pieces. This makes the problem easier to understand because I can focus on one part at a time. However, systems thinking shows that understanding the individual parts is not enough. A system can have components that work perfectly on their own and still fail when they interact with other components. This is why looking at the relationships between parts is just as important as looking at the parts themselves.

Systems thinking is a way of looking at a system as a whole while also considering its environment, boundaries, interfaces, and interactions. The International Council on Systems Engineering (INCOSE, 2018) explains that systems thinking considers how decisions and actions in one part of a system can affect other parts. This is important because improving one component does not always improve the overall system.

This idea changed the way I think about digital systems. It is easy to think about technology in a linear way: a user enters information, a program processes it, and the system produces an output. That approach can work for simple problems, but real-world systems are rarely that simple. A small change in one area can create unexpected effects somewhere else.

Looking Beyond Individual Components

For example, consider an online shopping system. It may include a website, mobile application, database, payment system, inventory system, shipping service, authentication process, cybersecurity controls, and customer support. Each component has a specific purpose, but the customer experiences them as one system. If the payment system works but the inventory information is incorrect, the overall experience can still fail. Similarly, a website may be fast and easy to use, but if authentication or payment processing does not work properly, the system has not achieved its purpose.

This also connects to the idea of system complexity discussed by Holt (2023). One challenge of systems engineering is that complexity can make it difficult for people to understand how everything fits together. Communication can also become difficult when different people focus on different parts of a system. A developer, business manager, customer, and security professional may all look at the same system from different perspectives. None of these perspectives is necessarily wrong, but they may emphasize different goals and concerns.

Systems thinking encourages us to consider these different perspectives together. Instead of asking only, “Does this component work?” I think a better question is, “How does this component affect the rest of the system?” That question can reveal problems that might otherwise remain hidden.

Thinking About the Entire Life Cycle

Another important part of systems thinking is considering the system throughout its life cycle. A system is not simply designed and then finished. It may be developed, tested, deployed, operated, maintained, modified, and eventually replaced or retired. A decision that seems reasonable during development could create problems later during maintenance or operation. Thinking about the entire life cycle helps system designers consider those future effects instead of focusing only on the current stage.

Why Interfaces Matter

Interfaces are another area where systems thinking becomes especially important. Two applications may each work correctly when tested independently but fail when they exchange information. For example, one application might send information in a format that the other application cannot interpret. The problem is not necessarily inside either application. It exists in the relationship between them. This is one reason why focusing only on individual components can provide an incomplete picture.

Before learning about systems thinking, I might have considered these issues as separate technical problems. Now I see that many of them are connected. A system's success depends not only on the quality of its individual components but also on how those components work together and support the needs of users and other stakeholders.

Moving Toward Model-Based Systems Engineering

This way of thinking leads naturally to Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE). If modern systems are too complex to understand through isolated pieces of information, engineers need a better way to represent the relationships among those pieces. MBSE provides a way to do that by using models as an important part of understanding and developing a system.

In the next part, I will look more closely at how MBSE supports systems thinking and why modeling can be useful when developing complex digital systems.

From Systems Thinking to MBSE

Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE) is more than simply creating diagrams. What I find most useful about MBSE is the idea of making a model a central representation of the system and its relationships. Instead of depending entirely on separate documents, spreadsheets, and diagrams, a model can help organize information about the system in a connected way.

Traditional document-based approaches can become difficult to manage as a system becomes more complex. Information may be stored in many different documents, and a change in one requirement may require changes in several other places. If those changes are missed, different documents can contain conflicting information. MBSE helps address this problem by providing a more connected way to represent system information (Holt, 2023).

Why Models Matter

However, a model should not be viewed as a complete copy of reality. A real system can contain an enormous amount of information, so a model is an abstraction that focuses on information important for understanding and designing the system. The value of the model comes from helping people see important relationships without having to examine every detail at once.

This seems especially useful for modern digital systems. A digital system may involve software, hardware, networks, databases, users, business processes, security controls, and external services. Looking at these elements separately can make the system seem manageable, but understanding how they interact can become much more difficult. A model can provide a structured way to represent those interactions.

Communication and Traceability

MBSE can also improve communication. Different stakeholders may use different terminology or focus on different concerns. A shared model can provide a common representation of the system that people from different areas can discuss. This does not eliminate disagreements, but it can make those disagreements easier to identify and discuss.

Another benefit is traceability. For example, a system requirement can be connected to design decisions and the parts of the system that are expected to satisfy that requirement. If the requirement changes, the team can better understand which parts of the design may also need to change. This becomes increasingly important as systems grow larger and more interconnected.

NASA's systems engineering guidance also emphasizes the importance of a multidisciplinary and systematic approach to developing complex systems (National Aeronautics and Space Administration [NASA], 2017). This supports the broader idea behind systems thinking: system-level decisions cannot always be made by looking at one technical area in isolation.

A Different Way of Thinking About Systems

At the same time, I do not think systems thinking means that we should stop using simple, linear thinking. Linear thinking is still useful when solving a small and clearly defined problem. The challenge is knowing when that approach is no longer enough. When many components, stakeholders, interfaces, and life-cycle stages are connected, looking at relationships becomes much more important.

The biggest lesson I take from systems thinking and MBSE is that complexity does not disappear simply because we divide a system into smaller pieces. Breaking a system apart can help us understand it, but we also have to put the pieces back into context and understand how they interact.

This has changed the questions I would ask when evaluating a digital system. Instead of asking only whether the system technically works, I would also ask whether it meets user needs, how it interacts with other systems, what happens when requirements change, whether different stakeholders understand it, and how it will be maintained throughout its life cycle.

What I Take Away From Systems Thinking and MBSE

For me, systems thinking is the mindset, while MBSE is one practical way to apply that mindset. Systems thinking encourages us to see the whole and understand relationships. MBSE provides tools and models that can help represent those relationships in a structured way. Together, they offer a better approach to dealing with the complexity of modern systems.

The more I learn about digital systems, the more I realize that successful engineering is not simply about making individual components work. It is about making the entire system work together toward a meaningful purpose. That is why systems thinking and MBSE are valuable: they help us move from thinking only about individual parts to understanding the system as a whole.

Thinking in Life Cycles: Choosing the Right Path to Build Better Digital Systems

Introduction: Building a System Is Only the Beginning

Developing a digital system involves more than designing software and making it work. A system must meet user needs, remain reliable, adapt to changes, and eventually be maintained or retired. Life cycle thinking helps engineers consider these responsibilities from the beginning rather than focusing only on development. Different life cycle models organize the work in different ways, so choosing an appropriate model can affect a system’s quality, cost, flexibility, and long-term usefulness.

Developing a digital system also requires balancing different priorities, including user expectations, available resources, technical limitations, and future needs. A system that works well today may become less effective as technology changes or users expect new features. Therefore, engineers need to consider not only how to build a system but also how to keep it useful and reliable over time. Life cycle models help organize these responsibilities and guide decisions throughout development.

Life cycle thinking is especially important because decisions made early can influence maintenance costs, system flexibility, and future improvements. Understanding the available models helps teams select an approach that fits their goals and reduces avoidable problems.

What Is the Systems Development Life Cycle?

In my understanding, the systems development life cycle (SDLC) is the organized process of planning, developing, using, maintaining, and eventually retiring a system. It provides a framework for understanding how a system evolves and what activities are needed at different stages. Holt (2023) explains that a system life cycle can include six stages: conception, development, production, utilization, support, and retirement.

During conception, stakeholders’ needs are identified. Development involves exploring possible solutions and selecting an appropriate design. Production creates and tests the actual system. Utilization begins when users operate the system, while support includes maintenance and problem resolution. Finally, retirement involves taking the system out of service responsibly. These stages may be arranged differently depending on the selected life cycle model.

The life cycle model describes how the stages are organized and completed. The three important models are linear, iterative, and incremental. Each offers different benefits and limitations depending on the project’s requirements.

The Linear Life Cycle Model: Structure and Predictability

The linear model follows a defined sequence, with each major stage generally completed before the next begins. Teams typically identify requirements, design the system, build it, test it, and then release it. This approach works well when requirements are clearly understood and unlikely to change significantly (Holt, 2023).

One advantage is predictability. Teams can establish schedules, budgets, responsibilities, and documentation early. Completing major stages in order can also make progress easier to track. For example, a university developing a simple internal application with stable requirements might benefit from planning the system thoroughly before construction begins.

However, the linear model has limitations. If users discover new needs after development has progressed, changing the system can become expensive and time-consuming. Users may also wait until late in the process to see a working product. This makes the model less suitable for projects with uncertain requirements or rapidly changing technology.

Therefore, I would choose a linear approach when the system is relatively stable, well-defined, and unlikely to require frequent changes. It offers structure, but its success depends heavily on understanding requirements early.

The Iterative Life Cycle Model: Learning Through Repetition

The iterative model develops a system through repeated cycles of work. Instead of expecting the first version to be complete, the team builds an initial version, evaluates it, collects feedback, and improves it in later iterations. Holt (2023) describes iterative development as an evolutionary approach in which repeated work supports learning and refinement.

A major advantage is flexibility. Teams can identify problems earlier and use feedback to improve the system. For example, a university might develop an initial student portal, test its navigation with students, and revise confusing features before releasing a more polished version. This process can help the team understand what users actually need rather than relying entirely on early assumptions.

The iterative model also has drawbacks. Repeated development and testing require time and resources. If changes are poorly controlled, the project may expand beyond its original scope. Teams need clear priorities, regular evaluation, and appropriate documentation to keep the work manageable.

IBM (n.d.) explains that software development life cycle approaches provide structured ways to plan and manage development activities. The appropriate process depends on project needs and constraints. In practice, iterative development is particularly useful when requirements are expected to evolve or when user feedback is important to the final result.

The Incremental Life Cycle Model: Delivering Value in Parts

The incremental model divides a system into smaller, useful portions that can be delivered in separate releases. The team considers the overall system but develops and delivers selected capabilities in stages. Each increment adds functionality until the system meets its intended needs (Holt, 2023).

For example, a university student portal could first provide course registration, followed by grade viewing, tuition payment, and advising features. Students could begin using the available functions without waiting for every feature to be completed.

This model’s main advantage is that it delivers useful functionality earlier. Teams can prioritize essential features, receive feedback from actual users, and spread development effort across releases. It can also help organizations manage risk by avoiding a single large delivery at the end.

However, incremental development requires careful planning. The system must be divided into portions that can work meaningfully, and those portions must integrate properly. If the team overlooks shared data, security, or interfaces, later increments may create compatibility problems. Some systems also cannot be divided easily into independently useful components.

The incremental model is therefore most appropriate when a system can be delivered in manageable parts and users benefit from receiving important features early.

Comparing the Three Life Cycle Models

The three models differ mainly in how they organize development and respond to change.

The linear model emphasizes a planned sequence. It supports predictable scheduling and works best when requirements are stable, but late changes can be costly.

The iterative model emphasizes repeated evaluation and improvement. It supports learning and adaptation, but requires ongoing coordination and control.

The incremental model emphasizes delivering functionality in separate portions. It provides value earlier, but requires careful integration and a design that supports staged delivery.

These approaches are not simply competing choices. A project may combine their strengths when appropriate. For example, a team could plan an overall architecture in advance, develop features incrementally, and refine each feature iteratively. The important consideration is whether the chosen approach matches the system’s complexity, uncertainty, user needs, and constraints.

Why Life Cycle Thinking Matters for Digital Systems

Life cycle thinking matters because digital systems continue to affect people and organizations after their initial release. A system may need security updates, maintenance, new features, compatibility improvements, and eventually replacement. Ignoring these responsibilities can lead to unexpected costs, unreliable services, and risks to users.

Systems engineering also considers how system components interact. Holt (2023) explains that systems contain elements connected through interfaces. When a digital system is updated, changes to one component may affect other components. For example, adding a payment feature to a student portal may require changes to databases, authentication, and reporting. Considering these relationships throughout the life cycle helps teams reduce integration problems.

Life cycle thinking is also important for sustainability. A system designed only for immediate delivery may become difficult to maintain or require premature replacement. Planning for efficient resource use, maintainability, upgrades, and responsible retirement can extend a system’s useful life. The NASA Systems Engineering Handbook (National Aeronautics and Space Administration [NASA], 2017) emphasizes systematic engineering across the life cycle, supporting consideration of a system beyond its initial design and development.

Security should also be considered throughout the life cycle. Teams need to think about protecting information during design, testing, operation, maintenance, and retirement. Similarly, changing technologies can make systems obsolete or incompatible with newer platforms. Planning for updates and eventual retirement helps organizations manage these changes responsibly.

My Reflection: Choosing the Right Model

If I were involved in developing a university student portal, I would favor a combination of iterative and incremental development. I would first identify the main user needs and plan the overall system. Then, I would release essential features in increments and improve them using feedback from students and university staff.

This approach would allow users to benefit from the portal before every feature was finished. It would also help the team identify usability issues and correct problems as development continued. However, I would establish clear priorities, document important decisions, and test connections between components to prevent uncontrolled changes and integration problems.

I would not automatically use this combination for every project. A small system with stable requirements might be better suited to a linear approach. My main lesson is that life cycle models should be selected based on the project’s needs rather than personal preference alone.

Conclusion

The systems development life cycle provides a way to consider a system from its initial conception through development, use, support, and retirement. Linear development offers structure, iterative development supports continuous improvement, and incremental development delivers useful features in stages. Each has advantages and limitations, so the best choice depends on the system and its environment.

For me, life cycle thinking means recognizing that building a working system is only one part of successful engineering. Systems must remain useful, secure, maintainable, and adaptable over time. Choosing an appropriate life cycle model helps teams make better decisions throughout that journey.

Human First: Designing Digital Experiences Around How People Think and Feel

Introduction: A Working Interface Is Not Always a Usable Interface

Imagine a student opening Canvas shortly before an assignment deadline. The student needs to find the instructions, upload a file, and confirm that the submission was successful. However, the page contains competing links, the submission button is difficult to identify, and the confirmation message is easy to overlook. Canvas may be working technically, but the experience still creates confusion and stress. This illustrates an important principle of human-centered design: a system is not successful simply because its features work. It must also help people accomplish their goals.

Human-centered design (HCD) focuses on the people who use a product, service, or interactive system. Designers investigate users’ needs, behaviors, and environments rather than relying only on assumptions. ISO 9241-210 provides a structured framework for designing interactive systems, while IDEO.org’s The Field Guide to Human-Centered Design emphasizes empathy, creativity, experimentation, and learning from people (IDEO.org, 2015; International Organization for Standardization [ISO], 2019). Together, these approaches show why understanding human perception and behavior is essential to creating usable digital experiences.

ISO 9241-210 and IDEO: Structure Meets Empathy

ISO 9241-210 describes six connected steps in human-centered design: planning the process, understanding the context of use, specifying user requirements, producing design solutions, evaluating designs against requirements, and determining whether further iteration is needed (ISO, 2019). These steps are not simply a one-way sequence. Evaluation may reveal a misunderstanding or missing requirement, requiring the team to return to an earlier stage.

IDEO.org (2015) organizes its approach around three phases: inspiration, ideation, and implementation. Inspiration involves learning about people and their experiences. Ideation involves interpreting insights, generating ideas, and exploring solutions. Implementation involves putting ideas into practice and learning from their results. The guide also highlights seven mindsets: empathy, optimism, iteration, creative confidence, making, embracing ambiguity, and learning from failure.

The main difference is their emphasis. ISO offers a formal structure for planning and evaluating design activities, while IDEO encourages the attitudes and experimentation that help designers understand people and develop ideas. Both are valuable. A team can follow a formal process without genuinely listening to users, while a creative team can develop appealing ideas without testing their effectiveness. Combining structured evaluation with empathy helps designers create solutions that are both practical and responsive to real needs.

Empathy and Understanding the Context of Use

Empathy means making a serious effort to understand what people are trying to accomplish and what obstacles they encounter. IDEO.org (2015) encourages designers to learn directly from people instead of depending entirely on assumptions. This matters because designers may use a system differently from beginners or people with different abilities, responsibilities, and access to technology.

Consider a student struggling to submit an assignment in Canvas. A designer might assume that the upload button needs to be larger. However, observation and interviews could reveal that the actual problem is uncertainty about acceptable file types or confusion about whether the submission succeeded. Without understanding the context of use, the team might improve the wrong feature.

The ISO process helps turn these observations into practical requirements. For example, a team might specify that students should be able to identify an assignment deadline and confirm submission status without searching through unrelated information. It could create a prototype, ask students to complete a realistic task, observe their difficulties, and revise the design based on the results.

Designers must also consider different stakeholders. Students may want a simple interface, instructors may need detailed grading controls, and administrators may prioritize security and consistency. Human-centered design does not mean accepting every request. It means understanding different needs, identifying conflicts, and making informed decisions based on evidence.

Perception and Attention: Why Visual Organization Matters

People do not give equal attention to every item on a screen. They notice certain elements first, recognize patterns, and use context to decide what matters. Johnson’s (2020) discussion of human perception explains why interfaces should account for how people see structure, interpret visual patterns, and process color.

Visual hierarchy communicates importance through size, position, contrast, spacing, and grouping. On a Canvas assignment page, the title, deadline, instructions, and submission control should be easy to distinguish from secondary information. If every link and heading competes for attention, students must work harder to identify the next step. Grouping related information makes the task easier to follow.

Color can reinforce visual hierarchy, but it should not communicate meaning alone. A red indicator might suggest a warning, but users may interpret colors differently or have difficulty distinguishing them. Text labels, icons, and sufficient contrast should support the same message. Designers must also consider mobile devices, keyboard navigation, and assistive technologies.

Perception is influenced by expectations as well as appearance. An icon that seems obvious to a designer may confuse a first-time user. Testing labels and visual patterns with real users can reveal these problems. Ultimately, a usable interface does not merely display information; it helps people understand what is happening and what to do next.

Four UX Laws in Everyday Interface Design

UX laws describe common patterns in human behavior and provide useful starting points for design decisions. They are not absolute rules, so designers should test their assumptions with users (Yablonski, 2020).

Jakob’s Law: Respect Familiar Expectations

Jakob’s Law suggests that users expect digital products to work similarly to products they already know. Familiar navigation and recognizable labels allow people to apply existing knowledge to a new interface (Yablonski, 2020). In Canvas, predictable locations for course materials, assignments, grades, and discussions can help students find information without learning an unfamiliar system.

However, familiarity should not prevent improvement. A common pattern may still be confusing or inaccessible. Designers should preserve useful conventions while improving features that create unnecessary barriers.

Fitts’s Law: Make Important Actions Easy to Select

Fitts’s Law explains that larger, closer targets are generally easier to select than smaller, more distant ones (Yablonski, 2020). This matters for buttons and touch controls. A submission button should be easy to locate and large enough to select accurately, especially on a phone. Small controls placed close together may cause accidental selections. Designers should prioritize important actions and provide sufficient space between controls.

Hick’s Law: Organize Choices Around User Goals

Hick’s Law describes how the complexity of available choices can affect decision time (Yablonski, 2020). A page filled with competing options may overwhelm users. On an assignment page, clearly presenting the instructions, file-upload control, and submission action can make the process easier to understand. However, removing too many options may hide useful features. The goal is to organize choices around user priorities rather than eliminate choice altogether.

Miller’s Law: Group Information Meaningfully

Miller’s Law is associated with research on the limits of immediate memory. It is often summarized as approximately seven, plus or minus two, meaningful chunks of information. This should not be treated as a strict rule limiting every menu to seven items. A more useful lesson is that related information can be easier to process when grouped meaningfully (Miller, 1956; Yablonski, 2020).

For example, a course page containing dozens of files in one long list may be difficult to navigate. Grouping files into weekly modules or topics gives students context and makes related materials easier to recognize.

Together, these laws address expectations, target selection, decision-making, and information organization. Applying them can improve usability, but their effectiveness still depends on the needs and experiences of actual users.

Iteration and Learning from Failure

IDEO.org (2015) encourages designers to embrace ambiguity, make ideas tangible, and learn from failure. These mindsets challenge the assumption that the first design should be the final solution. Instead, early designs become opportunities to test assumptions and discover problems.

For example, a team might make the Canvas submission button more prominent but discover that students still cannot tell whether their work was submitted. Testing may reveal the need for a clearer confirmation message or a visible record of the uploaded file. Each revision should respond to evidence about the user experience.

Prototypes make this process practical because teams can test sketches or simplified screens before developing a complete system. ISO’s evaluation step similarly encourages teams to compare design solutions against requirements and determine whether changes are necessary (ISO, 2019). Iteration does not mean changing everything whenever someone offers an opinion. It means evaluating feedback carefully and revising designs when evidence shows that they are not meeting user needs.

Conclusion: Put People Before Assumptions

Human-centered design recognizes that technology is experienced by people with different goals, abilities, expectations, and circumstances. ISO 9241-210 provides six connected steps for understanding context, defining requirements, developing solutions, evaluating designs, and improving them. IDEO’s Field Guide complements this process with empathy, creativity, experimentation, and learning from failure.

The four UX laws offer practical ways to think about familiarity, target selection, decision-making, and information processing. Visual hierarchy and meaningful grouping further help people find and understand important information. However, no principle guarantees a successful experience without testing.

My main takeaway is that good design is not simply about making a screen attractive or adding features. It is about understanding what people need to do and removing unnecessary barriers. Designers must observe, listen, test, and revise rather than assume that their own experience represents everyone. When people remain at the center of the process, digital systems have a better chance of becoming understandable, useful, and inclusive.
