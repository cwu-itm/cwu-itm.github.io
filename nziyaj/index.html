Module 1.1 Blog Post: Systems Thinking

Systems Thinking

What is systems thinking?

Systems thinking means looking at the whole situation and understanding how its parts affect each other. Instead of focusing on one problem, we ask how that problem connects to other parts of the system. We also consider the people who use the system and what they need. In my own words, systems thinking is a way to understand connections before making decisions.

A digital system includes more than software. It can include computers, networks, databases, users, and rules. These parts must work together to reach a goal. For example, imagine a hospital website that allows patients to schedule appointments. The website depends on patient accounts, scheduling information, staff, and confirmation messages. If one part fails, patients may have trouble completing the task.

I believe the main value of systems thinking is that it helps us avoid solving one problem while creating another. A website may become faster, but speed alone does not make it useful. Patients must also understand the instructions and receive correct information. To me, a successful system helps people complete their task safely and clearly.

How is it different from linear thinking?

Linear thinking follows a simple path: find a problem, identify the cause, and fix it. This approach can work well for small problems with a clear cause. For example, if a program adds numbers incorrectly because of a coding mistake, correcting that mistake may solve the problem. Breaking work into steps can also make tasks easier to manage.

However, complex systems have several connected causes. Imagine that the website connection loads slowly. A quick response might be to buy a stronger server. Systems thinking would first ask more questions. Is the database slow? Are too many users visiting the website at once? Is another service causing delays? Are patients sending the same request several times? The answers affect which solution will help.

This difference matters because improving one part does not always improve the whole system. A stronger server might send requests to a database that is already struggling. The website could remain slow even after the hospital spends more money to fix it. I will first study the connections and find where the work is getting delayed.

I still think linear thinking is very useful. Engineers need clear steps, detailed tests, and careful checks of individual parts. Systems thinking adds a wider view; it helps us connect a smaller task to the entire goal. I believe both approaches can support each other when we know when to use them.

Why feedback and time matter

Systems thinking also looks at feedback, which happens when the results of an action affect what happens next. Suppose a website is slow. Patients may click the appointment button again because they think the first click did not work. Those extra requests may make the website slower. The delay leads to actions that increase the original problem.

This pattern is called a reinforcing feedback loop. One possible response is to show a clear message that the request is being processed and prevent accidental duplicate requests. That change addresses both the website’s behavior and patient reactions. My point is that some technical problems depend partly on how people respond to the technology.

Some effects also take time to appear. A hospital could introduce a difficult account recovery process to protect patient information. At first, the change might seem to be successful. Later, more patients could call support because they cannot access their account. I would check the results over several weeks. A decision should be judged by its wider effects rather than only its first result.

What I learned from the course reading

In System Engineering Demystified, Holt (2021) explains that complexity, unclear communication, and lack of understanding can contribute to system failure. These problems can also affect each other. His discussion helped me see why building a system requires more than writing good code. Teams also need clear information and a shared understanding of the goal.

For example, two teams could understand the word “confirmed” differently. The software team might say an appointment is confirmed when the request is saved. The scheduling team might say it is confirmed only after a worker approves it. Both teams could follow their own rules correctly, but the patient might receive the wrong message. I would make sure everyone agrees on important terms before development begins.

Holt (2021) also explains that different stakeholders can understand the same need differently. Stakeholders are people or groups with an interest in the system. For a hospital website, patients may want easy navigation, staff may want fewer errors, and security workers may want strong protection. These needs should be discussed together so that the design supports the whole task.

People are part of the system. The National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA, 2016) describes systems as combinations of parts that include hardware, software, people, process, and procedure. This definition is useful for digital design. Even when a project is focused on an application, the people using and supporting it help determine whether it works well.

In the hospital example, I would ask patients, scheduling staff, support workers, developers, and security workers to review the design. Each group sees different problems. Patients may notice confusing instructions, staff may notice missing information, and developers may notice technical limits. Bringing these views together can reveal problems that one team would miss.

I would also consider users who have limited internet access or difficulty using a smartphone. If the website is designed only for confident users, other patients may need more telephone support. The hospital might reduce work in one area while increasing it elsewhere. My view is that a system’s success should include the experience of people who find it difficult to use.

What is the model-based approach?

A model is a simplified picture or description of a system. It helps people understand how the system is organized and how it should work. A model can show parts, connections, actions, and rules. It does not need to include every detail; it should include only the information needed to answer an important design question.

Singam and Carter (n.d.) explain that model-based systems engineering uses formal models to support work throughout a system life cycle. Instead of leaving important information in separate documents, teams can connect it through models. I see this as a practical way to help different people work from the same understanding.

For the hospital website, one model could show the main parts: patient accounts, appointment scheduling, the database, and the messages. Another could show what happens when a patient requests an appointment. The team could follow the process from logging in to receiving a confirmation. These models would help people check the design before building the complete system.

The model should also show problems that might occur. What happens if the requested appointment is already taken? What happens if the database is unavailable? What happens if the confirmation message cannot be sent? Asking these questions early gives the team time to plan useful responses and explain clearly to patients.

Why do models help with complex digital systems?

Complex digital systems contain many connections. A change to one part may affect several others. For example, changing the login service could affect passwords, account recovery, access rules, and support instructions. A connected model can help the team identify these effects before making the change.

Models can also connect requirements to design choices and tests. A requirement is something the system must do. Suppose the website must save an appointment before showing a success message. I would connect this rule to the booking process and to a test that checks it. If the process changes later, the team can see and test the need for review.

Another benefit is comparing options. If the scheduling service stops working, the website could reject requests or hold them for later. Holding requests may help patients, but they need to understand that an appointment is still pending. A model showing requested and confirmed appointments can help the team discuss the advantages and problems of each option.

NASA (2016) distinguishes verification from validation. Verification checks whether a system meets its requirements. Validation checks whether a system meets users’ needs. I would check that the website follows the booking rules, then ask patients to try it. Both checks matter because following a written rule does not guarantee that the task is easy to complete.

Models also have limits

A model is useful, but it is not a real system. It depends on assumptions and skips details. A model might assume that every patient has a phone or that an outside service is always available. If those assumptions are wrong, the design may fail in real use. I would first write down important assumptions and test them.

Models also need updates. If the website changes and the model stays the same, people could make decisions using old information. I would review the model whenever an important part or rule changes. I will also keep them simple enough to understand. Adding details should help answer a question rather than making the model hard to use.

Finally, models cannot decide what is fair. A cheaper design might be harder for some patients to use. A strict security rule might increase support work. These choices require discussion with the people affected. I believe systems thinking should help teams make those effects visible and explain why they choose one option over another.

My personal reflection

Systems thinking encourages me to ask better questions before choosing a solution. I will start by identifying the goal, the people involved, and the important connections. Then I would use models to explain the design, review possible problems, and compare options. After the system is built, I will check whether the results match what the team expected.

The most important lesson for me is that working parts do not automatically create a successful system. Their connections and effects on people also matter. Systems thinking helps us understand these relationships. A model-based approach gives us a practical way to discuss and test them. Together, they support digital systems that are easier to understand, maintain, and use.
