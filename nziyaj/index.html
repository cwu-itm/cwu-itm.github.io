Module 1.1 Blog Post: Systems Thinking

Systems Thinking

What is systems thinking?

Systems thinking means looking at the whole situation and understanding how its parts affect each other. Instead of focusing on one problem, we ask how that problem connects to other parts of the system. We also consider the people who use the system and what they need. In my own words, systems thinking is a way to understand connections before making decisions.

A digital system includes more than software. It can include computers, networks, databases, users, and rules. These parts must work together to reach a goal. For example, imagine a hospital website that allows patients to schedule appointments. The website depends on patient accounts, scheduling information, staff, and confirmation messages. If one part fails, patients may have trouble completing the task.

I believe the main value of systems thinking is that it helps us avoid solving one problem while creating another. A website may become faster, but speed alone does not make it useful. Patients must also understand the instructions and receive correct information. To me, a successful system helps people complete their task safely and clearly.

How is it different from linear thinking?

Linear thinking follows a simple path: find a problem, identify the cause, and fix it. This approach can work well for small problems with a clear cause. For example, if a program adds numbers incorrectly because of a coding mistake, correcting that mistake may solve the problem. Breaking work into steps can also make tasks easier to manage.

However, complex systems have several connected causes. Imagine that the website connection loads slowly. A quick response might be to buy a stronger server. Systems thinking would first ask more questions. Is the database slow? Are too many users visiting the website at once? Is another service causing delays? Are patients sending the same request several times? The answers affect which solution will help.

This difference matters because improving one part does not always improve the whole system. A stronger server might send requests to a database that is already struggling. The website could remain slow even after the hospital spends more money to fix it. I will first study the connections and find where the work is getting delayed.

I still think linear thinking is very useful. Engineers need clear steps, detailed tests, and careful checks of individual parts. Systems thinking adds a wider view; it helps us connect a smaller task to the entire goal. I believe both approaches can support each other when we know when to use them.

Why feedback and time matter

Systems thinking also looks at feedback, which happens when the results of an action affect what happens next. Suppose a website is slow. Patients may click the appointment button again because they think the first click did not work. Those extra requests may make the website slower. The delay leads to actions that increase the original problem.

This pattern is called a reinforcing feedback loop. One possible response is to show a clear message that the request is being processed and prevent accidental duplicate requests. That change addresses both the website’s behavior and patient reactions. My point is that some technical problems depend partly on how people respond to the technology.

Some effects also take time to appear. A hospital could introduce a difficult account recovery process to protect patient information. At first, the change might seem to be successful. Later, more patients could call support because they cannot access their account. I would check the results over several weeks. A decision should be judged by its wider effects rather than only its first result.

What I learned from the course reading

In System Engineering Demystified, Holt (2021) explains that complexity, unclear communication, and lack of understanding can contribute to system failure. These problems can also affect each other. His discussion helped me see why building a system requires more than writing good code. Teams also need clear information and a shared understanding of the goal.

For example, two teams could understand the word “confirmed” differently. The software team might say an appointment is confirmed when the request is saved. The scheduling team might say it is confirmed only after a worker approves it. Both teams could follow their own rules correctly, but the patient might receive the wrong message. I would make sure everyone agrees on important terms before development begins.

Holt (2021) also explains that different stakeholders can understand the same need differently. Stakeholders are people or groups with an interest in the system. For a hospital website, patients may want easy navigation, staff may want fewer errors, and security workers may want strong protection. These needs should be discussed together so that the design supports the whole task.

People are part of the system. The National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA, 2016) describes systems as combinations of parts that include hardware, software, people, process, and procedure. This definition is useful for digital design. Even when a project is focused on an application, the people using and supporting it help determine whether it works well.

In the hospital example, I would ask patients, scheduling staff, support workers, developers, and security workers to review the design. Each group sees different problems. Patients may notice confusing instructions, staff may notice missing information, and developers may notice technical limits. Bringing these views together can reveal problems that one team would miss.

I would also consider users who have limited internet access or difficulty using a smartphone. If the website is designed only for confident users, other patients may need more telephone support. The hospital might reduce work in one area while increasing it elsewhere. My view is that a system’s success should include the experience of people who find it difficult to use.

What is the model-based approach?

A model is a simplified picture or description of a system. It helps people understand how the system is organized and how it should work. A model can show parts, connections, actions, and rules. It does not need to include every detail; it should include only the information needed to answer an important design question.

Singam and Carter (n.d.) explain that model-based systems engineering uses formal models to support work throughout a system life cycle. Instead of leaving important information in separate documents, teams can connect it through models. I see this as a practical way to help different people work from the same understanding.

For the hospital website, one model could show the main parts: patient accounts, appointment scheduling, the database, and the messages. Another could show what happens when a patient requests an appointment. The team could follow the process from logging in to receiving a confirmation. These models would help people check the design before building the complete system.

The model should also show problems that might occur. What happens if the requested appointment is already taken? What happens if the database is unavailable? What happens if the confirmation message cannot be sent? Asking these questions early gives the team time to plan useful responses and explain clearly to patients.

Why do models help with complex digital systems?

Complex digital systems contain many connections. A change to one part may affect several others. For example, changing the login service could affect passwords, account recovery, access rules, and support instructions. A connected model can help the team identify these effects before making the change.

Models can also connect requirements to design choices and tests. A requirement is something the system must do. Suppose the website must save an appointment before showing a success message. I would connect this rule to the booking process and to a test that checks it. If the process changes later, the team can see and test the need for review.

Another benefit is comparing options. If the scheduling service stops working, the website could reject requests or hold them for later. Holding requests may help patients, but they need to understand that an appointment is still pending. A model showing requested and confirmed appointments can help the team discuss the advantages and problems of each option.

NASA (2016) distinguishes verification from validation. Verification checks whether a system meets its requirements. Validation checks whether a system meets users’ needs. I would check that the website follows the booking rules, then ask patients to try it. Both checks matter because following a written rule does not guarantee that the task is easy to complete.

Models also have limits

A model is useful, but it is not a real system. It depends on assumptions and skips details. A model might assume that every patient has a phone or that an outside service is always available. If those assumptions are wrong, the design may fail in real use. I would first write down important assumptions and test them.

Models also need updates. If the website changes and the model stays the same, people could make decisions using old information. I would review the model whenever an important part or rule changes. I will also keep them simple enough to understand. Adding details should help answer a question rather than making the model hard to use.

Finally, models cannot decide what is fair. A cheaper design might be harder for some patients to use. A strict security rule might increase support work. These choices require discussion with the people affected. I believe systems thinking should help teams make those effects visible and explain why they choose one option over another.

My personal reflection

Systems thinking encourages me to ask better questions before choosing a solution. I will start by identifying the goal, the people involved, and the important connections. Then I would use models to explain the design, review possible problems, and compare options. After the system is built, I will check whether the results match what the team expected.

The most important lesson for me is that working parts do not automatically create a successful system. Their connections and effects on people also matter. Systems thinking helps us understand these relationships. A model-based approach gives us a practical way to discuss and test them. Together, they support digital systems that are easier to understand, maintain, and use.

Module 1.2 Blog Post: Thinking in Life Cycles

Systems Engineering Life Cycle Models

Introduction

Systems is about more than just building a system. Engineers also need to think about how the system will be planed, designed, built, tested, used, and maintained, improved, and, eventually replaced.

This full process is called the system life cycle. A system can be many things . It can be a computer network, a website, a mobile application, a hospital system, a cloud platfom,or a cybersecurity system.

These systems usually have many parts that need to work together.Because of this,engineers need a clear process to manage the work.

A life cycle model helps engineers organize the development of a system. It gives the team a way to plan the work and understand what should happen at this stage. In System Engineering Demystified, Holt explains different life cycle models that engineers can use.

The Three important models are the linear model, iterative model,and incremental model(Holt, 2021).

Each model has advantages and disadvantages. Some projects may work better with a linear model, while others may need an iterative or incremental approach. Understanding these models is important because it helps engineers choose the best way to develop and improve system.

What is the Sytem Development Life Cycle

The system developement life cycle is the process a system goes through from the beigining to the endnof its useful life. The process usually start when a problem or a need is identified. For example , a company may need a new website, a hospital may need a new patient system,or a business may need stronger cybersecurity

After the need is identified,the organization decides what the system should do. This is called gathering requirements. Engineers build the system. The system is then tested to make sure it works correctly. Once testing is completed, the system can be released to users. However, the work does not stop there. The system still needs maintenance,updates,security patches,and improvements.

Once testing is completed, the system can be released to users. However, the work does not stop there. The system still needs maintenance, updates, security patches,and improvements

NASA explains that systems engineering supports a system through its develpement, operation,and retirement(NASA,2017). This shows that engineers should think about the complete life of a system can be easier to manage later.

The Linear Life Cycle Model

The linear life cycle model follows a clear order. One stage is completed before the next stage begins. For example, the process may look like this: Requirements --> Design --> Development --> Testing -->Deployment --> Maintenance

The team usually completes the requirements before moving to design. Then the design is completed before develpment begins. Holt describes the linear model as one way to organize system development in a structured order (Holt,2021).

One advantage of the linear model is that it is easy to understand. Everyone knows what stage the project is in and what comes next. It can also make planning easier. Managers can create schedules and budgets because the stages are clearly separated.

The linear model works well when the requirements are already clear and not expected to change very much. For example, if a company knows exactly what kind of system he wants and requirement are unlikely to change, a linear approach can be usseful.

However, the linear model also has disavantages. One problem is that changes can be difficult. If the project is already in the testing stage and the team discovers a major design problem, they may need to go back and change earlier work. This can take extra time and money.

Another problem is that users may not see the system until later in the project. They may then realize that the system does not work exactly the way they expected.This is difficult with digital systems because technology changes quickly. For example, a new cybersecurity threat may appear during development.A company may also changes its business needs. A strict linear model may make it harder to respond to these changes. I think the linear model works best when the requirements are simple, clear, and stable.

The Iterative Life Cycle Model

The iterative life cycle model works differently. Instead of trying to build the perfect syste on the first attempt , the team develops a version of the system, tests it, learns from it, and then improves it. This process can happen several times. For example: Version1-->Test-->Improve-->Version 2--> Test-->Improve. The systems Engineering Body of knowledge explains that iterative development allows teams to repeat parts of the development process as they learn more about the system (SEBok, 2024).

One important advantages of the iterative model is flexibility. If the team discover a problem, they can fix it in the next version. Users can also test the system and provide feedback. For exmaple, imagine a company is developing a cybersecurity dashboard. The First version may show network alerts. After users test it, they may say that the dashboard has too many alerts and is difficult to understand. The development tesm can then improve the dashboard in the next version.

This is useful because the team learn from real users instead of making all decisions at the beginning. Another advantage is that problem can be found earlier. If the team tests the system often, they may discover design or security problem before the final productis released.

However, the iterative model also has disavantages. One problem is that the project may continue for a long time if the team keepsmaking changes.The cost can also increase because developers may repeat the same work several times.

Another problem is that users may continue requesting new features. This can make the project much larger than originally planned. The team needs good project management so that the system does not continue changing forever. In my opinion, the iterative model is very useful for digital systems because software and technology change often

The Incremental Life Cycle Model

The Incremental model builds a system in small parts. Instead of building the entire system at one time, the team creates one useful part first. Then more feature are added later. For example, imagine a hospital is creating a patient portal. The first version may allow patient to see their appointment, the second version may add test results, the third version may addd secure messages, the fourth version may add prescripton requests. Each new part is called increment.

Holt explains incremental development as another life cycle approach that allows a system to grow in smaller parts over time (Holt,2021). One major advantage of the incremental model is that users can start using the system earlier.

The organization does not need to wait until every feature is finished. This can be helpful for businesses becuase they can start receiving value from the system while other features are still being developed.Another advantage is that smaller parts can be easier to test. If the team finds a problem, they can focus on one part instead of the whole system. It can also reduce risk because the team is not trying to build everyting at once.

However, the incremental model also has disavantages. The team must make sure that all the different parts work together. If the original design is poor, adding new features can become difficult.

Another problem is that users may expect features that are not ready yet. For example , a patient may be able to appointment but may not yet be able to send meessages to the hospital. The team must clearly explain what each version can and cannot do.

I think the incremental model is a good choice for large digital systems because it allows teams to build the system gradually.

Comparing the Three Models

The linear, iteretive, and incremental models, all have different ways of managing development. The linear model follows a fixed order. It works well when requirements are clear and do not change much. The iterative model focuses on repeating work and improving the system. It works well when the team expects to learn and make changes. The incremental model focuses on adding features in smaller parts.It works well when an organization wants to release useful parts of the system before the entire project is finished.

One model is not always better than the others. The best choice depends on the project. Some companies may also combine different models. For example, a team may use the incremental model to release different features, while also using iteration to improve each feature. This is why systems engineers need to understand different approaches.

Why Life Cycle Thinking is Important?

Thinking about the life cycle of a system is important because systems do not stay the same forever. Technology, Users changes, Security Threats changes, Business needs change. A system that works today may need changes the next year. A company can not install a security system once and never update it.New vulnerabilities and attacks are discovered all the time. System need patches, monitoring, testing and improvements

Life cycle thinking is also important for sustainability. Enginners should think about how long a system will last and what resources it will use. For example, a poorly designed system may require too much computing power or may be difficult to upgrade. A better deesign may allow the orgnization to keep the system longer and reduce waste.

Engineers should also think about maintenance. If a system is very difficult to upgrade, future employees may have trouble managing it. This can create technical debt. Technical debt happens when quick decisions create bigger problems later.

Thinking about the life cycle can help engineers amake better long term decisions.

Conclusion

System engineering life cycle models help teams organize the development of complex systems. The linear model follows a clear sequence and works well when requirements are stable. The iterative model allows teams to test, learn, and improve the system many times. The incremental model model allows teams to build and release the system in smaller parts. Each model has advantages and disadvantages

System engineers need to understand the need of the prject before choosing the model. Life cycle thinking is important because a system continues to change after it is built.

Digital systems need maintenance, security, updates,new features, and improvements. Thinking about the full life cycle can help engineers build systems that are more useful, secure, sustainable , and easier to manage over time

Module 2.1 Blog Post: Human First

Introduction

A good webiste should help people find information and complete task easily. Human-centered design focus on understanding users and creating products that meet their needs. This includes thinking aboutwhat people notice, how they make decisions, and what challenges they face.

In this post, I compare the approaches of ISO and IDEO, and explain how perception, attention, empathy, and four UX laws can guide better design choices

Designing for people: Making webiste Easier to Use

imagine trying to make a medical appointment online while your child is crying and you are getting ready for work. You open the hospital website, but you can not find the appointment button.

There are many links, unfamiliar words, and colorful announcements. The website works, but it does not make your task easy. This example shows why designers need to understand people.

Human-centered design, also called HCD, means designing products around people need, abilities, and situations. Designers need to understand who will use a product, what those people want to do, and what problems they might face.

ISO (2019) explain That human-centered design applies throughout the life of an interactive system. This means thinking about users from the beiginning and continuing to improve the [roduct over time.

To me, designing for people means respecting their time and making their next step clear. A website should help people complete a task without making them feel confused or embarrased.

A beautiful page is useful when people can also understand it. In this post, I use a medical appointment webiste as hypothetical example to explain how design choices can help or hurt users

Comparing ISO and IDEO

ISO 9241-210 provides requirement and recommendations for human-centered design. It helps teams organze their work and consider users throughout a project (ISO,2019). I see this approach as a way to give a team clear direction.

Instead of building a website based only on personal opinons, the team follow a process that include understanding people and checking wether the design meets their needs, IDEO.org's Field Guide gives designers practical ways to learn about poeple and develop ideas. It include method for understanding users exploring possible solutions, and making prototypes.

A prototype is an early version of a product used to try an idea before building the complet product (IDEA.org, 2015).

I think these approaches work well together. ISO helps organize the process, while IDEO helps designers explore problems and possible solution. For the appointment website, I would use ISO to guide the project the IDEO's methods to learn from patients

For example, I could interview patients, create a simple drawing of the website, and ask them to try it. However, neither approach should become an activity that a team completes only to say it followed the rules. Interviews and planning documents have value when they change design decisions.

If patients explain that a page is confusing, the team needs to use that feedback to improve it

Emphathy:Understanding the User's Situation

Emphathy means trying to understand another person's feelings and experiences. In design, it also means listening instead of assuming that everyone thinks like the design, it also means listening instead of assuming that everyone thinks like the designer. IDEA(2015) encourages designers to underrstand the people they are trying to help.

A dveloper may find a website easy because they already know how it works. A new user does not have that knowledge. Some users may also have limited computer experience, difficulty reading English, or disability. Other may be tired, worried, or using a small phone screen.

For the appointment webiste, I would ask peoeple what they what they expect to happen when they choose an appointment type. I woud watch where they stop or ask questions. If several peoeple choose the wrong options, i would review the lables and instructions. Their mistakes could show that the design need improvement

Perception: Helping People Understand What They see

Perception is how we make sense of information we see. On a website peopel use headings, spacing, colors, and placement to understand the page. These choices can help people find information or make the page harder to follow. For example, a form becomes confusing when a label appear closer to the wrong box.

A user might enter their phone number in the wrong place because the layout suggessts that the labels belong there. I would keep each label close to its correct field and leave clear space between section. Color can help communicate a message, but it should not carry the whole meaning. If a form has an error, i would include a clear message such as " Enter your phone."

Simply making a box red may not explain the problem. The message should help the person understand what to fix

Attention and Visual Hierachy

Attention is what we focus on. A website may contain a lot of infomration., but users are ususlly looking for something specific. Visual hierarchy means arranging contemt so that its importance is clear, Heading, groups, and the order of information can guide people through a page.

On the appointment website, I would place common tasks near the begining of the page. Theses could include making an appointment, sens a message. Less urgent information could appear farther down. This arragement would support the tasks patients came to complete

I would also avoid making every item look equally important. If everything demands attention, people must spend more efort decding where to look. A clear page should help visitors anaswer three questions: Where am i? What can i do? What happen next?

Jakob's law: Use Familiar Patterns

Jakob's law explains that people bring expectations from other website they have used. Chan (2024) explains that these experiences shape mental models, or people's beliefs about how a system works. For example people expect a link called "Appointement" to take them to appointment information. A creative label such as " Your Health Journey" may sound interesting, but it may not clearly explain where the link goes. I would choose familiar words that help users predict what willl happen.

Fitts's Law: Make Buttons Easy to Select

Fitts's law describes how a target's size and distance affect the time neede to reach it. Large targets and shorter movements generally make pointing easier (Budiu, 2022). This matters when peopele click with a mouse or tap a phone.On the appointment website, I would make important buttons large enough to select comfortably. I would also place the Continue button near the information users have just entered. Small buttons placed far away could require extra effort.

Hick's law: Make Choices Easier to Understand

Hick's law conect descision time with the number and complexity choices Yablonski (n.d.-a) recommends making complex tasks more manageable while avoiding too much simplication. A patient may struggle with a long list of medical departments. Instaed, the website could begining with a simple questiom:"what kind of help do you need?". It could then show clear categories and an option for people who are unsure. Still, fewer choices do not always create a better experience. An experienced patient may already know the department they need. I would consider offering both a guided process and a direct way to find a departement. The goal is to make decision understandable while keeping useful option available

Miller's Law: Reduce what People Must Remember

Miller's law is often connected to remembering about seven items, plus or minus two. Howver, Yablonski (n.d.-b) warns against treating this number as a strict design rule. The useful lesson is to group information meaningfully and reduce unnecessary memory demands. For the appointmentform, I would group information into patient details, appointment selection, and review. Before confirmation, I would show the provider, date, time, and location together. Users could check their choices without remembering information from earlier pages.

Testing and mproving the Design

These ideas would guide my first design, but testing would show whether it actually helps people. I would ask users to makee an appointment, change a selection, and find the confirmation. I would observe mistakes, pauses, and requests for help. I would also ask what they believe happened. Completing a task does not always mean the experience was clear. Someone may finish booking but still feel unsure whether the appointment was saved. That uncertainty would show a need for the better feedback. Iwould also check whether peoplencan use the website with a keyword and understand its instructions. My biggest lesson is that designers to question their own assumptions. The person using the appointment website wants to arrange care and continue with their day. Understanding perception, attention, empathy, and UX laws can help make that possible. Listening to users and improving the design are what turn those ideas into a useful experience.

Conclusion

Desining for people measn understaning their needs and making tasks easier to complete. ISO provides a structured approach, while IDEO offers practical methods for learning from users and exploring ideas.

Clear visual hierachy helps people notice important information, and the four ux laws helps designers create familiar controls, manageable choices, and clear steps.

However,these principles cannot replace feedback from real users. To me, good design means listening, testing, and improving a product until people can use it with confidence.
