Systems Thinking and Model-Based Systems Engineering

What is System Thinking ?

When thinking about system thinking, it is best defined as an approach that focuses on understanding how various components of a system interact and the relationship between them. To best understand how system thinking works, its best to look at the characteristics and elements of a system. The book, Systems Engineering Demystified states that a system is made up of a set of system elements, which are comprised of two types. Those two types are the system interest and the enabling system. System of interest refers to a system that is under development, and enabling system refers to any system that has an interest in or interacts with a system of interest. It is important to note that a system element can be much more complex and can be broken down into lower-level system elements and develop a hierarchy for a specific system.

System Elements

Looking at how these system elements interact, the connection between system elements also allows for interfaces to be both identified and defined. Moving onto Attributes of a system, they essentially describe the system and the system elements. They are typically represented as nouns that can also take on numbers of different values, types, or units. They can also be more complex like timestamps or data structure. The boundaries simply serve to identify the scope of the system. The boundaries may be physical, conceptual, or stakeholder boundaries. Physical boundaries can come in the form of an enclosure on the system that separates it from the outside world. Conceptual boundaries are non-physical boundaries that cannot be observed, and its important to identify where that boundary may be. Stakeholder boundaries is the difference in view from different stakeholders, and that can mean that each stakeholder may see the boundaries in different areas. The key of overcoming a stakeholder boundary is to find common ground between stakeholders and work withing their point of views to match them and deliver a system every stakeholder can accept. The needs are the purpose of a system or of a system element. The needs can be in the form of requirements, features, and goals. A requirement need can come in the form of a statement of something that is desired for a system to do and are most commonly tied to the functionality of a system. A feature need related to a collection of functions that may be needed and dont have to be tied to a specific function. A goal need represents a need for the overall system and what it is designed and built to accomplish. Moving onto the constraints serve as limiting the realization of a system and serve to limit how a system is realized. They can be quality, implementation, environmental, and safety. Quality restraints are applicable to almost every system. These quality constraints relate to the use of best practices like standards and serve to identify all the standards and shape how the system is developed. Implementation constraints limit how a system can be built in limiting the materials used. Environmental constraints apply to where a system is deployed and is often dictated by the environment in which a system is deployed and can lead to certain constraints. Finally, safety constraints apply to almost all systems as they ensure a system can function in a safe way and prevent any errors tat may be detrimental to the success of a system. Overall, system thinking requires a holistic approach to best utilize all parts of a system and its elements..

System Thinking Vs. Linear Thinking

System thinking differs from a more linear traditional ways of thinking in that linear ways of thinking revolve around a step-by-step approach to problem solving, and a focus on individual parts. system thinking takes the system and its complexity all into account. The article, Systems Thinking vs. Linear Thinking: Understanding the Key Differences, showcases how linear thinking can be applied, stating that linear thinking “simplifies complex situations by breaking them down into smaller, more manageable tasks, focusing on cause-and-effect relationships”. (Critical Thinking secrets, 2026). On the other hand, the article also states that “Systems thinking emphasizes the interrelationships and patterns between system elements… offers a comprehensive way of understanding complex systems, as it focuses on the relationships between system components rather than individual components”. System thinking is so important to system engineering because of the complexity systems and their elements. The number of variables that are present in system engineering call for a more holistic approach, and that holistic approach requires that system engineers look at every element to build a system that is within a scope, functions properly, performs the functions it is called to do, and satisfies what stakeholders want from the system.

Using Model-Based System Engineering

When it comes to a model-based approach, and why its applicable in the design of complex digital systems, its best to understand why those systems are complex and how the model-based approach can be used in system engineering. As mentioned above, systems are made up of different elements, and they can grow to be more complex as the scope is identified and established. Complexity can come in two forms, those being essential and accidental complexity. Essential complexity is the natural level of complexity that is inherited in the system. In other words, as the scope grows, a natural level of complexity grows as well, and it cannot be lowered, but managed and controlled as long as its identified. Accidental complexity is not natural and is often applied by inefficiencies in things like staff, processes, and tools. The model-based approach or MBSE is an approach to systems engineering that “uses models, not just documents, to capture requirements, design, analysis, and verification of systems” (reforge learning, 2026). Instead of reading through pages, diagrams and simulation models are used to represent the system’s behavior and structure. MBSE is applicable to systems engineering because of the increasing level of complexity that systems have in the current day and age. Where technology is constantly advancing, systems-of-systems are more common, and by modeling a system digitally, engineers can get a better understanding of the interactions and behavior of a system. MBSE also helps in enhancing communication and collaboration via visual representation and can help get stakeholders on the same page as engineers. It can also improve quality as it can help in reducing risk of errors and inconsistencies, as well as improving efficiency and speed by allowing early detection and correction issues. MBSE is also scalable to projects of various sizes and complexities and can aid in the lifecycle of a product. Some of the tools that are used in the MBSE approach are systems modeling language (SysML), software tools, simulation and analysis tools, management tools and more depending on the project. SysML is a graphical modeling language that extends unified modeling language and provides a standard to presenting systems with various diagrams to aid in visualization. Software tools serve to offer environments for creating, editing, and managing SysML models. The simulation and analysis tools work to help in testing systems and analyzing system behaviors under certain conditions. Finally, there are the management tools, as they aid in keeping track of the project and making sure the requirements are all tracked and compatible with the system design (IBM, 2026). Overall, the use of MBSE is essential in todays day and age, simply because of the increasing level of complexity systems have. Having systems-of-systems is more prevalent than ever before and having the ability to create models to showcase how systems behave and interact with system elements makes developing them and understanding them much easier

Thinking In Life Cycles

What is Life Cycle Thinking

Moving on to life cycles, its better to acknowledge it as an evolution of one or more entities over time, and that there can be many types of entities. Each entity should be thought of as living and ever changing. In this case, a system is a type of entity, one that is ever evolving as it gets developed and deployed, that being the life cycle. Its also important to note that each life cycle has its stages, and they differ from life cycle models. Where life cycle models show how an entity evolves and describes the order of stage execution, a life cycle shows what the evolution is and identifies the stages that exist in that evolution. The stages used in both life cycles and life cycle models depend on the nature of the entity that the life cycle is describing, changing based on the type of entity that is considered. The book, “Systems Engineering Demystified” states that the system life cycle is the development of a system that is comprised of many stages, those being conception, development, production, support, and retirement. The conception stage revolves around identifying and defining the needs of a system, based on stakeholder analysis and the definition of verification and validation criteria. In other words, a clear picture has to be made, in accordance with what stakeholders may want and validate them to satisfy stakeholder needs. The development stage is all about identifying potential candidate solutions for problems that may be associated with stakeholder needs and finding possible solutions. Its important to note that this stage can also involve the creation of a prototype prior to the production stage. The production stage takes the solutions found and developed to create the actual system itself. It involves the previous stages criteria to ensure the system is built correctly and that the right system is built in accordance with stakeholder needs. These two actions are commonly seen as verification and validation of the system. Another aspect of life cycles is progression through a life cycle gate. The stage gate can result in many steps, those being progress onto the next stage, staying in the same stage, going back to a previous stage, or ultimately cancelling a life cycle which is something that is usually an extreme measure, but should be considered during the development of a life cycle. Process is also another concept of life cycles, defined as a set of activities that are executed for a specific set of outcomes. Its important to know that any number of processes may be done in any stage, meaning that a single process can be done in a single stage, multiple can be done in a single stage, the same process can be done in more than one stage, and the same set of processes can be executed in more than one life cycle.

The Types of Life Cycle Models

Moving onto linear, iterative, and incremental life cycle models, it’s best to note that the differences between the three lies on the emphasis of the order the stages are executed. Starting with the linear life cycle model, the book describes the model as the most simples linear sequence and showcases the waterfall model. The waterfall model is synonymous with the linear cycle model because of the way the stages are executed, leaving them to be executed in a specific order after the previous stage, often in order. In the model, lies the advantage of the linear model, as it’s simple and easy to understand, and offers a clear understanding due to the products it uses and the technologies being used for it. The disadvantage of the linear model lies in its simplicity, as it’s not suitable for large, complex projects and systems where needs may need to change or for long-term projects. The article, “What is a Waterfall model? Definition and guide” showcase more disadvantages of the model, stating the “Waterfall design isn't adaptive; when a flaw is found, the entire process often needs to start over” and that “The Waterfall model delays testing until the end of the development lifecycle”. Moving onto the iterative life cycle model, the book states that unlike the linear life cycle model that only does one pass through, the iterative life cycle model commits to several passes through the stages, naming them iterations. The principle behind the iterative life cycle model is that if the linear life cycle model is great for short, well-defined projects, then its possible to break down a more complex project or system into a series of shorter and simpler projects. When this is done, it is known as an iteration, and its simply a pass through the stages, starting from the concept stage to the end of the production stage into utilization. The advantages of the iterative life cycle model is that after every iteration, there is a more complete and improved version of the project compared to the last, and if the latest iteration ends up being a disaster, then its easy to return to the previous iteration and work from there. The disadvantage of this life cycle model is that its usually only used in software development rather than on systems development, which has led to an emphasis on getting projects out on time rather than completeness or functionality. The other disadvantage is that the nature of the model means that needs can be changed frequently by stakeholders, so its important to develop a viable needs process in place. Finally, the incremental life cycle model is very similar to the iterative model, differences between the two is that there isn’t a single pass through the stages, but multiple, typically done so the final system is deployed in multiple releases. The incremental model also starts from the concept stage where it covers all the needs. This leads to a product that takes different subsets of the needs and produces partial solutions that don’t comprise the whole system and can be deployed. It’s important to note that both iterative and incremental life cycle models are known as the evolutionary models. This model essentially is built around the idea that system betas are deployed, as the system is worked on overtime to deliver the full system product. The only downside of this model is that this model isn’t as versatile as the others, as this model can only be done on certain systems.

Design, Sustainability, and Evolution

Thinking about life cycles and thinking about design, sustainability, evolution of digital systems, what stands out is the nature of life cycle thinking, and its considerations of the stages of a system’s life cycle. The stages serve to provide a foundation for thinking about the design and sustainability of system. Stages like the conception and development serve as a catalyst to thinking about the needs of stakeholders as well as identifying potential issues and working to solve for them. This means that sustainability is inherently tied to the design of a system. This is aided by the model that is used to develop a system, as the iterative model is perfect for considering the design, sustainability, and the evolution of a system. The iterative model allows for developers to backtrack into earlier stages with the knowledge to better past stages of the system, and allows developers to constantly improve upon a system.

Memory and Mental Models in User Interactions

To better understand how mental models and memory influence user interactions, it’s best to understand what mental models are. Yablonski in “laws of UX” states that mental models are what we think we know about a system and how it works. Whether it’s a digital system like a website or a physical system like a checkout line, we make assumptions and form mental models of how a system works. This phenomenon also means that we may apply those same mental models to new situations that have similar systems (Pg.9, 2024). Designers can use these mental models by designing a digital environment that carries over mental models and therefore influences users to use a system based on previous user experience, whether the user may have used the same type of system in a product ecosystem or other outside product systems. Now moving onto memory; we can see how certain mental models are inherently tied to user experience and memory and how that can influence the user interaction. In the book, “Designing with the Mind in Mind” by Jeff Johnson, we get an insight into what memory is and how they are created. Johnson states that, “Memory formation consists of changes in the neurons involved in a neural activity pattern that make the pattern easier to reactivate in the future” and that “Activating a memory consists of reactivating the same pattern of neural activity that occurred when the memory was formed”. This insight allows us to understand why memory and the mental models we create (aka Patterns) when using systems influence user interactions (pg. 105, 2026). The system should be easy to remember and should help users remember essential information from one moment to the next. A great example of this is navigation through a system, in which whether people are using software, a device, phone menu, or a website, it often involves navigating to desired information or a goal. The key is designing a system that is easy to navigate, especially for those who are non-technical through deep hierarchical systems. These aspects are based on the limited capacity of human working memory. This is where mental models appear, as creating a system that utilizes a common mental model like a shopping website UI or navigation hierarchy allows for users to have more positive user interactions with a system.

Usability Via Consistency and Feedback

When looking at interface consistency and system feedback, its best to consider what feedback is and how that applies to interface consistency. Tesler’s Law provides a great foundation to what interface consistency is and how it may look for users when designing a system for usability. The book “Laws of UX” explores the concept of interface consistency using Tesler’s law, stating that “interface consistency would benefit not only users but also developers because standards could be encapsulated in shared software libraries”. Interface consistency means creating an interface that has uniformity in visual elements, layouts, and interaction across different parts of a system. By prioritizing consistent interfaces, it can aid users in developing a mental model of the system, aid in the creation of memory in using the system and can help users differentiate between the modes of a system. The article “The Role of Feedback in UX Design: Enhancing User Experiences” shares how providing adequate feedback to users gives them a sense of goal completion, satisfaction, and encourages engagement. The article also provides different types of feedback, sharing different types of feedback like visual, auditory, tactile, and contextual. Visual feedback are things like color changes, animations, or any other visual cues to acknowledge user actions. Auditory feedback is things like sounds of alerts or notification sounds that inform the user of events or actions. Tactile feedback is things like vibrations. Contextual feedback is real-time feedback that is directly related to the user’s current action such as autofill suggestions. Now when thinking about usability, the combination of a consistent interface and valuable feedback bring many benefits such as reducing learning time of a system, lowering the cognitive load for people to use a system, and it helps build trust and confidence in a system as a consistent system feels more reliable to users.

The Specific Example

Thinking about poor design, what comes to mind when thinking about unnecessary cognitive load is the sheer amount of text and visual elements that can sometimes aid the user in achieving their goals but can also play a very big part in effecting memory, traversal of a system, and overall hinder a user’s ability to find what they are looking for. First looking at text, Johnson states that needless text is bad for software and websites, as many users will find ways to ignore excessive text in their efforts to reach their goals. This also means that you can lose users based on the amount of text present, and that having too much text can even alienate good readers and turns an interactive system into an intimidating piece of work (Johnson, 2026). I find that a great example of this is the MYCWU Student Dashboard, as it’s filled with text as soon as you enter the portal, and can be hard to distinguish between different widgets. It’s even more apparent in the navigation tabs, where a click of a tab drops down to show other links that redirect the user to different parts of the website. The tab “Advising” showcases 13 links, the most out of all the tabs available. This can be confusing for new users as these tabs present a lot of information, can hinder how users navigate through the website, and overall can Aliante new users. Difficulty for new users can escalate, especially when they must do things like enroll in new courses, find campus jobs, create course schedules, apply for university housing and pay off outstanding balances. In my personal experience, I have felt this way, especially navigating through different portals like enrolling or applying for dorms. A specific example is enrolling into class, as it requires one to click on the “Enrollment” drop down menu, click “Add Classes”, select the term, and search for classes. This gets more confusing due to having to reselect the term. Select your major, and sift through the sea of available classes.
