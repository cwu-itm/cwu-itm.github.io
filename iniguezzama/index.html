Module 1.1 Blog Post

What is System Engineering?

This module's required reading includes an introduction to systems engineering. Holt, J., and Weilkiens, T. (2023) explain the history of systems engineering, describing it as a tale as old as human history, from the inventions of the pyramids to WW2 and the present day. The authors explain that there are many different types of systems; these include natural systems, which are open systems outside human control (e.g., weather, environment). Designed physical systems are what most people conceptualize as systems, namely real-world physical artifacts such as cars, smartphones, and bridges. There are designed abstract systems, which are non-physical concepts used to explain ideas or concepts (e.g., models, equations). Another system includes human activity systems, which are human-based systems and encompass groups of people who have a shared goal and work together (e.g., political systems and social groups). Transcendental systems are another group that includes concepts beyond current human understanding. The authors also further explain characteristics of a system. These include the system of interest, which refers to the specific system being developed, as well as the enabling system, which is any interacting system that supports or affects the system of interest. Interfaces and interactions focus on the physical and information flows between elements. The authors define stakeholders as a characteristic of a system that involves anyone who has an interest in a system. These people sit outside the system boundary and represent roles. The stakeholder is not defined by their name, such as Robert, but is defined by roles; for example, a car is a system, and the stakeholders of a car may include the owner for someone who owns the car, the driver for someone who drives the car, and the maintainer for someone who performs maintenance on the car. Just about anything can have an interest in this system; it doesn't have to be a person. For example, a company that owns a car can be a stakeholder, or laws that regulate a car can also be stakeholders. Many people can be stakeholders, and one entity can hold multiple stakeholder roles relating to the system. Attributes are also characteristics of a system, as the name implies; they are high-level properties describing the system. This can include the system's dimensions, weight, software, and data format. Boundaries are another characteristic of a system and define system scope, such as what is inside and outside of the system and where interfaces exist. Some boundaries include physical boundaries, such as an enclosure that surrounds the system; this can include a house or a wall. A conceptual boundary is a type of boundary that is not physical but can be imagined, such as the boundary between morning and afternoon. Another form of boundary is that of a stakeholder boundary, which is the boundary that the specific stakeholder has in relation to the system, which may vary from stakeholder to stakeholder. Another characteristic of a system is its needs, which define the system's purpose across hierarchical levels, such as its goal or top-level overall objective. Features include a high-level collection of capabilities, and requirements are specific, detailed statements of functional behavior. Constraints are another characteristic of a system and are defined as limitations on how a system can be realized. The authors define the three evils of system engineering. These are the root causes of why the system fails. The first is complexity, which is unmanaged relationships between system elements or external systems. There is essential complexity, which is the inherent complexity of the problem. And accidental complexity, which is unnatural complexity introduced by poor tools, processes, or human inefficiencies. The authors give the example of a car and how the main purpose of a car has gone unchanged since its introduction, mainly of providing a form of safe transportation; however, complexity has grown with developing cars in the modern day to meet regulations such as safety regulations as well as emission regulations and be able to provide amenities and features that can compete with other cars in the market. Another root cause that leads to system failure is communication failure, in which communication between teams breaks down. Lack of understanding or ignoring different stakeholder viewpoints is another root cause of system failure. I find this cause of system failure to be particularly understandable along with communication and complexity, as I feel as though they are intertwined with one another. Complexity can lead to a lack of communication, which then leads to ignoring stakeholders' viewpoints. An example can be a real-world relationship between two people. Let's say a couple begins to move in together, pay the bills balanced along with career goals, and have responsibilities such as raising children; their relationship becomes more complex. This complexity then results in broken communication, which prevents them from handling their problems, and this lack of communication then leads to ignoring the other's viewpoints and the ultimate relationship failure.

The System Design Process

An article by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA, 2025) defines systems thinking as a methodical, multidisciplinary approach for the design, realization, technical management, operations, and retirement of a system. The interesting aspect about this is that many NASA projects are the go-to examples of systems engineering. When we think about Apollo missions, they contain many of the aspects that Holt and Weilkiens describe, which include dealing with constraints, stakeholders, attributes, and boundaries. However, in this article by NASA, I particularly like their emphasis on the big-picture perspective. My earliest introductions to systems thinking involved looking at the big picture and identifying the system as a whole instead of the individual parts. NASA defines four system design processes. The first is defining baseline stakeholder expectations. This makes sense when engineering a system. When we think of the earlier example of creating a car, it's important to be able to understand stakeholder expectations. This can be that of the buyer, which is understanding how much they are willing to pay for the car. The driver, which involves what kind of driving experience it is able to provide. And the passenger wants to know what kind of comfort or safety the car is able to provide for them. These are just a few examples of expectations that stakeholders may have, and they are essential for engineering a system. Technical requirements are another step in the system design process. When you think of the Apollo mission of getting to the moon, this would have obviously included rockets and much more advanced technology in order to achieve something that had never been done before. Another step in the system design process is decomposing the requirements into logical and behavioral models, which is a sort of designed abstract subsystem. Another step in the system design process is the design solution definition, which involves converting the technical requirements into a design solution that will satisfy the baseline stakeholder expectations, the final step forward of transforming a conceptual model to a plan of action.

Life Cycle of a System

An article by the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA, 2009) provides a model for the life cycle of a system, which they call the sequential VEE model. The model is in the shape of a V and begins with project planning, then concept of operations, then requirements, then design and specifications, software/hardware implementation, then integration and verification, deployment and acceptance, and then validation. Next is operations and maintenance, and finally retirement and replacement. This model does make me question what Holt and Weilkiens defined as the three root causes of system failures. Some systems can fail over time just due to their lifecycle naturally ending. For example, a car as a system can ultimately reach its end in a number of different ways, whether it be totaled or reach the end of its operational capabilities. While this may involve complexity, it does not involve any understanding or communication failure. But at the same time, one may argue that this is not a system failure but the system behaving exactly as it was designed to, which is the car operating a certain number of years/miles and then reaching the end of its operational capabilities. While that may be true for designed physical systems, it is much harder to argue this for other types of systems, such as natural systems, which are beyond our control.

What is System Failure

This also makes me ask the question: what truly is system failure? In my earlier example of a relationship between a couple, I defined system failure as the ending of the relationship, but that is from my perspective. I believe that system failure is ultimately a semi-subjective thing. While you can see a system break down, it may be hard to see that when certain systems break down, they are really shifting in what the system is. For example, a couple that's in a relationship, such as marriage, was once friends or engaged. A system can therefore also break down and become part of something greater. A car that is broken down gets refurbished into scrap metal and becomes part of another car or another greater system. Overall, just like how stakeholders can have multiple roles within a system, systems can also be part of even greater systems, and I have the belief that what we define as system failure has some subjective attributes. When it comes to designing digital environments such as a website, it is hard to argue that a website being shut down or going offline is not some sort of system failure, as the website is not being recycled into something greater, but at the same time there may be an unrealized benefit to website shutting down.

Module 1.2 Blog Post

Systematically exploring

This module explores chapters 3- 5 of the book Systems Engineering Demystified. Holt, J., and Weilkiens, T. (2023). In these chapters, we explore the topics of systems and interfaces, life cycles, and systems engineering processes. In order to define a system, we need to understand certain aspects of what makes up systems. Such as system hierarchy, which is how many levels of subsystems can exist. We must also understand interactions between system elements (e.g., system to system, subsystem to subsystem) and interactions between levels (between system and subsystem).

System Hierarchy

All systems have system hierarchies; however, the authors present us with four levels of hierarchy. There is the system, which is at the top of the hierarchy. Then, below the system, there are a number of subsystems, which a system is made up of. And below the subsystem is an assembly, which a number of assemblies make up, and below this is a component, and a number of components make up an assembly. A real-world example is a car, which is an entire system of its own, and below this we have a subsystem such as the engine, which helps make up the car. And below this we have the assembly, which is the fuel injection assembly, which is made up of multiple parts that help make up the engine, and below this we have an individual fuel injector, which is a component that helps make up the fuel injector assembly. Each position lower in the hierarchy helps contribute towards creating the system. The engine is just one subsystem, and there are multiple subsystems that make up the system. After all, it takes more than just an engine to make a car. And how we define a system also depends on the stakeholder; for example, for an engineer, an engine can just as well be its own system. The authors utilize Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE) and an ontology to define legal interactions between system elements. The authors explain that elements of a system can interact horizontally between elements on the same hierarchy if the ontology permits, but cannot interact vertically, as it adds lots of complexity. For example, a component can and does directly interact with the system; however, it becomes very difficult to map out these relationships, especially when considering how many components it takes to create a system.

System Interface and Lifecycle

The authors also explain that interfaces are how systems connect to interact. And that there are two types of interfaces, namely flow interfaces and service interfaces. Flow interfaces are when something flows or transfers between elements. Service interfaces are when one system element provides a service or capability to another system element. For example, a flow element would be something like fuel or electrical energy for a car. And a service element would be something like a Wi-Fi router providing network access to a laptop. The authors also discuss concepts such as system lifecycles. They explain that system life cycles exist within stages. The stages that the authors present are the conception stage, development stage, production stage, utilization stage, support stage, and retirement stage. The names self-describe what each stage entails. What I find particularly interesting is that in a previous blog, I had wondered exactly about a system life cycle. And what exactly entails system failure? I questioned if a system ending meant system failure, but as presented by the life cycle stages, it does not necessarily mean failure. Retirement is often a planned part of a system's life cycle. Now I view system failure as dependent on the stakeholder, as if a system becomes something that goes against a stakeholder's interest then it can be seen as a failure to the stakeholder. For example, if a stakeholder who is an automaker has an interest in the cars they sell and there turns out to be a defect in their car that makes it spontaneously combust, then that would be system failure for that stakeholder, as the defect of that car would mean a loss of business for that stakeholder, something that they do not want. The authors also describe system processes as a set of activities used to achieve a particular result. Process is described as essential for allowing for organized Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE). There are also a number of desirable properties associated with processes, such as processes must be repeatable, processes must be measurable, and processes must be demonstrable. These properties help ensure consistency and organization within systems. If a process can’t be repeated, then the system can lead to many inconsistencies, which can lead to undesired outcomes for stakeholders. If I borrow the earlier example of a stakeholder that is an automaker, if they can’t repeat the process of making cars, then they have significant problems for their business, such as defects and high costs. The process that needs to be measurable is also very important for being repeatable. An example is a cooking recipe; if each time you add a different amount of an ingredient, then the final result is always somewhat different. The process needs to be demonstrable so that stakeholders are able to gain confidence that the model actually drives successful system development.

  • Development
  • Production
  • Utilization
  • Support
  • Retirement

Software System Life cycle

An article by GeeksforGeeks (2023). Provides insight into the system lifecycle for software systems. What's interesting about this is that the development lifecycle for software includes different stages than what Holt and Weilkiens described. This article presents 7 lifecycle stages, namely analysis, design, development, testing, implementation, and maintenance. The main difference between these models is the scope, as Holt's and Weilkiens' life cycle models are broader. The GeeksforGeeks (2023) model focuses on the software development lifecycle. However, despite these many stages in both life cycle models, they encompass the same activities; for example, the planning and analysis stage is much like the conception and development stage that Holt and Weilkiens describe. The development and testing stage is also similar to Holt and Weilkiens’s development and production stage and the GeeksforGeeks (2023) model’s implementation and maintenance stage, and Holt's and Weilkiens’s utilization and support stages. They are similar in a high-level overview of what these stages encompass but differ in that the GeeksforGeeks (2023) model is specific to software development. One great difference between the two models is that GeeksforGeeks (2023) does not have a retirement stage. What is interesting about this is that many software developers do nothing about the end of life for the system they create. In theory, this makes sense, as unlike physical projects, software projects can exist in perpetuity. After all, a car can only function as long as the physical components that make up the car are able to function. For a car, this can be up to 200,000 miles or 20 years. Software is not dependent on the same physical obstacles. However, this isn’t exactly true after all; most people nowadays are not using software from the 80s, and many software become outdated and tossed aside even sooner than physical systems. Software that is unsupported for new technology becomes inaccessible, and software that does not receive security patches becomes a security risk. We might also assume that software systems do not need a retirement stage, as their abrupt end is often inconsequential compared to that of physical systems; however, that is not true. How often is it that we hear of essential services and infrastructure utilizing old, outdated software, for example, in banking, healthcare, and manufacturing, as this old software that is utilized becomes essential to their operations but is very difficult to change despite the software no longer being supported? This old, outdated software presents significant security and operational risks because many of them never had a retirement plan.

System Cost

System engineering at its core is about utilizing resources effectively; one such resource is that of money. An article by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (2025) explains the systems engineer’s dilemma. For systems engineers, considerations often need to be made in order to remain within budget. These considerations are called cost considerations. Oftentimes the goal when engineering a system is to balance costs and effectiveness. Oftentimes a design decision may improve effectiveness without increasing cost or reduce cost without sacrificing effectiveness. However, often that is not the case. NASA explains that every cost-effectiveness solution comes with trade-offs. For example, to decrease cost at constant risk, performance must be lowered; to reduce risk at constant cost, performance must be reduced; and to reduce cost at constant performance, more risks must be taken. To lower risk while maintaining performance, larger expenses must be tolerated. What also makes this interesting is considering the economic side of system design. The fact that projects often have allocated budgets and these budgets are often exceeded due to unforeseen costs supports the importance of measurability within the system design process that Holt and Weilkiens present. Measurability helps prevent variances. Variances in a system, such as a car in the manufacturing process, can result in an imprecise allocation of resources, which can result in increased costs. The problem that systems and projects face is that these unforeseen costs cause changes to the final system or project. These changes are trade-offs the system engineers must make, for example, trading cost for quality or quality for cost. A real-life example would be that of a car manufacturer with the goal of creating a car with a 0-60 MPH time of under 3 seconds while maintaining many amenities such as leather seats for comfort, a touch screen, and self-driving capabilities. The goals of performance and comfort are contradictory. These amenities utilize money that could be used for performance, and vice versa. Not to mention that these amenities may also sacrifice performance. A car manufacturer may be able to have both with an increase in cost or have one at the sacrifice of the other while saving costs.
