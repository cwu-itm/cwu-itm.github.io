Understanding Systems Thinking

In a world that is becoming more interconnected digitally, the ways we look at technology, conceptualize, design, and manage it needs to evolve over time. Traditional problem-solving frameworks that often use linear cause-and-effect reasoning have helped solve problems in engineering for a very long time, but as software design/architecture, hardware infrastructure, and business/social environments change over time to adapt to the expanding capabilities and evolution of technology, linear cause-and-effect problem solving by itself will not be enough to maintain future digital infrastructure. In this blog, I will talk about the core concepts and understanding of systems thinking, and how it compares and contrasts to linear thinking.

Defining Systems Thinking

Fundamentally, systems thinking is cognitive framework and a very general problem-solving philosophy. Instead of viewing individual components of an issue in isolation, it views those components as interdependent parts of a larger whole made up of all these individual components. As a cognitive framework, systems thinking actively thinks about the correlation between two components, focusing on relationships, pattern recognition, and behaviors that emerge when several components interact. From my point of view, systems thinking can add on to basic cause-and-effect reasoning quite strongly. While basic cause-and-effect reasoning can provide the what (the effect) and the why (the cause), systems thinking goes deeper and looks at the “how.” Instead of looking at the two small components that cause a result, systems thinking is like taking a step back and looking at the bigger picture, and trying to understand why those two small components interacted the way they did in the first place. In Systems Engineering Demystified, Jon Holt states that a system is considered fundamentally more than just a sum of parts working together. It is defined by the interactions of those parts and the context behind the way those parts interact with each other and how the system operates (Holt, 2021).

Systems Thinking vs. Traditional Linear Thinking

To fully appreciate the depth of systems thinking, we should contrast it with traditional, minimalizing, linear thinking. Linear thinking assumes that problems can be broken down into smaller, manageable pieces, solved independently, and reassembled back into a whole again to create something predictable. While this works very well on problems with a clear solution, it does not work long term on a more complex situation often found in a dynamic digital system. Linear thinking relies greatly on analysis, or the breaking down of information. It can be better at finding the solution to a smaller problem. System thinking relies on synthesis, which is combining parts to understand their role both with each other and with the whole system they are a part of. When you alter one component of a system, it often can cause a ripple effect with other parts of that same system, indirectly affecting a part that no one would have expected to be affected otherwise. The entirety of how problem solving is viewed in systems thinking is completely different. In linear cause-and-effect thinking, the common thought process is “how do I fix this specific problem right now?” In systems thinking, one of the more ideal and common thought processes is “how do I use this information to understand how similar future incidents might happen and to understand how this happened in the first place?” Some examples are linear thinking managing database performance and UI responsiveness, while systems thinking examines how these programs, for example, interact with each other and external stakeholders, to make sure that changing the functionality or optimization of one of these programs does not affect the other one negatively.

Direct Causality vs. Feedback Loops

Linear thinking assumes that cause and effect are closely related in both time and space. If a metric in a system drops, a linear thinking system seeks to find the immediate single cause in the local area where that problem happened. Systems thinking recognizes that cause and effect in complex environments are often distant in time and space, indirectly affected due to being linked by the complex feedback networks listed below: - Reinforcing Loops (Positive Feedback): Processes that amplify change, leading to exponential growth or rapid systemic collapse (e.g., a cascading server outage caused by retry storms). - Balancing Loops (Negative Feedback): Processes that resist change and seek equilibrium (e.g., automated load balancers distributing traffic during unexpected usage spikes). Understanding these feedback mechanisms and their differences allows system architects to design stable digital structures to keep systems intact when they interact with themselves in complicated ways, rather than “applying a short-term band-aid” the way that a linear thinking system would, which would worsen system-wide issues over time (Senge, 2006).

Determinism vs. Emergence

Linear systems show deterministic behavior. If you know the inputs and the properties of each part, you can precisely predict the output. Complex digital systems, however, have a problem solving trait that Meadows calls emergence—properties or behaviors that belong to the system as a whole and cannot be found in any of the individual components operating in isolation (Meadows, 2008). For example, high availability of a product, cybersecurity, and user experience are not static features you can code into a single small service perfectly, they are emergent properties generated continuously by the integration of network protocols, database consistency models, UI design, and human operational compliance. Systems thinking specifically designs for positive emergent outcomes while mitigating negative emergent phenomena like security vulnerabilities or unexpected performance bottlenecks.

The Need For Model-Based Approaches in Evolving Complex Digital Systems

As modern digital systems grow in scale and complexity, document-centered engineering has come close to its limits in capabilities towards handling these new complex and evolving systems. Traditionally, systems engineering relied on static documents, spreadsheets, individual design diagrams, and text-to-code note documents. With the increased use of tools like multi-cloud communications, edge computing, Internet of Things networks, and AI-driven workflows (especially the last two), this document-centered method is becoming dated. This document-based approach frequently suffers from “version control issues, ambiguous language, data silos, and an inability to analyze system-wide impacts effectively” (INCOSE, 2021).

Structural and Dynamic Complexity

Digital systems are characterized by both structural complexity (which includes lines of code, API endpoints, and diverse hardware) as well as dynamic complexity (which includes live data streams, changing user demands live, and the constantly-evolving security threats). A model-based approach uses standardized visual modeling languages—such as Systems Modeling Language (SysML) or Unified Modeling Language (UML)—to represent structure, behavior, requirements, and parametric relationships in a machine-readable, human-understandable format (INCOSE, 2021). According to Holt (2021), a model-based approach enables systems engineers to create abstract representations of reality that focus on specific views or viewpoints without losing connection to the overarching architecture. By abstracting lower-level implementation details into high-level models, systems architects can evaluate architectural trade-offs, model system states, and simulate operational flows long before physical implementation or deployment begins.

Conclusion

Systems thinking is far more than just a methodology; it is a necessary mindset required to navigate the evolution of modern technology. By looking beyond isolated components to focus on relationships, feedback loops, and emergent properties, systems thinkers can design architectures that are robust, flexible, and aligned with real-world needs. As digital environments continue to grow in complexity, mastering systems thinking will remain an essential skill for IT professionals, as well as systems engineers.

Understanding Systems Life Cycle Models

Systems engineering is rarely about simply “building things.” It is actually a disciplined practice of guiding complicated and detailed systems through a structured life cycle from inception to retirement. According to the text Systems Engineering Demystified, various life cycle models provide distinct frameworks for thinking about how systems are developed, maintained, and evolved. Adopting a structured approach allows systems developers to handle the increased complexity and risk of a system efficiently. Understanding the core three models (linear, iterative, and incremental) and where and when to apply them, is essential for any systems engineer to create sustainable and adaptive digital systems (Holt, 2023).

Defining the Systems Development Life Cycle

At its core, a System Development Life Cycle is a conceptual framework that explains the stages involved in developing, operating, maintaining, and retiring a system. The International Council on Systems Engineering states that a life cycle provides a structured governance system that aligns stakeholder requirements, technical execution and planning, and risk management together.

Instead of just jumping straight into coding or hardware construction, life cycle thinking forces teams to break down development into manageable stages, including requirements and prerequisites analysis, architectural design, implementation, verification ad validation, operations, usability, and stability (INCOSE, 2015). INCOSE guidelines also emphasize the importance of multi-disciplinary stakeholder engagement, bridging the crucial gap between end-users, software engineers, and project managers. Bringing these perspectives together early prevents requirements from becoming isolated in static documentation, ensuring that a system's evolving capabilities accurately reflect both technical functionality and user needs throughout its life cycle (INCOSE, 2015).

The Three Core Life Cycle Models

Different projects require different philosophies of development and require different types of sentiment and preparation. In the sections following this one is a breakdown of each of the three core life cycle models, including characteristics, advantages, and drawbacks of each one.

The Linear Life Cycle Model

The linear model, also referred to as the Waterfall model, is characterized by strict and direct progression phases. One phase must be formally completed in a process before moving on to the next one, and eventually all of the phases lead to the finished system. One strong example of the linear model is the Nintendo video game Mario Kart Wii. Mario Kart Wii was released as a completely finished, final product on a physical disc for the Nintendo Wii console in the late 2000’s. The Mario Kart games already had a legacy for being a popular franchise, so Nintendo was able to take advantage of that by understanding what core mechanics were already appreciated and loved by users and implementing them into a new game.

Key Characteristics

The key characteristics of the linear model are defined by the strict and direct sequential phases it has. It follows a simple and direct path of requirements first, then design, implementation, verification, and finally maintenance. Heavy documentation of the strict path it follows, and fixed upfront planning are also key characteristics of the linear model.

Advantages

The linear model is highly predictable in terms of planning, budgeting, and scope when requirements are understood and stable. Clear milestones make it easier for project managers to keep track of progress on a project.

Drawbacks

The linear model is highly rigid, making sudden changes in the middle of the life cycle difficult and costly to accommodate. The final product and working system that users can see is not visible to users until later on in the life cycle of a system, which makes the risk of building the wrong thing greatly amplified if the initial planning and requirements for the system were flawed.

The Iterative Life Cycle Model

The iterative model focuses on repetition and refinement. Instead of building the entire system in a single flawless path, using a strict and direct order of actions, iteration relies on a team building a complete or partial version of a system, evaluating it against user feedback, and using that feedback to refine the system through successive cycles. One real-world example of this system working successfully is Subnautica, a video game that was released in an early access form before becoming a full product. Subnautica relied on user feedback and experiences to replace game assets, fix bugs, and complete the detailing of the map and the story of the game in a way that was tailored best to the audience of the game.

Key Characteristics

Repeated execution of development cycles, evolutionary refinement, and continuous integration of user feedback. The iterative model uses repeated evolution to help a system adapt and grow constantly.

Advantages

Early risk identification is one of the greatest advantages of the iterative model. Flaws in architecture or assumptions are caught proactively and corrected in early iterations rather than the final delivery, which promises users a better long-term experience with that system. Iterative is highly responsive to evolving stakeholder needs and changing the environment of the system to fit the needs of stakeholders accordingly.

Drawbacks

Scope creep is one of the largest and most likely drawbacks of this system if the feedback received is not strictly managed. Too much response to feedback or too much response to the wrong type of feedback can cause overdevelopment, increased system errors due to lack of management, and too much multitasking, ending in the system collapsing. The unpredictability of this system relying on user feedback long-term can affect how the final budgeting and resource usage looks for the final product of the completely finished system.

The Incremental Life Cycle Model

While iterative systems focus on refining the product through continued loops, the incremental model focuses on expanding the functionality of a product by delivering pieces of the system in steps. One strong example of an incremental system in the real world is the development of the video game Minecraft. Minecraft has been around for over 17 years and started out as a simple game about breaking trees, mining in caves, and building simple structures. Over the course of the last 17 years, the game has slowly been receiving consistent updates that add new content such as new items, gameplay, multiplayer features, and fixing bugs. Minecraft has been continuously expanding ever since it came out, which is what the incremental model is all about.

Key Characteristics

Staged delivery of operational capabilities, modular architecture that gets modified more over time, and prioritization of core features first, and then applying secondary enhancements.

Advantages

Delivers value to stakeholders much earlier, as usable subsets of the system are incrementally released to users. It also reduces the initial investment risk and allows for operational feedback from earlier increments added to help decide what future increments to add and how to apply them (ISO/IEC/IEEE, 2015).

Drawbacks

Requires very robust modular architecture from the start. If the system is not properly prepared to be easily modified from the start, adding new increments to expand the system can cause architectural fragility.

Reflection: Why Life Cycle Thinking Matters for Digital Systems

In contemporary digital engineering, like large scale software platforms and AI pipelines, thinking about the development of a product purely in terms of the initial launch, and nothing else before or after that launch, is a huge mistake long-term for the system of that product. Systems engineering teaches us that delivery is only one small part of the system’s entire life cycle.

A fundamental limitation this presents with the linear model is its vulnerability to the exponential growth of rework costs, a principle well-documented in software and systems engineering economics. In a strict linear pipeline, finding and fixing a requirements or design flaw during the initial analysis phase is relatively inexpensive. However, because subsequent phases are locked and completed chronologically, discovering that same flaw later—such as during the verification, testing, or post-launch operations phase—requires backtracking through finished architecture, rewriting core codebases, and discarding pre-produced assets.

This dynamic creates the steep Cost of Change curve inherent to the linear “Waterfall” system, where fixing an error late in the life cycle can cost orders of magnitude more than catching it early. In contrast, other systems like iterative or incremental are much more change-friendly and deliberately flatten this curve. By introducing recurring feedback loops and early prototyping, iterative models catch architectural misalignment when it is cheap to alter, preventing the catastrophic financial buildup that could occur otherwise.

Managing Technical Debt and Sustainability

Even though digital systems do not go through physical wear, they can break down in a social, communal, or mindset-driven way. They can still degrade through software entropy and shifting dependencies. Embracing a life cycle mindset requires that a digital architect designs a system for the sake of maintainability, observability, and decommissioning right from the requirements phase at the beginning of the life cycle (INCOSE, 2015).

Navigating unpredictability in Systems Evolution

Real world requirements for a system are very rarely static. Stakeholders often do not know what they truly need until they interact with a working iteration or set of increments for a product. By moving away from the linear model and leaning more towards iterative and incremental models, organizations more easily face the reality that we cannot predict all future requirements upfront for a system, and that we need to always be prepared to adapt and make changes. The linear model can only provide so much longevity when its reliability is limited to predictable and pre-existing franchises or series.

Conclusion

Systems engineering provides the understanding needed to govern complex projects successfully. By understanding the linear, iterative, and incremental models outlined by Holt (2023) and organizations like INCOSE and ISO, engineers can select the right strategy for the right problem. Ultimately, framing development through a comprehensive life cycle lens transforms digital systems from fragile codebases into resilient, sustainable, and evolutionary ecosystems.
