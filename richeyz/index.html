Part 1: Overview of Systems Thinking, Systems Engineering, and MBSE

This week, we began our first module of the quarter about abstracting systems. We picked up learning about systems thinking, systems engineering, and a type of systems engineering called MBSE – model-based systems engineering.

Before diving into systems engineering, I want to first explore the idea of systems thinking. Systems thinking can be roughly defined as a method of thinking that considers all parts of a system within context of the system as a whole (Lutkevich, 2025). This is different compared to a more traditional way of thinking that breaks a system down into individual parts and analyzes them separately. Systems thinking can be applied to just about anything, making it an incredibly useful and versatile mindset to adopt.

When it comes to engineering, this term itself is rather broad. The way I’ve chosen to think about it relative to this course, is the development of digital environments. Rather than thinking about a physical system, which tends to come to mind first, we’re thinking about virtual ones and how we can design them in a way that accomplishes all of our goals. Systems engineering takes this idea and thoroughly builds upon it. It creates a framework that can be used to engineer new digital environments or system components that are well designed, user-friendly, extremely functional, and nicely secure. As technology evolves, it’s becoming more important to put this extra level of care into digital spaces. People spend more time online now than ever, so we need to design our virtual spaces and applications with new goals in mind. Systems engineering helps us do this.

With that said, let’s take a deeper look into systems engineering. The first thing the textbook does is define a system. There are many different kinds of systems, these being:

  • Natural systems
  • Designed physical systems
  • Designed abstract systems
  • Human activity systems
  • Transcendental systems (Holt, 2021)

Sorting systems into these categories makes it easier to evaluate the characteristics of each system. A naturally occurring system for example, may have components that a human designed system may not.

The next thing to look at would be what characteristics a system can have. A system is made of elements, and interacting elements or attributes are what make up the system as a whole. Other factors we are encouraged to examine are the stakeholders for the system.

Who is invested and why? Whether a person or organization, stakeholders play a role in how the system can be built and operated. Then there are also boundaries that define the scope of the system. These can be physical, conceptual, or defined by stakeholders. Finally, we have constraints that in some way, limit the system. All of these concepts combined play a role in how a system functions and how we think about the system as part of the systems thinking process.

Systems engineering takes all of this and applies it to the development of systems in a way that encompasses much more than a traditional way of thinking could. It’s not linear, with all of the above mentioned concepts playing an equally important role and facilitating better decision making overall.

Another great benefit to systems engineering is the fact that it can be used at any and all stages of a system’s life cycle. Many systems of today’s world are constantly changing and evolving to suit changing technology and social norms. Systems engineering will take this into account and consider future possibilities for change rather than viewing a system as stagnant and unchanging once it can operate.

It’s pretty clear that systems engineering has some perks. As a whole, it’s a well developed idea and is utilized in many professional areas. This said, there are what the textbook describes as ‘evils’ that can hinder systems engineering. These are understanding, communication, and complexity (Holt, 2021). To deepen our understanding of systems engineering this week, we also learned about a specific type of systems engineering called MBSE or model-based systems engineering. This has the ability to mitigate these evils using a model. As the name implies, models are used to help visualize a system and all of the attributes the system needs in order to succeed.

This idea of model-based systems engineering is largely replacing the more traditional document-centric approach (Simon, n.d.). As it has evolved, systems engineering started with more text-based documentation and has shifted to visual models with time. Models do a better job of mapping out the intricacies of a system and showing how attributes work together. Since this is an important part of the idea of systems thinking, it makes sense that the model-based approach has seen more success.

Part 1: Life Cycle Models

This week, we went through a few more chapters of the text to get some deeper learning about systems, interfaces, life cycles, and systems engineering processes. The following blog will contain a summary of each chapter followed by some personal reflections on the content.

Chapter 3, the first chapter we read, is about systems and interfaces. The first thing we look at are systems. Systems contain multiple parts and the text explores this idea by breaking systems down into what is called a system hierarchy. The hierarchy of a system essentially contains the system, subsystems, assemblies, and components – with these being listed from biggest to largest. It provides a view or diagram of how this works using a car as an example. A car would be the system with smaller subsystems being things like the chassis or body. Within those subsystems, there are even smaller groups called assemblies and then finally the individual parts, the nuts and bolts that make everything up.

As the chapter goes on, there is a repeated emphasis on the ontology. This is a word I had honestly never really heard before. It can be roughly defined as the naming, categorization, and defining of relationships between parts. It shows both elements and relationships as part of the ontology of systems as a whole. I think this is a good frame that we can use to view our entire learning throughout this course as learning about a system. The ontology itself is somewhat of a system – albeit a system of categorization and models and naming schemes.

That aside, Chapter 3 also talks about other concepts like interfaces and ports. It expands the model, adding each of these. Ports could be of different types like flow type and service type ports. Flow describes physical or other flows, using the model to display directions and types. Service types, as the name implies, describe services.

The next chapter talks about life cycles. A life cycle model shows the stages of a system’s life. Any system is going to have multiple stages of life – albeit slightly different from a living organism. First there is planning, creating concepts, development, and implementation (U.S. Department of Transportation, 2017). Something else worth noting is retiring systems. I think this is easily overlooked as part of the life cycle, but it’s very important because if you retire a system then you’ll likely need a replacement and transitioning to that replacement could be a complicated process on its own.

There are three different types of models that I learned about this week. The first type of model is the iterative model. It shows multiple ‘iterations’ of the life cycle stages, with each pass building on the last in order to fix problems and adapt to new situations. The second model is called the incremental model. This one focuses more on specific parts of the system and getting each one to work until eventually the system is complete. It functions well for longer projects. I also read online about a third one called the sequential model – which is similar to the incremental method, but follows a more strict order, “sequential steps from initiation to completion” (U.S. Department of Transportation, 2017).

The final chapter for this week returns back to systems engineering and how these new ideas about systems and life cycles relates to the Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE) approach.

Here we learn about processes, they are defined and their properties are listed. Processes should be repeatable, measurable, and demonstratable. While it’s not exactly desirable, processes CAN be complex, difficult to understand, unrealistic, irrelevant, or out of date (Holt, 2021). Like many of the concepts before, processes can also be broken down into multiple different types and concepts. Like systems, processes can be modelled and mapped. The ontology contains things like outcomes, activities, tasks, etc.

This chapter also introduces us to standards. Standards are important because they can demonstrate quality to stakeholders. Compliance with standards keeps things consistent across the board. An example of this is ISO 15288 – software and systems life cycle processes. This is the “most widely used Systems Engineering standard in the world” (Holt, 2021) and as such is definitely work taking a look at. Part of this standard is the “Need Context View”. It shows why processes or parts are needed and therefore can help explain why a standard might be needed. Another view is the “Process Structure View” that uses different process structures, groups and actually suggests the use of a Life Cycle (Holt, 2021).

There are four different process group types.

  • Agreement Process Group: processes related to customer and supplier relationship or acquisition and supply.
  • Organizational Project-Enabling Process Group: processes that apply across an entire organization.
  • Technical Management Process Group: processes that are applied for technical activities on a project by project basis.
  • Technical Process Group: processes like modeling and design that are often associated with Systems Engineering Activities.

(Holt, 2021).

Overall, the chapter describes a bit more of the nitty gritty details that have to happen within processes during the development of a system.

Part 1: Designing for People

This week, our learnings focused on the idea of human-centered design for the user experience. We learned the basics and then delved deeper into specific standards before taking a look at the psychology and laws of user experience.

Human-Centered Design

The idea of human-centered design or HCD is something that has been growing more popular in the world of UX, or user experience. It’s not just a method, it’s a mindset that emphasizes placing “real-people at the center of problem-solving” (Vinney, 2024). According to the UX Design Institute, human-centered design has four main principles.

These are:

  1. 1. Be people-centered: focus on people who use the product and their context.
  2. 2. Find the right problem: focus on the fundamental problem that will solve the root cause of other problems.
  3. 3. Think of everything as a system: keep the big picture for the user in mind, even if you are only working on one part of a larger system.
  4. 4. Small and simple interventions: focus on iterative work and continuously prototyping instead of rushing into big solutions.

The exact process of HCD can vary, but many organizations like ISO and IDEO have popularized different steps and phases – discussed in the next section.

ISO 9241-210 vs. IDEO

The first excerpt we read about this week came from a book that described the ISO 9241-210 Standard. The Internation Organization for Standardization (ISO) put forth this particular standard with the title “Ergonomics of human-system interaction – Part 210: Human-centered design for interactive systems” in 1999. At its core, the main idea is that we want to develop products and systems with good usability for humans (Kauer-Franz & Michaela, 2024).

While this is a pretty simple idea on paper, what makes it more complex is that in order to make a system usable, you need a very solid understanding of the users. Human-centered design focuses on understanding humans and their behavior, the tasks they have, and the environments in which they work in order to develop a usable system. ISO 9241-210 describes the five-step full design process around these ideas as follows:

  1. Step 1: Plan the Human-Centered Design Process
  2. Step 2: Understand and Specify the Context of Use
  3. Step 3: Specify the User Requirements
  4. Step 4: Producing Design Solutions
  5. Step 5: Evaluating the Design

(Kauer-Franz & Michaela, 2024).

The second excerpt we read about this week was published by IDEO – the “pioneers of human-centered designed and design thinking” according to their website (2026). IDEO uses slightly different phases – these being:

Inspiration: Understanding people, observing them, getting ideas.

Ideation: Brainstorming, testing and refining solutions.

Implementation: Bringing your solution to life and maximizing impact.

When working through these phases and designing a system, IDEO emphasizes creating a balance between different traits. For people, the system should be desirable. This is the first and most important place to start, hence human-centered design. The solution must also be viable for a business, and feasible given the available technology.

While both ISO and IDEO have slightly differing perspectives, it’s clear that both were created with humans in mind and have a goal of designing a successful, easy to use solution or system.

How Perception and Visual Hierarchy Affect Usability

When we start talking about user experience, it’s important to consider how people operate. After millions of years of evolution, we have psychological tendencies to perceive things and observe in specific ways. These tendencies change how we view the world around us – keeping this in mind, we can use our tendencies to create a better overall user experience.

One example of this is the concept of perceptual priming. Our past perceptions can bias our current perceptions (Johnson, 2021). This is a concept we see a lot in advertising – seeing certain things can prime us to see similar things later on when they are intentionally presented to us. Another example of perception impacting us is habituation. Seeing the same thing over and over can dull our sensitivity to it, which is important to keep in mind for the user experience so that we can keep people engaged and not bored – reflexively repeating actions. A final example would be our emotional responses. A lot of human actions are driven by emotions. The psychology behind emotions is so intense after millions of years of evolution. This can be absolutely abused in marketing, or engagement farming. But it can also be used to greatly enhance UX by understanding how system components could make a person feel and how those feelings will change how they interact with the system.

Visual hierarchy is another concept that can effect the way people process information. In order to use this to our advantage, we can break information into sections and subsections so that it’s organized in a way that attracts our attention to the correct elements. Outside of the actual organization, you can accomplish this by changing things like font size, or positions relative to other content. The essential gist of creating a visual hierarchy is to allow people to “quickly separate what is relevant” as they are scanning (Johnson, 2021). Doing this ultimately creates a better user experience because people can find the information they are looking for – or they are exposed to the information that you want them to see.

The Four UX laws (Jakob’s, Fitts’s, Hick’s, and Miller’s)

Something else that we learned about this week were various UX ‘laws’ as described by author Jon Yablonski in the book “Laws of UX”.

The first of these was Jakob’s law: “Users spend most of their time on other sites, and they prefer your site to work the same way as all other sites they already know” (Yablonski, 2024). The focus of this is to use and reference other models rather than trying to create something brand new. In the previous section, I talked about psychology and how we can use this to our advantage for UX. In the case of Jakob’s law, people like familiarity and the law uses this to establish a good way to improve designs and system structures.

The second law is Fitt’s law. “The time to acquire a target is a function of the distance to and the size of the target” (Yablonski, 2024). While originally coined in the 1950’s regarding physical targets or locations, in the world of UX this is a law largely focused on usability – where targets refer to touch targets that users can interact with a virtual space. In a world where mobile devices and touchscreens are so popular, this is a critical component of usability because without it, we would have no way to accurately pilot whatever device we are using.

Third, is Hick’s law. “The time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices available” (Yablonski, 2024). A key pattern that we’ve seen across the different units this course is the idea of breaking things down into smaller parts. Whether it’s creating a visual hierarchy that includes subsections or modelling components on a system diagram, Hick’s law demonstrates that doing these things actually makes decisions and critical thinking much easier for users. Complex things can be made simple by breaking them down, highlighting recommend options, and minimizing choices when there are simply too many.

Lastly, we have Miller’s law. “The average person can keep only 7 (+ or – 2) items in their working memory” (Yablonski, 2024). Similar to how Hick’s law emphasizes how we need to be careful not to overwhelm users with too many choices, Miller’s law shows us that short-term memory can only go so far. Smaller bits of information are better when it comes to helping people memorize and understand what they are seeing.

By learning about this, we can design things that simply work better for people. In addition to learning about some industry standards and visual perception concepts, we read over some of the ‘laws of UX’ that complimented them nicely by providing even more guidelines for how UX can be built.

Overall, everything fit together pretty nicely. The standards and UX laws and bits of human psychology piece together to create a solid picture of how building a good user experience is in a way, its own art form.

Part 1: Consistency and Cognition

This week, we continued learning about the various factors that can influence the way we interact with digital environments and how our tendencies can be used to improve user experience.

Memory and Mental Models

The first idea we begin exploring is brought to us from the Johnson textbook, Designing with the Mind in Mind. Johnson brings up two important ideas regarding our attention and our memory. Both of these are effectively limited. We can only focus on so many things at once and we can only remember so much. Despite this, our memory is one of the most powerful influencers of how we interact with the world around us. In order to learn how to use something, we must use our memory to retain knowledge and apply it in the future. We use both short-term and long-term memory when interacting with technology to help us understand how to use it and remember what our goal is as we use it. In a way, this relates to how last week, we learned that people tend to prefer familiarity – their memory using other UI helps them understand how to use anything that is similar.

Breaking this down further, we also have a working-memory. Its capacity and volatility “have many implications for the design of interactive computer systems (Johnson, 2021). Some examples of this are how voice user interfaces are more taxing on memory than graphical user interfaces. How systems that use ‘modes’ (like a car in drive, or a camera in video mode) can often result in errors as people forget what mode they are in. Good designs will require adaptations to account for our limited working memory. Like a light on my keyboard that reminds me that caps lock, is in fact on, as I try to type in my computer password. These are just a couple of examples, but it ultimately goes to show that our memory has a huge impact on our experience and it should absolutely be considered during the process of any system design.

Then, we have mental models. A mental model can be roughly defined as “what the user believes about the system (web, application, or other kind of product) at hand” (Chan, 2026) and help a user predict how to interact with the interface. Feedback and the reality of interacting with the interface will then confirm or contrast the user’s beliefs – creating a cycle.

You can find examples of mental models and their impact in spaces where online environments attempt to mirror real world ones. Consider an online website dedicated to shopping. In the real world, people usually find items they want to buy, physically pick them up, and bring them to a register to check out and purchase the item. This is a pattern many people recognize, allowing them to create a mental model of how the shopping experience should go. Most online spaces work to replicate this in a way with virtual elements. They store items you want to purchase go in a “shopping cart” area, which does the same thing online as it does in real life. You might hit a “checkout” button when you’re ready to finalize your purchases. By understanding mental models and our tendencies to lump experiences together, we can create better and far more intuitive user design.

Interface Consistency and System Feedback

The next factors we may want to consider when it comes to usability are consistency and system feedback. While they may seem intuitive, it’s actually a bit more difficult to keep both of these in place all the time. A common example of inconsistency could be a website with multiple search bars. I’ve seen this regularly on search sites for libraries, internet archives, and wikis. You may have a search bar on the main page for articles (or whatever the site is hosting), and an additional one in the corner for the content of the website itself. Another example might be buttons with inconsistent names. If you want to shop online and the ‘shopping cart’ area is named different things on different pages (“My Bag”, “Cart”, “Items”) the user might not know what to click. Inconsistency can easily create confusion and makes it trickier for users to navigate the interface.

Feedback is something else that good design tends to incorporate. The science of learning relies heavily on feedback. We are constantly being positively and negatively reinforced and punished and our behavior is shaped by this. In the case of UX, we are constantly getting feedback when we interact with various system elements. This could be in the form of ‘confirmation messages’ or pop-ups that let us know an action we took like a purchase or sign-in was successful. This reinforces us and tells us that what we did worked and can be repeated again in the future. Error messages do the same but in reverse – telling us what went wrong and maybe even providing information that will help us solve the issue. Both of these things absolutely improve usability and help guide users by teaching them how to use the system.

Poor Design Issues

Just as good UI can greatly improve our experiences, bad UI can just as easily ruin them and make life much more difficult than it needs to be. We can think of many examples of poor UI to help us know what ‘not to do’ – thinking of them as common mistakes. A good framework from which to view this is considering the cognitive load a system creates for the user. Cognitive load is “the total amount of mental effort being used in working memory” (Graf, 2026). This is relevant because our cognitive load is impacted every time we interact with an interface. If our cognitive load is exhausted, we are being overloaded with too much information or poor quality of information. This leads to negative things like decreased work quality, task completion rates, user satisfaction, and more.

I have an example from my own experience that’s rather fresh in my mind – it’s a class I’m currently taking for my Law and Justice minor. Due to an emergency with a professor, one of my classes was swapped from an in-person to a virtual format with a completely different, and very new professor. While I am sure he is a pleasant person and I have no ill-feelings toward him, it’s incredibly obvious that he has never used Canvas before and wasn’t super prepared to teach virtually. There are no modules, many assignments without titles and due dates, it’s difficult to access discussion boards and exams, some assignments are accidentally locked making them inaccessible until students let him know, and he was initially confused on how to even see our submitted assignments to grade them.

From the perspective of a student, there are multiple concepts of ‘good’ UI that he is breaking and consequently creating a higher cognitive load for us as we try to do our work. The first of these is visual clutter. All assignments are listed in the same place in semi-chronological order, with multiple postings for each discussion board. There is no other structure and now that we’re mid-way through the quarter, the only way to see what is upcoming and due is to scroll through a long list of past assignments. Another concept that would help is the idea of chunking information in a meaningful manner. Most professors I’ve taken courses with create ‘modules’ that chunk course content into digestible, logical sections that correlate with weeks in the quarter, units of a textbook, or course topics. Grouping content like this significantly reduces cognitive load because the human brain is better at processing grouped information over scattered information (Graf, 2026). This said, it’s important to keep in mind another important rule we learned this week – Tesler’s Law. This law essentially states that “for any system there is a certain amount of complexity that cannot be reduced” (Yablonski, 2024).
