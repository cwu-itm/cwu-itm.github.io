Behind the Screen: How Systems Thinking Helps Us Design Better Digital Services

Starting with an everyday problem

Picture a student trying to submit an assignment shortly before the deadline. The assignment is finished, but the student cannot get past the login screen. It is tempting to call this a website problem and leave it at that. However, the website may be working while another service is unavailable. The student's account, internet connection, verification method, and course permissions could all affect what happens next. For the person waiting at the screen, those separate pieces are part of the same experience.

This imagined situation is how I would explain the value of systems thinking. A digital environment is built from connections, and those connections deserve attention when something is designed or changed. Throughout this post, I use a hypothetical college learning platform to explore that idea. My focus is on how a team could understand the whole service, compare possible improvements, and use models to make its decisions clearer.

Looking at how the pieces fit

To me, systems thinking means looking at a problem with enough context to understand how its parts influence one another. Instead of stopping at the first explanation, I would ask what the service is supposed to accomplish, what it depends on, and how a change could affect the people using it. INCOSE describes this perspective as paying attention to the whole system and the relationships among its parts (International Council on Systems Engineering [INCOSE], n.d.-a).

Holt (2021) also explains the importance of understanding elements through their relationships with other elements and systems. I take this to mean that knowing what each component does is only part of the job. For example, an account service and a course platform could each operate correctly but still fail to exchange the information needed for access. In that situation, testing the two components separately would not explain the student's difficulty.

I would begin by deciding what belongs inside the project. For the learning platform example, I would include the login process, account records, course access, support procedures, and the people involved. A student's home connection would be an outside dependency. The college might not control it, but the team would still need to consider how a weak connection affects access. Setting a boundary helps keep the project manageable without pretending that outside conditions do not matter.

Why the first answer may not be the best answer

A more linear way of thinking moves from a problem to a cause and then to a solution. Someone might reason that the platform is slow because the server is too small, so the college needs a larger server. If the evidence supports that explanation, the solution may be reasonable. Simple problems do not always require a complicated investigation. A broken cable, for example, might only need replacement. The concern is assuming a direct explanation before checking what is happening.

For the imagined learning platform, I would want to know when the slowdown occurs and which step takes the longest. Does it happen before the student signs in or after the account has been confirmed? Does it affect everyone or only certain courses? Is the problem worse near an assignment deadline? These questions would help separate possible causes. A larger server might help one part of the process while leaving the actual delay somewhere else.

Another possibility is that user behavior contributes to the slowdown. Suppose the login page gives no sign that it is processing a request. Students might press the button several times, adding more requests. If those requests increase the delay, students may click even more. In this hypothetical feedback loop, a response to the problem makes it worse. A clearer message and a way to prevent repeated submissions might help address the pattern.

I would also look beyond the immediate result of a change. Imagine that a stricter account recovery policy reduces approved requests during its first week. That number alone could make the policy look effective. However, students who cannot recover their accounts might start contacting instructors or support staff instead. The work has moved elsewhere rather than disappeared. I would want to consider successful access, security, and support demand together before deciding whether the change was an improvement.

The human side of a digital system

It is easy to describe a digital environment using only technical terms. However, NASA's explanation of a system includes people, procedures, and processes as well as equipment and software (National Aeronautics and Space Administration [NASA], n.d.). That wider description makes sense for this example. A support worker's decision can affect account access, and a student's interpretation of an error message can affect what the student does next.

Consider a student whose verification phone is lost or broken. A system might have strong login protection but no practical recovery path for that situation. I would want the design team to discuss an alternative before the service is released. That discussion should include how identity is checked, who can authorize a recovery, and what information the student receives. Security and usability both belong in the conversation because access should be protected and legitimate users still need a workable way back in.

The people reviewing the design would probably see different needs. An instructor may care most about whether students can submit work on time. A support worker may notice unclear instructions that lead to repeated calls. A security specialist may identify opportunities for account misuse. I think those differences are useful. Bringing them into the discussion could expose problems that a team focused only on coding might overlook. The goal would be an agreed design, with reasons for its tradeoffs.

Using models to make the discussion concrete

Understanding the connections is one task; explaining them clearly to a team is another. That is where a model-based approach could help. A model represents selected details of a system so people can examine them. INCOSE explains that model-based systems engineering supports requirements, design, analysis, verification, and validation through modeling across the life cycle (INCOSE, n.d.-b). I see its value in giving the team something specific to review and update as decisions develop.

For the college platform, I would start with a model of the main services and the information moving between them. It could show how the browser sends a login request, how the account service checks it, and how the learning platform receives an access decision. The connections would need meaningful labels. A page full of boxes and arrows would not help much if everyone interpreted those arrows differently. Clear labels would make it easier to discuss responsibilities and assumptions.

I would then model a few situations rather than only the successful login. One situation could involve an incorrect password. Another could involve a verification service that stops responding. A third could involve account recovery after a lost phone. For each situation, the team could follow the steps and check what the student sees. This would be a useful way to notice missing messages, unclear recovery options, or responsibilities that have not been assigned.

Models could also help connect requirements with implementation and testing. Suppose a requirement says that account recovery must preserve access to existing courses. I would connect that requirement to the account record, the recovery steps, and the test that checks course access afterward. If the design changes, those links would help the team identify what needs another review. The benefit is keeping the purpose of a feature visible throughout the project instead of losing it once development starts.

Trying an idea before students depend on it

A model-based approach would be especially helpful when a design has several possible outcomes. For example, the college could compare a recovery process that always requires staff approval with one that allows some approved cases to be completed automatically. Before choosing, the team could estimate how many requests arrive and how much staff time each process needs. This would help reveal workload concerns, although it would not settle every question about security or fairness.

A simulation could explore a busy deadline period using those estimates. If requests arrive faster than staff can review them, the model might show a growing queue. The team could then consider changes to staffing, instructions, or the process itself. I would label the assumptions clearly and try more than one scenario. A quiet week and a deadline week could produce different results. Treating one estimate as certain would make the exercise much less useful.

The proposed design would still need checks against real conditions. NASA separates verification, which asks whether requirements are met, from validation, which asks whether the product serves its intended purpose in the intended environment (NASA, n.d.). In this example, verification could check that recovery follows the approved steps. Validation could involve seeing whether legitimate students can complete those steps and regain course access. Following a procedure correctly does not automatically mean the experience meets the user's need.

This distinction changes how I would judge success. I would not be satisfied only because a feature passed its individual checks. I would also want to know whether it works with the surrounding services and whether people can use it under realistic conditions. A recovery option that works during a quiet demonstration might be difficult to use when support demand rises. Testing those conditions would make the team's confidence more meaningful.

Keeping the approach useful

Although I see benefits in modeling, I would be careful about relying on it too much. A model only reflects the information and assumptions included in it. If the team forgets a dependency or leaves an outdated policy in place, its conclusions could be misleading. In the college example, changing the verification method would require reviewing the recovery model too. Someone would need responsibility for keeping those details current.

There is also a practical limit to how much detail a project needs. I would not create an elaborate simulation just to change the wording of a button. I would choose the model based on the decision being made and the consequences of getting it wrong. A change affecting account recovery would deserve closer examination because it could influence access, security, and support work. The time spent modeling should help the team make a better decision.

I would also invite people outside the technical team to review the parts relevant to them. Support workers could point out exceptions missing from the recovery process, while students could explain which messages are confusing. Their feedback would not replace technical analysis, but it could challenge assumptions that seemed reasonable during design. For me, this is a reminder that a model should encourage discussion rather than become something people hesitate to question.

What this means for my approach to IT

As an ITAM student interested in cybersecurity, I want to get better at connecting technical choices with their wider effects. This topic gives me a practical starting point. Before proposing a fix, I would identify the service's purpose, the people it supports, and its major dependencies. Then I would compare possible changes and decide what evidence would show that the chosen approach actually helps.

The imagined login problem shows how much can sit behind a simple screen. Systems thinking helps me look for relationships, feedback, and effects that appear later. A model-based approach helps make those ideas specific enough to examine with others. I would carry both habits into future projects: ask how the pieces work together, and use a clear representation to check the design before people have to depend on it.
