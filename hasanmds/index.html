Sakib Hasan

Behind the Screen: How Systems Thinking Helps Us Design Better Digital Services

Starting with an everyday problem

Picture a student trying to submit an assignment shortly before the deadline. The assignment is finished, but the student cannot get past the login screen. It is tempting to call this a website problem and leave it at that. However, the website may be working while another service is unavailable. The student's account, internet connection, verification method, and course permissions could all affect what happens next. For the person waiting at the screen, those separate pieces are part of the same experience.

This imagined situation is how I would explain the value of systems thinking. A digital environment is built from connections, and those connections deserve attention when something is designed or changed. Throughout this post, I use a hypothetical college learning platform to explore that idea. My focus is on how a team could understand the whole service, compare possible improvements, and use models to make its decisions clearer.

Looking at how the pieces fit

To me, systems thinking means looking at a problem with enough context to understand how its parts influence one another. Instead of stopping at the first explanation, I would ask what the service is supposed to accomplish, what it depends on, and how a change could affect the people using it. INCOSE describes this perspective as paying attention to the whole system and the relationships among its parts (International Council on Systems Engineering [INCOSE], n.d.-a).

Holt (2021) also explains the importance of understanding elements through their relationships with other elements and systems. I take this to mean that knowing what each component does is only part of the job. For example, an account service and a course platform could each operate correctly but still fail to exchange the information needed for access. In that situation, testing the two components separately would not explain the student's difficulty.

I would begin by deciding what belongs inside the project. For the learning platform example, I would include the login process, account records, course access, support procedures, and the people involved. A student's home connection would be an outside dependency. The college might not control it, but the team would still need to consider how a weak connection affects access. Setting a boundary helps keep the project manageable without pretending that outside conditions do not matter.

Why the first answer may not be the best answer

A more linear way of thinking moves from a problem to a cause and then to a solution. Someone might reason that the platform is slow because the server is too small, so the college needs a larger server. If the evidence supports that explanation, the solution may be reasonable. Simple problems do not always require a complicated investigation. A broken cable, for example, might only need replacement. The concern is assuming a direct explanation before checking what is happening.

For the imagined learning platform, I would want to know when the slowdown occurs and which step takes the longest. Does it happen before the student signs in or after the account has been confirmed? Does it affect everyone or only certain courses? Is the problem worse near an assignment deadline? These questions would help separate possible causes. A larger server might help one part of the process while leaving the actual delay somewhere else.

Another possibility is that user behavior contributes to the slowdown. Suppose the login page gives no sign that it is processing a request. Students might press the button several times, adding more requests. If those requests increase the delay, students may click even more. In this hypothetical feedback loop, a response to the problem makes it worse. A clearer message and a way to prevent repeated submissions might help address the pattern.

I would also look beyond the immediate result of a change. Imagine that a stricter account recovery policy reduces approved requests during its first week. That number alone could make the policy look effective. However, students who cannot recover their accounts might start contacting instructors or support staff instead. The work has moved elsewhere rather than disappeared. I would want to consider successful access, security, and support demand together before deciding whether the change was an improvement.

The human side of a digital system

It is easy to describe a digital environment using only technical terms. However, NASA's explanation of a system includes people, procedures, and processes as well as equipment and software (National Aeronautics and Space Administration [NASA], n.d.). That wider description makes sense for this example. A support worker's decision can affect account access, and a student's interpretation of an error message can affect what the student does next.

Consider a student whose verification phone is lost or broken. A system might have strong login protection but no practical recovery path for that situation. I would want the design team to discuss an alternative before the service is released. That discussion should include how identity is checked, who can authorize a recovery, and what information the student receives. Security and usability both belong in the conversation because access should be protected and legitimate users still need a workable way back in.

The people reviewing the design would probably see different needs. An instructor may care most about whether students can submit work on time. A support worker may notice unclear instructions that lead to repeated calls. A security specialist may identify opportunities for account misuse. I think those differences are useful. Bringing them into the discussion could expose problems that a team focused only on coding might overlook. The goal would be an agreed design, with reasons for its tradeoffs.

Using models to make the discussion concrete

Understanding the connections is one task; explaining them clearly to a team is another. That is where a model-based approach could help. A model represents selected details of a system so people can examine them. INCOSE explains that model-based systems engineering supports requirements, design, analysis, verification, and validation through modeling across the life cycle (INCOSE, n.d.-b). I see its value in giving the team something specific to review and update as decisions develop.

For the college platform, I would start with a model of the main services and the information moving between them. It could show how the browser sends a login request, how the account service checks it, and how the learning platform receives an access decision. The connections would need meaningful labels. A page full of boxes and arrows would not help much if everyone interpreted those arrows differently. Clear labels would make it easier to discuss responsibilities and assumptions.

I would then model a few situations rather than only the successful login. One situation could involve an incorrect password. Another could involve a verification service that stops responding. A third could involve account recovery after a lost phone. For each situation, the team could follow the steps and check what the student sees. This would be a useful way to notice missing messages, unclear recovery options, or responsibilities that have not been assigned.

Models could also help connect requirements with implementation and testing. Suppose a requirement says that account recovery must preserve access to existing courses. I would connect that requirement to the account record, the recovery steps, and the test that checks course access afterward. If the design changes, those links would help the team identify what needs another review. The benefit is keeping the purpose of a feature visible throughout the project instead of losing it once development starts.

Trying an idea before students depend on it

A model-based approach would be especially helpful when a design has several possible outcomes. For example, the college could compare a recovery process that always requires staff approval with one that allows some approved cases to be completed automatically. Before choosing, the team could estimate how many requests arrive and how much staff time each process needs. This would help reveal workload concerns, although it would not settle every question about security or fairness.

A simulation could explore a busy deadline period using those estimates. If requests arrive faster than staff can review them, the model might show a growing queue. The team could then consider changes to staffing, instructions, or the process itself. I would label the assumptions clearly and try more than one scenario. A quiet week and a deadline week could produce different results. Treating one estimate as certain would make the exercise much less useful.

The proposed design would still need checks against real conditions. NASA separates verification, which asks whether requirements are met, from validation, which asks whether the product serves its intended purpose in the intended environment (NASA, n.d.). In this example, verification could check that recovery follows the approved steps. Validation could involve seeing whether legitimate students can complete those steps and regain course access. Following a procedure correctly does not automatically mean the experience meets the user's need.

This distinction changes how I would judge success. I would not be satisfied only because a feature passed its individual checks. I would also want to know whether it works with the surrounding services and whether people can use it under realistic conditions. A recovery option that works during a quiet demonstration might be difficult to use when support demand rises. Testing those conditions would make the team's confidence more meaningful.

Keeping the approach useful

Although I see benefits in modeling, I would be careful about relying on it too much. A model only reflects the information and assumptions included in it. If the team forgets a dependency or leaves an outdated policy in place, its conclusions could be misleading. In the college example, changing the verification method would require reviewing the recovery model too. Someone would need responsibility for keeping those details current.

There is also a practical limit to how much detail a project needs. I would not create an elaborate simulation just to change the wording of a button. I would choose the model based on the decision being made and the consequences of getting it wrong. A change affecting account recovery would deserve closer examination because it could influence access, security, and support work. The time spent modeling should help the team make a better decision.

I would also invite people outside the technical team to review the parts relevant to them. Support workers could point out exceptions missing from the recovery process, while students could explain which messages are confusing. Their feedback would not replace technical analysis, but it could challenge assumptions that seemed reasonable during design. For me, this is a reminder that a model should encourage discussion rather than become something people hesitate to question.

What this means for my approach to IT

As an ITAM student interested in cybersecurity, I want to get better at connecting technical choices with their wider effects. This topic gives me a practical starting point. Before proposing a fix, I would identify the service's purpose, the people it supports, and its major dependencies. Then I would compare possible changes and decide what evidence would show that the chosen approach actually helps.

The imagined login problem shows how much can sit behind a simple screen. Systems thinking helps me look for relationships, feedback, and effects that appear later. A model-based approach helps make those ideas specific enough to examine with others. I would carry both habits into future projects: ask how the pieces work together, and use a clear representation to check the design before people have to depend on it.

Thinking in Life Cycles: What Happens After a Digital System Goes Live?

By Sakib Hasan

A launch date is only one part of the story

When I think about a digital system, my attention usually goes to what I can see: the login screen, the menus, and whether I can finish a task without getting confused. A college student portal is a useful example. Students need to register for classes, check information, and reach important services. However, a portal that looks good on its first day can still become difficult to maintain, expensive to operate, or unsafe to use. Thinking in life cycles changes the question from whether a system works today to whether it can continue meeting people's needs over time.

For me, the most useful lesson is that finishing development does not finish the responsibility. Someone must support users, update software, manage information, and eventually replace the system. This post uses a hypothetical college portal to explore those responsibilities. Comparing linear, iterative, and incremental models helps explain how different development choices affect the same system. My view is that choosing a model should depend on uncertainty, risk, and the usefulness of partial releases, rather than on which approach sounds the most modern.

Understanding the life cycle before choosing a model

Holt (2023, pp. 134–137) makes an important distinction between a life cycle and a life cycle model. The life cycle identifies the stages through which a system evolves. The model describes how those stages are executed, including their sequence. In other words, listing the stages does not automatically tell a team how it should organize the work. Two projects can involve similar stages while following different paths through them.

The textbook identifies conception, development, production, utilization, support, and retirement as system life cycle stages. Applied to a student portal, conception would involve understanding student and staff needs. Development would explore possible solutions, while production would create and test the chosen system. Utilization would involve everyday use, and support would keep the portal operating effectively alongside that use. Retirement would address its eventual removal and replacement. These examples are my application of Holt's framework, rather than a description of a particular university's internal practices.

This wider perspective matters because a development project and the system it produces do not necessarily have the same lifespan. Holt (2023) explains that several projects can exist within a system's life cycle. A portal might have separate projects for registration, authentication, and a later migration. Treating each project as isolated could leave gaps between teams. For example, a team might finish an upgrade without clearly assigning responsibility for the older service that still holds student records.

Linear models: clear steps with limited flexibility

In a linear model, stages follow a defined sequence, with each stage completed before the next begins. Holt (2023, pp. 145–146) describes this approach as suitable for smaller, well-understood projects whose needs are unlikely to change. Its strengths are easy to appreciate. A team can establish responsibilities, review deliverables, and understand what must happen next. For a narrow portal change, such as implementing a clearly specified downloadable form, this structure could provide enough control without unnecessary complexity.

The difficulty appears when the original understanding is incomplete. Imagine a team developing an entire registration portal from an initial requirements document. If students only try the completed system near the end, they might discover that the registration process is confusing on a phone or that error messages do not explain how to fix a problem. Those discoveries could affect decisions already built into several parts of the system. A clear schedule would not make those problems disappear.

I would therefore be cautious about choosing a strictly linear approach for a large portal replacement. College policies, user expectations, and connected services can change during development. The model's simplicity is valuable, but that value depends on having a problem stable enough to justify it. For me, the drawback is delayed learning: the team may make major commitments before it has enough evidence that the proposed experience works for its users.

Iterative models: improving through repeated learning

An iterative model uses repeated passes through stages rather than relying on one pass. Holt (2023, pp. 146–147) explains how shorter iterations make complex work more manageable. A portal team could develop an early version of a registration workflow, evaluate it with users, and revise it. The next iteration might improve navigation, clarify prerequisites, or make the confirmation screen easier to understand. The important feature is the opportunity to revisit decisions using what the team has learned.

NASA's systems engineering guidance also describes iteration as repeating a process on the same product to address discrepancies or variations from requirements (National Aeronautics and Space Administration [NASA], n.d.). This supports a practical interpretation: repeating work should have a purpose. In the portal example, changing a menu because students repeatedly choose the wrong option is a meaningful iteration. Redesigning that menu simply because someone prefers a different appearance would need a stronger justification.

Iteration also creates management challenges. Stakeholders may keep suggesting changes, and the team could spend too much time polishing one function while other necessary work remains unfinished. Feedback can conflict, too. Students might prefer fewer steps, while staff need additional information to process a request. I would address this by giving each iteration a specific question and measurable acceptance criteria. For example, can test users complete registration and correctly identify their final enrollment status? An iteration should produce evidence for a decision, not just another version.

Incremental models: delivering useful parts over time

An incremental model delivers a system through several releases. Holt (2023, pp. 148–149) describes an initial concept stage covering the overall needs, followed by development and production of subsets. For a portal, the first release might provide secure access and basic student information. Later releases could add registration, advising appointments, and additional services. Each increment should provide a usable capability while contributing to the broader system.

The main advantage is that people can receive useful functions before the complete system is finished. A college could introduce one service, provide focused training, and learn about operational problems before expanding. Smaller releases could also make it easier to identify which change caused a new issue. However, these benefits depend on whether the system can be divided into useful parts. Holt (2023) emphasizes that some systems cannot be separated into suitable subsets.

For the hypothetical portal, authentication and authorization would be essential foundations. Releasing access to student records before reliable permission checks were available would be an unacceptable choice. There would also be integration work between releases: a later registration feature would need to use the same student information correctly. My concern is that an attractive release schedule could hide architectural weaknesses. Incremental delivery works best when the team plans shared data, interfaces, and dependencies before promising individual features.

Choosing an approach for the portal

The difference between iteration and increments becomes clearer when I separate improvement from added capability. Revising an existing registration screen after testing is iterative. Introducing an advising appointment feature alongside registration is incremental. A team could do both by delivering the portal in useful increments and improving each increment through repeated evaluation. These approaches can work together, but the terms describe different aspects of the work.

For this portal, I would favor incremental delivery supported by iterative evaluation. Students could begin using a limited set of dependable services while the team developed additional capabilities. Within each release, testing would help refine usability and check assumptions. A linear sequence could still be appropriate for a small, stable task within that larger effort. This is a proposed approach for the example, rather than a claim that one model is universally superior.

I would also use review gates to decide whether a release was ready. Holt (2023, pp. 142–143) explains that gates can lead to moving forward, remaining in a stage, returning to an earlier stage, or cancellation. For the portal, passing a gate should require evidence such as successful permission testing, a workable support plan, and a recovery procedure. Reaching a planned date alone would not demonstrate readiness.

Design and security across the system's life

Life cycle thinking affects design because today's choices become tomorrow's maintenance responsibilities. Suppose the portal uses a custom component that only one developer understands. It might satisfy the immediate deadline, yet leave future staff struggling to diagnose errors. I would want the team to consider documentation, accessible interfaces, replaceable components, and clear ownership from the beginning. These choices might not be visible to students, but they would affect how reliably the portal serves them.

Security provides another reason to look beyond launch. Souppaya et al. (2022) explain that secure development practices need to be integrated into software development life cycle models. Their Secure Software Development Framework aims to reduce vulnerabilities, limit their potential impact, and address their underlying causes. The implication for my example is that choosing an iterative or incremental approach does not automatically make the portal secure. The team still needs deliberate security practices within its chosen model.

In my proposed portal, those practices would include reviewing access rules during design, testing them before release, and assigning responsibility for vulnerabilities discovered later. If a student changes programs or an employee leaves, permissions must continue matching legitimate needs. I would also want a clear process for reporting a suspected problem. A successful launch would be a starting point for these responsibilities, not evidence that the security work had ended.

Sustainability, evolution, and retirement

I understand sustainability here as the ability to keep providing value without creating avoidable environmental, financial, or human burdens. For the portal, that could mean reviewing unnecessary data storage, keeping supported equipment useful where practical, and avoiding features that create more maintenance than benefit. These are design questions to investigate, not automatic environmental savings. For example, keeping old equipment longer would need to be balanced against its reliability, support status, and energy use.

There is a human side as well. A portal that requires students to contact staff for routine tasks may transfer its design problems into support workloads. An update that works only on newer devices could make access harder for some users. I would assess sustainability partly by asking whether people can continue using and supporting the system with the resources actually available. The cheapest initial solution might become expensive if it repeatedly interrupts essential services.

Evolution also requires attention to the relationships between systems. NASA (n.d.) emphasizes that systems include people, procedures, hardware, and software, with value arising from how elements work together. Applied to the portal, replacing one component could affect staff workflows and connected services. I would therefore assess changes across those relationships instead of judging a new component only by its individual features.

Finally, retirement deserves planning before it becomes urgent. Holt (2023) includes safe and secure decommissioning in the life cycle. For the portal, I would plan how to migrate necessary records, verify their accuracy, close obsolete accounts, and remove services that should no longer be accessible. Simply switching off the old website would not resolve every dependency. A retirement plan should also identify who confirms that essential functions have transferred successfully and who handles any remaining records.

What I would take into future IT work

My main takeaway is that a digital system should be judged across its whole period of usefulness. Linear models offer clarity when needs are stable. Iterative models create opportunities to learn and refine. Incremental models deliver useful capabilities over time, provided those capabilities can be separated responsibly. Each approach has tradeoffs, and the team must make those tradeoffs visible.

As I prepare for work in IT and cybersecurity, I want to ask questions that reach beyond immediate functionality: Who will maintain this? How will users get help? What happens when a dependency changes? How will the system be retired? Those questions make life cycle thinking practical. A system succeeds when it can serve people reliably, adapt with purpose, and eventually be replaced without leaving unnecessary problems behind.

Module 2.1: How Four UX Laws Shape Our Digital Experiences

By Sakib Hasan

Introduction

When I use a website or an app, I usually notice whether it feels easy or frustrating before thinking about the design behind it. A simple menu, a clear button, or a familiar layout can make a big difference. Jakob's Law, Fitts's Law, Hick's Law, and Miller's Law help explain why these design choices matter. Understanding these principles can help us create digital experiences that are easier to navigate and understand.

Human-Centered Design: Understanding People First

Human-centered design means creating technology around the needs, abilities, and experiences of real people. Instead of assuming that a system is useful simply because it works, designers need to understand how people actually interact with it. ISO 9241-210:2019 emphasizes considering users, their tasks, and the environments in which they use technology throughout the design process (International Organization for Standardization, 2019).

I think this approach is important because people have different levels of technical experience. For example, a university registration system might seem simple to its developers, but a new student could struggle to understand the available options. A human-centered approach would involve listening to students, observing common difficulties, and improving the system based on their experiences.

IDEO's Field Guide to Human-Centered Design introduces three phases: Inspiration, Ideation, and Implementation. Inspiration focuses on understanding people and their needs. Ideation involves developing and testing possible solutions. Implementation focuses on putting those solutions into practice (IDEO.org, 2015). What stands out to me is that good design requires learning from people rather than making every decision based on assumptions.

Perception, attention, and empathy also influence design decisions. Perception affects how users recognize information, while attention determines what they notice first. Empathy helps designers consider the frustrations and challenges that different users may experience. For example, clear instructions and visible navigation options can help students complete online tasks without unnecessary confusion.

Sources: ISO 9241-210:2019 and IDEO Human-Centered Design.

1. Jakob's Law: Why Familiar Designs Matter

Jakob's Law explains that people spend most of their time using other websites, so they expect new websites to work in familiar ways. When a website uses a layout that people already recognize, they can spend less time figuring out how to navigate it and more time focusing on what they actually want to accomplish (Yablonski, n.d.).

For example, most online shopping websites place the shopping cart near the top-right corner. If a website suddenly moved the cart to an unusual location, customers might struggle to find it. Even if the design looked creative, it could make shopping more frustrating.

I think this law is important because creativity should not make a website unnecessarily difficult to use. However, following familiar patterns does not mean every website must look identical. Designers can still introduce new ideas as long as users can understand how to interact with them.

Source: Jakob's Law - Laws of UX (Yablonski, n.d.).

2. Fitts's Law: Making Buttons Easier to Use

Fitts's Law explains that the time needed to reach a target depends on its size and distance. In simple terms, larger buttons that are closer to the user are usually easier and faster to select. This principle is important when designing websites and mobile applications because people need to interact with buttons, menus, and other controls (Yablonski, n.d.).

For example, imagine trying to press a tiny button on a smartphone screen while walking. It can be frustrating because the button is difficult to select accurately. A larger button with enough space around it would make the experience easier. This is especially important for people who have difficulty with precise movements.

From my perspective, Fitts's Law shows that a good design should consider how people physically interact with technology. However, making every button large is not always practical. Designers need to balance button size, available screen space, and the importance of each action. Important actions should be easy to reach without accidentally selecting something else.

3. Hick's Law: Why Too Many Choices Can Be Overwhelming

Hick's Law explains that the time it takes a person to make a decision generally increases as the number and complexity of choices increase. This matters in website design because too many options can make it difficult for users to decide what to do next (Yablonski, n.d.).

For example, imagine visiting a university website to register for classes. If the homepage displays dozens of unrelated links without clear categories, students may struggle to find the registration page. A simpler menu that groups related options together could make the process much easier.

I believe Hick's Law is especially useful when designing websites that contain a lot of information. However, removing too many options can also make important features difficult to find. The goal should be to organize choices logically rather than simply eliminate them. A well-designed website helps users make decisions without feeling overwhelmed.

Source: Hick's Law - Laws of UX (Yablonski, n.d.).

4. Miller's Law: Understanding How People Process Information

Miller's Law is based on research suggesting that people can hold a limited number of information chunks in short-term memory. The commonly cited number is around seven, plus or minus two, although this should not be treated as a strict rule for every person or situation (Miller, 1956).

For example, imagine a student using an online learning platform with a long list of assignments, announcements, grades, and course materials displayed together. It can become difficult to remember where everything is located. Dividing the information into clear sections, such as Assignments, Grades, and Course Resources, can make the platform easier to understand.

In my opinion, Miller's Law reminds designers to organize information in manageable groups. However, it does not mean that every website should have exactly seven menu items. The more important lesson is to reduce unnecessary memory demands by using clear labels, familiar categories, and logical organization.

Source: Miller's Law - Laws of UX (Yablonski, n.d.).

Comparing the Four UX Laws in Everyday Technology

Although Jakob's Law, Fitts's Law, Hick's Law, and Miller's Law focus on different aspects of user experience, they share an important goal: making technology easier for people to understand and use. Jakob's Law deals with familiarity, Fitts's Law focuses on physical interaction, Hick's Law addresses decision-making, and Miller's Law highlights the challenges of remembering and processing information.

One example where all four principles come together is an online university registration system. Students expect familiar navigation menus because they have used similar websites before. This connects with Jakob's Law. Registration buttons should be large enough to select comfortably, particularly on mobile devices, which relates to Fitts's Law. Course choices should be organized into meaningful categories to reduce confusion, reflecting Hick's Law. Finally, important instructions should be presented in smaller, manageable sections so students do not have to remember too much information at once, which connects with Miller's Law.

However, these laws should not be applied without considering the situation. A simpler menu might reduce the number of visible choices but make an important feature harder to find. Similarly, increasing button sizes can improve accessibility but may create a crowded screen. This is why designers need to test their ideas with actual users instead of assuming that following a particular law will automatically produce a good experience.

Accessibility, Empathy, and Responsible Design

Another important consideration is accessibility. People interact with technology in different ways depending on their abilities, devices, and surroundings. Someone using a smartphone with one hand may need larger touch targets, while another person using assistive technology may depend on clear headings and logical navigation. Human-centered design encourages developers to consider these differences rather than treating every user as identical.

In my opinion, empathy becomes meaningful when designers use it to make practical improvements. For example, a university website that provides clear error messages, straightforward registration instructions, and consistent navigation can reduce stress for students. These improvements may seem small, but they can make a meaningful difference when someone is working under a deadline.

I also think user experience has a connection to cybersecurity. When security settings are confusing, users may make mistakes or ignore important warnings. Clear instructions, understandable options, and well-designed confirmation messages can help people make safer decisions. This shows that usability and security should support each other rather than being treated as separate concerns.

Conclusion: Applying the Four UX Laws Together

After studying these four UX laws, I have realized that good digital design is not only about making a website attractive. It is also about understanding how people think, make decisions, and interact with technology. Jakob's Law emphasizes familiarity, Fitts's Law focuses on making interactions easier, Hick's Law explains the challenges of too many choices, and Miller's Law highlights the importance of organizing information.

For example, a university website could apply all four principles by using familiar navigation menus, making registration buttons easy to select, grouping course options into clear categories, and presenting information in manageable sections. These improvements could make the website more accessible and less frustrating for students.

As someone studying information technology and cybersecurity, I believe these principles are especially valuable because digital systems should be both secure and easy to use. A complicated interface can lead to mistakes, confusion, and unnecessary frustration. My biggest takeaway is that successful design should balance usability, accessibility, and functionality rather than focus only on appearance.

When a Simple Website Feels Complicated: Cognitive Load and Better Design

By Sakib Hasan | Module 2.2 Blog Post

The Moment a Website Becomes Work

Imagine opening a college website to complete one simple task: find the registration page and choose a class. That should not be difficult. Yet the screen might contain several menus, colorful announcements, unfamiliar abbreviations, and buttons that look almost the same. Before selecting anything, you have to stop and figure out where to start. The problem is not necessarily that the user lacks computer skills. Sometimes the design is asking the user to do work that the website could have handled more clearly.

This situation interests me because I use digital tools for school, communication, and technical projects. I have noticed that two websites can offer similar information but feel completely different to use. On one website, I can find the next action without thinking about the menu. On another, I repeatedly check where I am because I am worried about clicking the wrong thing. That extra thinking is part of cognitive load. Good design should help people focus on their goals instead of spending their attention decoding the interface.

What Cognitive Load Actually Means

Cognitive load is the mental effort we use while processing information and making decisions. When we interact with a website, we are not only reading its words. We are also trying to remember our goal, understand the available choices, recognize patterns, and decide what will happen after a click. Whitenton (2013) explains that interfaces should reduce unnecessary mental work because people have limited processing resources. This helps explain why a crowded page can feel exhausting even when none of its individual pieces is especially difficult.

Not all mental effort is bad. If I am comparing classes, I should spend time thinking about which class fits my schedule. That is meaningful work related to my decision. However, figuring out whether a button labeled 'Proceed' means save, pay, or submit is unnecessary work created by poor design. The distinction matters: a designer cannot remove every challenge from a complicated task, but the designer can avoid adding confusion that serves no useful purpose.

For example, consider a form asking for contact information. If it explains each field, keeps related questions together, and clearly marks what is required, I can focus on providing accurate details. If the same form hides its rules until the final screen, I may have to submit it several times to learn what the system expects. The second form creates extra effort without improving the task. In my view, that is a design problem, not a user problem.

Tesler's Law: Complexity Has to Go Somewhere

Tesler's Law, also called the law of conservation of complexity, suggests that a system contains some complexity that cannot simply disappear. Someone must handle it. The question is whether that responsibility belongs mainly to the people building the system or to the people using it. Yablonski (2024) argues that designers should take on as much of this complexity as possible instead of transferring unnecessary effort to users. I find this principle especially useful because it changes how we judge a design decision.

Think about booking an appointment. Behind the scenes, an application might need to check available times, avoid scheduling conflicts, account for time zones, and store the appointment correctly. The user should not need to understand all those technical steps. A good interface can show available choices, identify the time zone, and confirm the selected appointment. The software is still complicated, but most of the difficulty stays behind the screen. If the application instead asks users to calculate differences between time zones and manually verify availability, it has shifted too much responsibility onto them.

There is an important limit, though. Simplicity should not mean hiding information that a person genuinely needs. For example, an appointment system must still show the date, location, and cancellation rules clearly. Removing those details would make the screen look cleaner while creating risk later. Therefore, I understand Tesler's Law as a reminder to manage complexity carefully, not as permission to remove every detail. The best design makes complicated processes understandable without taking control away from the user.

From a student's perspective, this is relevant to learning platforms as well. A submission process may involve file formats, deadlines, and confirmation records, but a student should see straightforward instructions and a clear success message. When a submission disappears without confirmation, students may upload the file again or wonder whether the assignment was received. A little more work by the development team could prevent repeated uncertainty for many students.

Principle One: We Seek and Use Visual Structure

One principle from Jeff Johnson's Designing with the Mind in Mind is that people seek and use visual structure. A clear layout helps us understand which pieces of information belong together and which deserve attention first (Johnson, 2020). I think this principle is easy to overlook because design structure can seem like a visual preference. However, headings, spacing, and grouping do more than make a page attractive. They reduce the amount of searching that people must do.

Consider a college course page that puts announcements, instructor contact details, assignments, and grades in one uninterrupted block. Even if every word is accurate, the page can be difficult to scan. Now imagine the same information separated by meaningful headings and consistent sections. Students can recognize the area they need and skip unrelated material. This is particularly useful when someone has only a few minutes to check an assignment deadline or submit a discussion post.

Visual hierarchy also matters within a single form. A page title should be more noticeable than ordinary instructions. Required information should be grouped, and the main action should look more important than secondary options. If every button uses the same size and color, users must read each one carefully to understand its purpose. I would rather see a clear 'Submit assignment' button and a quieter 'Cancel' option. The design then communicates priority without asking the user to guess.

There is a balance to keep here as well. If a designer makes everything large, bold, or bright, nothing stands out anymore. Helpful hierarchy depends on contrast between important and supporting details. Good spacing is not wasted space; it can make relationships easier to see. For me, the key lesson is that organization should match how people actually search for information, not just how developers store that information behind the scenes.

Principle Two: Recognition Is Easier Than Recall

Another principle from Johnson (2020) is that recognition is easier than recall. Recognizing an option means seeing it and knowing what it does. Recalling information means pulling it from memory without an immediate reminder. Interfaces that require too much recall make users carry extra details in their heads. A familiar icon paired with a clear label often works better than an unexplained command that someone must memorize before using the system.

I can connect this idea to using software such as Visual Studio Code. Experienced users may remember many keyboard shortcuts, but beginners often depend on visible menus while they learn. Neither approach is wrong. A good program should allow people to discover commands through the interface and eventually use shortcuts when they become comfortable. If basic actions are only possible through hidden shortcuts, the software raises the learning barrier unnecessarily.

The same problem appears when a website asks users to remember information from an earlier page. Imagine choosing a payment option, moving to a confirmation page, and then finding that the selected option is no longer visible. You might go backward just to check what you chose. A small summary on the confirmation page would reduce that burden. The design would support recognition by displaying information at the moment it matters.

This principle does not mean people should never learn or remember anything. Learning keyboard shortcuts can make regular tasks faster. The problem occurs when a design assumes all users already have that knowledge. A new student, a first-time customer, and an experienced employee may approach the same interface differently. Providing visible choices makes the system more welcoming while still leaving room for advanced features.

When Poor Design Creates Real Problems

Confusing interfaces do not always lead to minor inconvenience. In a university setting, selecting the wrong option might mean missing a deadline or submitting the wrong file. In a workplace, a misleading message could cause someone to overlook an important security warning. When users are overloaded, they may start clicking quickly just to get through a process. That reaction is understandable, but it increases the chance of mistakes.

I am especially interested in this issue from an information technology and cybersecurity perspective. A security dashboard can contain dozens of alerts, technical terms, and status indicators. If everything appears equally urgent, an analyst has to spend extra time deciding where to look. A better dashboard could group related alerts, highlight the most important risks, and explain what action is expected. The underlying security work remains challenging, but the interface can support more focused decisions.

Accessibility is another reason design clarity matters. The World Wide Web Consortium (W3C, n.d.) recommends meaningful labels for form controls so users understand what information is requested. Clear labels can help people using assistive technologies, but they also help anyone who is tired, distracted, or unfamiliar with the application. A field labeled only 'Info' is less helpful than one labeled 'Student email address.' Small details like this make the interaction more predictable.

I also think designers sometimes underestimate how different a screen looks when someone is stressed. A user trying to finish a task before a deadline is not carefully studying every instruction. They are likely scanning for the next action. That is why understandable wording, visible feedback, and a logical sequence can matter more than decorative effects. A beautiful website can still be difficult to use if it does not explain what is happening.

How I Would Improve a Confusing Interface

If I were asked to redesign a confusing student portal, I would begin by watching how a new user tries to complete a real task. I would not assume the problem is obvious just because I know where everything is located. I would ask the user to find a class, check a deadline, or submit a document and note where they hesitate. Those moments could reveal confusing labels, missing feedback, or choices that are presented in the wrong order.

Next, I would group related information under clear headings and remove visual elements that compete with the task. I would use straightforward button labels such as 'Review schedule' or 'Submit assignment' rather than vague words such as 'Next' when the result is not obvious. I would also display a short summary before any important final action. These choices are consistent with Whitenton's (2013) advice to minimize unnecessary cognitive effort and help people concentrate on meaningful decisions.

Finally, I would test the revised design with different people rather than judging it only by appearance. An experienced student might know where to click because they have used the portal for years. A new student might not. A mobile user may face a completely different layout. Testing with more than one kind of user would help reveal problems that I might miss. The goal would not be to make everybody click in exactly the same way, but to make the route to success understandable.

My checklist for a better interface would include four questions:

  • Can users tell what this page is for within a few seconds?
  • Are related instructions and choices grouped together?
  • Can users recognize the next action without memorizing earlier details?
  • Does the system clearly confirm what happened after an important action?

These questions may seem basic, but they address different sources of cognitive load. They connect visual organization with memory support, and they ask the software to take responsibility for explaining its own behavior. They also remind me that good design is something to evaluate with actual tasks, not just with personal taste.

Final Reflection: Make the Work Worth Doing

Before learning these ideas, I might have described a confusing website simply as badly organized. Now I can explain more precisely why it feels difficult. Too many competing elements affect attention. Hidden commands increase the need to remember information. Unclear processes push complexity onto people who only want to finish a task. Cognitive load, Tesler's Law, visual structure, and recognition versus recall give me a practical way to examine those problems.

The biggest lesson I take from this topic is that simplicity is not the absence of complexity. It is the thoughtful handling of complexity. People should spend their mental energy choosing the right class, understanding a security alert, or making an informed decision, not trying to decode the website itself. When design makes those meaningful tasks easier to complete, technology becomes more useful, more accessible, and more respectful of the people who depend on it.

Designing for Flow: How Interface Design Shapes User Experience

Introduction

When people use a digital interface, they usually have a specific goal in mind. They may want to submit an assignment, purchase a product, send a message, or find information. Most users are not interested in understanding how the system was developed. Instead, they want to complete their tasks without unnecessary confusion or interruptions. This is where designing for flow becomes important. A well-designed interface helps users move naturally from one action to another while reducing frustration and maintaining their attention.

Before studying user experience design, I mainly judged websites and applications based on their appearance and functionality. However, I have started to realize that even small design decisions can influence how users think, behave, and make decisions. The placement of a button, the organization of a navigation menu, and the wording of an error message can all affect the overall experience. In this blog, I explore how interface layout, control design, psychological principles, and accessibility contribute to a smoother digital experience.

Understanding Flow in Digital Interfaces

Flow in interface design describes how smoothly a person can progress through a digital task. When an interface supports flow, users spend less time figuring out what to do next and more time accomplishing their actual goals. A good example is an online shopping website that guides customers from selecting a product to reviewing their cart and completing payment.

A poorly designed website may interrupt this process by introducing unnecessary registration requirements, confusing buttons, or unexpected navigation changes. Each interruption forces the user to stop and reconsider their next action. Although these problems may seem small individually, they can become significant when experienced repeatedly.

Johnson (2020) connects interface design with human perception and cognitive psychology. This relationship is important because people have limitations in attention and memory. An interface should support these limitations rather than create additional mental effort. Designers should organize information around users' goals instead of expecting users to understand the technical structure behind a system.

How Interface Layout Influences User Behavior

Interface layout determines how information and interactive elements are arranged on a screen. A clear layout helps users recognize important information, understand relationships between elements, and identify available actions. When headings, buttons, and navigation menus are organized consistently, users can develop expectations about how the system works.

For example, an educational website should make assignments, grades, course materials, and submission options easy to locate. If these features are scattered across unrelated menus, students may spend more time searching for information than completing their academic work.

Visual hierarchy is particularly important because not every element deserves the same level of attention. Headings should identify major sections, while buttons should indicate available actions. Spacing can separate unrelated information and connect related content. Johnson (2020) discusses how human perception influences the interpretation of visual information. Applying this understanding allows designers to create layouts that communicate meaning without requiring lengthy explanations.

However, simplifying an interface does not necessarily mean removing as much information as possible. Some users need detailed instructions, especially when completing unfamiliar tasks. I believe the better approach is to present essential information first while keeping additional information available when needed. This creates a balance between simplicity and functionality.

Control Design and User Decisions

Interactive controls such as buttons, links, checkboxes, and input fields influence how users move through an interface. A control should communicate its purpose before the user interacts with it. For example, a button labeled Submit Assignment provides more useful information than a button labeled Continue when the action involves submitting academic work.

Control placement also matters. When important buttons are located far away from related content, users may overlook them. Similarly, placing a destructive action directly beside a frequently used action can increase the possibility of accidental mistakes.

Nielsen (1994) identifies principles such as error prevention, system feedback, and user control as important considerations in usability design. These principles can be applied to everyday interfaces by providing confirmation messages, clearly identifying available actions, and allowing users to recover from mistakes.

In my opinion, good control design should reduce uncertainty rather than simply provide access to features. Users should understand what will happen when they select a button, whether their action was successful, and what options are available afterward. Clear controls create confidence and encourage users to continue interacting with a system.

The Aesthetic-Usability Effect

The aesthetic-usability effect explains how visually appealing interfaces can influence people's perceptions of usability. Yablonski (2024) discusses the tendency for users to perceive attractive designs as easier to use. Colors, typography, spacing, and visual consistency can contribute to a positive first impression.

Consider two websites offering the same service. One uses inconsistent fonts, crowded text, and poorly aligned buttons. The other presents organized content, readable typography, and a consistent color scheme. Even before interacting with either website, users may feel more comfortable using the second option.

Nevertheless, visual attractiveness cannot solve every usability problem. A website may look professional while containing confusing navigation, broken links, or inaccessible controls. In such cases, the attractive appearance may temporarily hide problems without addressing their underlying causes.

I think designers should treat aesthetics as a way to support functionality rather than replace it. Visual design should communicate structure, establish trust, and make information easier to understand. A successful interface needs to look appropriate for its purpose while allowing people to accomplish their goals effectively.

The Von Restorff Effect

The Von Restorff effect, also known as the isolation effect, describes how distinctive elements are more likely to attract attention and be remembered than surrounding elements (Yablonski, 2024). In interface design, this principle can help users identify important controls and information.

For example, an assignment submission page may contain several options, including viewing instructions, reviewing attached files, and submitting work. Giving the submission button a distinctive appearance helps communicate which action is most important at that stage of the process.

However, this principle becomes less effective when every element is designed to stand out. If several buttons use bright colors, large fonts, and bold borders, users may struggle to determine which action deserves their attention. Emphasis works best when it is used selectively.

There is also an ethical consideration. Designers should not use visual emphasis to pressure users into making decisions that benefit a business at the expense of the user. For example, emphasizing a paid subscription while making the cancellation option difficult to locate may manipulate behavior instead of improving the experience.

For me, the most valuable lesson from this principle is that visual attention should be directed toward meaningful actions. The goal should be to help users make informed decisions rather than simply encourage them to click a particular button.

Postel's Law and Flexible Interaction

Postel's Law encourages flexibility when accepting user input while maintaining consistency in system behavior. Yablonski (2024) applies this idea to user experience design, where interfaces can accommodate reasonable differences in how people enter information.

For instance, people may enter a telephone number using spaces, parentheses, or hyphens. A flexible form can recognize these different formats and convert them into a consistent format when appropriate. This prevents users from having to correct information that is already understandable.

Another example involves searching for information. A search feature that recognizes minor spelling mistakes can help users find relevant results without requiring them to enter an exact phrase. This is especially useful when users are unfamiliar with technical terminology or are communicating in a second language.

At the same time, flexibility should not mean accepting every possible input without validation. As an information technology student interested in cybersecurity, I recognize that input validation is necessary for protecting systems and maintaining reliable information. An application can accept different legitimate formats while rejecting invalid or potentially harmful input.

This connection between usability and security is particularly interesting to me. Security controls should protect the system without introducing unnecessary barriers for legitimate users. A well-designed interface can provide helpful instructions when an input is rejected instead of displaying a vague error message.

Personal Experience with GitHub and Canvas

One experience that helped me understand interface logic involved using GitHub Desktop for academic assignments. As someone still developing experience with version control, I initially found several parts of the workflow confusing. Terms such as commit, branch, fetch origin, push origin, and pull request were not immediately familiar to me.

The challenge was not necessarily that GitHub Desktop lacked functionality. Rather, I needed to understand the relationship between its controls and the overall assignment submission process. Saving an HTML file in Visual Studio Code did not automatically mean that the changes had been uploaded to GitHub. Similarly, committing changes was different from submitting a pull request for instructor review.

During previous assignments, I also experienced uncertainty when multiple blog updates appeared within the same pull request. This made me question whether my latest assignment had been submitted correctly. From a user experience perspective, the situation demonstrates how difficult it can be for beginners to understand the current state of a technical system.

In contrast, I generally find Canvas easier to navigate when submitting regular assignments. Its assignment pages provide instructions, submission options, and information about whether work has been submitted. These features help me understand my progress and determine what I need to do next.

GitHub and Canvas serve different purposes, so it would not be fair to expect them to have identical interfaces. GitHub supports complex development workflows, while Canvas focuses on education. However, I believe technical applications could benefit from optional beginner-friendly guidance that explains the relationship between different stages of a task.

For example, an educational version control interface could provide a progress indicator showing whether changes have been saved, committed, pushed, and submitted for review. This would help beginners understand their current position without removing advanced features needed by experienced developers.

This experience taught me that usability depends partly on the user's knowledge and previous experience. An interface that feels efficient to an experienced developer may feel overwhelming to someone using it for the first time. Designers should consider both groups rather than assuming that every user has the same technical background.

Accessibility as Part of Designing for Flow

Accessibility is another important consideration because users interact with technology in different ways. Some people use keyboards instead of a mouse, while others depend on screen readers, enlarged text, or alternative input devices. An interface that works well for one person may create significant barriers for another.

The World Wide Web Consortium (W3C, 2023) provides accessibility guidelines addressing areas such as contrast, keyboard interaction, and the presentation of digital information. These guidelines demonstrate that usability should extend beyond visual appearance and consider different interaction needs.

For example, a form that communicates errors only through color may be difficult for someone with color vision limitations. Providing a descriptive error message alongside visual indicators makes the information more understandable. Likewise, descriptive alternative text can communicate the purpose of an informative image to someone who cannot see it.

Responsive design is also relevant to accessibility and flow. Users frequently switch between laptops, tablets, and smartphones. If an image or navigation menu extends beyond a mobile screen, users may need to scroll horizontally or zoom repeatedly. These interruptions make completing tasks more difficult.

I believe accessibility should be considered throughout the design process rather than treated as an additional requirement at the end. Many accessible design decisions, including clear labels and readable content, can improve the experience for everyone.

Balancing Efficiency, Flexibility, and Ethics

An important challenge in interface design is finding a balance between efficiency and flexibility. Experienced users may prefer shortcuts and faster interaction methods, while beginners may need more explanation. Providing both options can support different levels of experience without making the interface unnecessarily complicated.

Designers must also consider whether reducing interaction steps is always beneficial. For example, deleting an important document should probably require confirmation, even though confirmation introduces an extra step. Similarly, security-related actions may require additional verification to protect sensitive information.

These situations demonstrate that good flow does not mean eliminating every interruption. Some interruptions serve a meaningful purpose. The important question is whether each additional step helps the user or creates an unnecessary obstacle.

I also believe designers have a responsibility to consider the consequences of their decisions. An interface can influence what people notice, how they respond, and which choices they make. Psychological principles should therefore be applied to support user understanding and independence instead of manipulating people into unwanted decisions.

Applying These Principles to Future Projects

In future academic and professional projects, I would begin by identifying the main goals of the intended users. Understanding these goals would help me determine which information and controls deserve priority. I would also consider whether the intended audience includes beginners, experienced users, or people with different accessibility needs.

After developing an initial design, I would ask people to complete representative tasks without providing detailed instructions. Observing where they hesitate, make mistakes, or ask questions could reveal problems that are not obvious to the designer. Their feedback would help guide improvements to navigation, terminology, and layout.

I would evaluate success based on more than appearance. Useful indicators could include task completion, the number of avoidable errors, and users' confidence in understanding what happened after an action. Comparing these results before and after design changes would provide practical evidence about whether the experience had improved.

As someone preparing for a career in information technology and cybersecurity, I can also apply these ideas to security dashboards, authentication systems, and incident reporting tools. These systems often present complex information, and poor visual organization can make important warnings harder to identify. Clear priorities and meaningful feedback could help professionals respond more effectively while reducing confusion.

Conclusion

Designing for flow involves understanding how users perceive information, make decisions, and interact with digital systems. Interface layout and control design influence whether users can complete tasks confidently or become frustrated along the way. The aesthetic-usability effect, Von Restorff effect, and Postel's Law offer valuable perspectives on how visual appeal, selective emphasis, and flexible interaction can improve digital experiences.

My experiences with GitHub and Canvas have helped me recognize that a functional system is not necessarily an intuitive system. Users need clear guidance, meaningful feedback, and opportunities to recover from mistakes. Accessibility and ethical considerations are equally important because designers should support different users without manipulating their decisions.

Overall, I believe the most effective digital interfaces are those that allow people to focus on their goals rather than the technology itself. By combining psychological principles with thoughtful design decisions, developers and designers can create experiences that are more understandable, accessible, and efficient.

Interaction That Matters: Designing Meaningful and Ethical User Experiences

By Sakib Hasan | Module 3.2

Introduction

Every time we interact with a digital platform, we expect something to happen in response to our actions. Whether we are submitting an assignment, purchasing a product, checking our email, or accessing an online account, the way a system responds influences our overall experience. A website may have an attractive appearance, but if it responds slowly, provides confusing instructions, or fails to communicate what is happening, users can quickly become frustrated. This demonstrates that effective user experience design involves much more than visual appearance.

Interaction design connects users with digital systems through actions, responses, and feedback. A meaningful interaction should help users accomplish their goals while making them feel confident and respected. Timing, motion, responsiveness, and error prevention all contribute to this process. However, designers also have an ethical responsibility to ensure that these techniques are not used to manipulate users into making decisions that are against their interests.

In this blog post, I explore the relationship between interaction design and user satisfaction through several important UX principles, including the Doherty Threshold, the Peak-End Rule, Jakob's Law, and Hick's Law. I also examine how these principles relate to everyday digital experiences, accessibility, and ethical decision-making.

Timing and the Doherty Threshold

One of the most important aspects of interaction design is how quickly a system responds. When users click a button or select an option, they naturally expect some indication that their action has been recognized. Without this feedback, users may assume that the system is not working correctly.

The Doherty Threshold suggests that maintaining a response time of approximately 400 milliseconds or less can support productivity and uninterrupted interaction between people and computers (Yablonski, 2020b). Although every digital task cannot be completed within this period, designers can provide immediate feedback while longer operations continue in the background.

Nielsen (1993) also explains that different response times affect how users perceive a system. Very short delays can make an interaction feel immediate, while longer delays may interrupt concentration. When a process takes considerable time, a progress indicator can help users understand that the system is still processing their request.

As a university student, I frequently use Canvas to access learning materials and submit assignments. When uploading an assignment, I want to know whether the upload has started and whether it has been completed successfully. If the page remains unchanged after I click the submission button, I may become concerned about whether my work was received. A progress indicator followed by a clear confirmation can reduce that uncertainty.

In my opinion, responsiveness is not simply about making technology faster. It is also about maintaining communication between the system and the user. Even when an operation requires additional time, honest feedback can make the experience more understandable and less stressful.

Feedback and User Confidence

Feedback allows users to understand the consequences of their actions. It can appear through confirmation messages, changes in button appearance, loading indicators, error notifications, or other visual cues. Effective feedback answers an important question: did the system recognize what I just did?

GitHub Desktop provides a useful example. When working on a website project, users need to understand the difference between saving a file, committing changes, and pushing those changes to a remote repository. Each operation serves a different purpose, and clear feedback helps users understand their current progress.

For someone who is still developing technical skills, these indicators are particularly valuable. A beginner may not immediately understand whether a local change has reached GitHub. An interface that clearly communicates the current status can prevent unnecessary confusion and repeated actions.

Designers should also avoid providing misleading feedback. For example, displaying a success message before an operation has actually finished could create serious problems. Users might close a page believing that their information has been saved when the process is still incomplete. Feedback should accurately represent the condition of the system.

Motion and Accessibility

Motion is another important element of interaction design. Animations and transitions can guide attention, communicate changes, and help users understand the relationship between different interface elements. A smooth transition between a menu and its content, for example, can make navigation easier to follow.

However, motion should have a clear purpose. Excessive animation may distract users from important information or make simple tasks unnecessarily complicated. Some people may also experience physical discomfort when exposed to certain types of motion.

The World Wide Web Consortium (W3C, n.d.) explains through its accessibility guidance that nonessential motion triggered by user interaction should be capable of being disabled under its Level AAA criterion. This highlights the importance of respecting different user needs when designing digital experiences.

I believe that designers should use animation to support understanding rather than simply decorate a website. A transition that helps explain a change is useful, but an animation that delays access to information may work against the user's actual goal. Providing reduced-motion options can make an interface more inclusive without removing its essential functionality.

The Peak-End Rule and Emotional Experience

The Peak-End Rule describes how people often remember experiences based on emotionally significant moments and how those experiences conclude (Yablonski, 2020a). This principle is particularly relevant to digital interactions because one frustrating moment or an unclear ending can influence a user's impression of an entire platform.

Consider an online shopping experience. A customer may find a product easily, review its description, and proceed through a well-organized checkout process. However, if an unexpected error appears after payment and the customer cannot determine whether the order was successful, that uncertainty may become the most memorable part of the experience.

On the other hand, a clear confirmation page that displays the order number, payment status, and delivery information can provide a positive ending. It allows customers to leave the website knowing what happened and what they should expect next.

This principle also applies to academic platforms. Completing a difficult assignment can already involve considerable effort. If the submission process ends with clear confirmation, students can move on to their next responsibility with greater confidence. The final interaction should reduce uncertainty rather than introduce additional stress.

Nevertheless, designers should not concentrate only on creating an impressive ending. A positive confirmation screen cannot completely compensate for an inaccessible or misleading experience. The entire interaction should remain respectful and functional.

Jakob's Law and Familiar Interaction Patterns

Jakob's Law suggests that users develop expectations from their experiences with other digital products. Familiar navigation patterns and recognizable interface elements can therefore make a new website easier to understand (Yablonski, 2020c).

For example, users commonly expect a website logo near the top of a page, navigation links in an identifiable area, and buttons that clearly indicate available actions. When these familiar patterns are maintained, users can spend more time accomplishing their tasks instead of learning an unfamiliar interface.

This idea is relevant to my own website development activities. When creating a blog page with HTML and CSS, I can organize the header, navigation menu, article content, and footer in a predictable way. A reader should not need detailed instructions simply to locate the main information.

Familiarity does not mean that every website must look identical. Designers can still introduce creative visual elements while preserving interaction patterns that users already understand. The challenge is to balance innovation with usability.

Hick's Law and Decision-Making

Hick's Law explains that the time required to make a decision generally increases as the number and complexity of available choices increase (Yablonski, 2020d). This principle helps explain why crowded interfaces can sometimes make simple tasks feel difficult.

Imagine opening a website that presents numerous buttons, menus, advertisements, and notifications simultaneously. Even if all of these elements are functional, users may struggle to identify which option is most relevant to their current goal.

Organizing related options into meaningful categories can reduce unnecessary decision-making. A navigation menu with clear labels can be more effective than presenting every available feature at once. Important actions should remain easy to locate without competing with unrelated information.

At the same time, simplifying an interface should not involve hiding important choices. Users still need access to privacy settings, cancellation options, and other controls that protect their interests. Effective simplicity supports informed decisions rather than restricting them.

Error Prevention and Recovery

Even experienced users make mistakes. They may select the wrong option, enter incorrect information, or accidentally attempt to delete an important file. A well-designed system should anticipate common mistakes and help users prevent or recover from them.

Useful error-prevention techniques include validating form inputs, clearly labeling required information, providing confirmation for destructive actions, and allowing users to undo reversible changes. These features help users maintain control over their activities.

In cybersecurity, error prevention is especially important. A security dashboard might contain controls for blocking network traffic, changing permissions, or responding to security alerts. Accidentally selecting the wrong action could affect other users or interrupt legitimate operations.

An effective security interface should clearly explain significant actions before they are executed. However, requiring confirmation for every minor action could create unnecessary delays and encourage users to ignore warnings. Designers should determine the seriousness of each action and apply an appropriate level of friction.

Visual Examples of Meaningful Interaction

Ethical Design and Persuasive Techniques

Interaction design can influence human behavior, which creates both opportunities and responsibilities. Persuasive techniques can encourage beneficial activities, such as enabling multifactor authentication, completing educational tasks, or developing healthier habits. However, similar techniques can also be used to manipulate users.

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC, 2022) discusses deceptive interface practices known as dark patterns. These practices can interfere with informed consumer choices by concealing important information, complicating cancellation processes, or encouraging unnecessary disclosure of personal information.

One example is a subscription service that allows customers to register through a simple process but makes cancellation extremely difficult. Another example is a privacy notification that highlights the acceptance button while making the rejection option difficult to identify. These designs may benefit an organization financially, but they do not necessarily respect the interests of the user.

Ethical interaction design should provide users with understandable information and meaningful choices. Designers should consider whether a person would still make the same decision if all relevant information were presented clearly. If the success of an interface depends on users overlooking important details, its design deserves careful reconsideration.

I believe transparency is particularly important when personal information and cybersecurity are involved. Users should understand why information is being collected, what permissions they are granting, and how their decisions may affect their privacy. Security should protect people without relying on unnecessary pressure or confusion.

Personal Reflection and Practical Application

Studying interaction design has encouraged me to look differently at the digital platforms I use regularly. Previously, it was easy to focus mainly on whether a website looked professional or whether its features worked. I now recognize that the smaller details of interaction can have an equally important influence on how users understand and experience technology.

My experience working with HTML, CSS, Canvas, and GitHub has also helped me understand why clear navigation and feedback matter. When learning a new technical process, uncertainty can become a significant obstacle. A clear instruction, recognizable button, or accurate status message can make a complicated task feel more manageable.

As someone interested in cybersecurity, I see a strong connection between usability and security. A system may offer powerful security features, but those features become less effective if users cannot understand or operate them correctly. Designing understandable authentication processes, meaningful security warnings, and accessible dashboards can help users make safer decisions.

I also recognize that different users may have different expectations. An experienced technical professional may prefer detailed controls, while a beginner may benefit from additional explanations. A person using assistive technology may interact with a website differently from someone using a mouse. Good interaction design should consider these differences instead of assuming that everyone approaches technology in the same way.

When developing future digital projects, I want to pay closer attention to the relationship between user actions and system responses. I would evaluate whether feedback is timely, whether navigation is predictable, whether important choices are visible, and whether unnecessary motion can be avoided. These considerations can improve both usability and trust.

Conclusion

Meaningful interaction design combines technical functionality with an understanding of human behavior. The Doherty Threshold demonstrates the importance of responsive systems, while the Peak-End Rule highlights how significant moments and endings shape user memories. Jakob's Law and Hick's Law further explain the benefits of familiar patterns and manageable choices.

Feedback, motion, and error prevention should work together to help users complete their tasks confidently. Nevertheless, these techniques must be applied ethically. Designers should respect accessibility requirements, protect user autonomy, communicate honestly, and avoid deceptive practices.

Ultimately, I believe a successful digital interaction is one that allows people to accomplish their goals without unnecessary confusion, frustration, or pressure. Technology should adapt to human needs rather than expecting people to adapt to every limitation of a digital system. By combining usability, accessibility, responsiveness, and ethical responsibility, designers can create experiences that are not only efficient but also trustworthy and meaningful.
