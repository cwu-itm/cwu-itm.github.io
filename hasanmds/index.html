Behind the Screen: How Systems Thinking Helps Us Design Better Digital Services

Starting with an everyday problem

Picture a student trying to submit an assignment shortly before the deadline. The assignment is finished, but the student cannot get past the login screen. It is tempting to call this a website problem and leave it at that. However, the website may be working while another service is unavailable. The student's account, internet connection, verification method, and course permissions could all affect what happens next. For the person waiting at the screen, those separate pieces are part of the same experience.

This imagined situation is how I would explain the value of systems thinking. A digital environment is built from connections, and those connections deserve attention when something is designed or changed. Throughout this post, I use a hypothetical college learning platform to explore that idea. My focus is on how a team could understand the whole service, compare possible improvements, and use models to make its decisions clearer.

Looking at how the pieces fit

To me, systems thinking means looking at a problem with enough context to understand how its parts influence one another. Instead of stopping at the first explanation, I would ask what the service is supposed to accomplish, what it depends on, and how a change could affect the people using it. INCOSE describes this perspective as paying attention to the whole system and the relationships among its parts (International Council on Systems Engineering [INCOSE], n.d.-a).

Holt (2021) also explains the importance of understanding elements through their relationships with other elements and systems. I take this to mean that knowing what each component does is only part of the job. For example, an account service and a course platform could each operate correctly but still fail to exchange the information needed for access. In that situation, testing the two components separately would not explain the student's difficulty.

I would begin by deciding what belongs inside the project. For the learning platform example, I would include the login process, account records, course access, support procedures, and the people involved. A student's home connection would be an outside dependency. The college might not control it, but the team would still need to consider how a weak connection affects access. Setting a boundary helps keep the project manageable without pretending that outside conditions do not matter.

Why the first answer may not be the best answer

A more linear way of thinking moves from a problem to a cause and then to a solution. Someone might reason that the platform is slow because the server is too small, so the college needs a larger server. If the evidence supports that explanation, the solution may be reasonable. Simple problems do not always require a complicated investigation. A broken cable, for example, might only need replacement. The concern is assuming a direct explanation before checking what is happening.

For the imagined learning platform, I would want to know when the slowdown occurs and which step takes the longest. Does it happen before the student signs in or after the account has been confirmed? Does it affect everyone or only certain courses? Is the problem worse near an assignment deadline? These questions would help separate possible causes. A larger server might help one part of the process while leaving the actual delay somewhere else.

Another possibility is that user behavior contributes to the slowdown. Suppose the login page gives no sign that it is processing a request. Students might press the button several times, adding more requests. If those requests increase the delay, students may click even more. In this hypothetical feedback loop, a response to the problem makes it worse. A clearer message and a way to prevent repeated submissions might help address the pattern.

I would also look beyond the immediate result of a change. Imagine that a stricter account recovery policy reduces approved requests during its first week. That number alone could make the policy look effective. However, students who cannot recover their accounts might start contacting instructors or support staff instead. The work has moved elsewhere rather than disappeared. I would want to consider successful access, security, and support demand together before deciding whether the change was an improvement.

The human side of a digital system

It is easy to describe a digital environment using only technical terms. However, NASA's explanation of a system includes people, procedures, and processes as well as equipment and software (National Aeronautics and Space Administration [NASA], n.d.). That wider description makes sense for this example. A support worker's decision can affect account access, and a student's interpretation of an error message can affect what the student does next.

Consider a student whose verification phone is lost or broken. A system might have strong login protection but no practical recovery path for that situation. I would want the design team to discuss an alternative before the service is released. That discussion should include how identity is checked, who can authorize a recovery, and what information the student receives. Security and usability both belong in the conversation because access should be protected and legitimate users still need a workable way back in.

The people reviewing the design would probably see different needs. An instructor may care most about whether students can submit work on time. A support worker may notice unclear instructions that lead to repeated calls. A security specialist may identify opportunities for account misuse. I think those differences are useful. Bringing them into the discussion could expose problems that a team focused only on coding might overlook. The goal would be an agreed design, with reasons for its tradeoffs.

Using models to make the discussion concrete

Understanding the connections is one task; explaining them clearly to a team is another. That is where a model-based approach could help. A model represents selected details of a system so people can examine them. INCOSE explains that model-based systems engineering supports requirements, design, analysis, verification, and validation through modeling across the life cycle (INCOSE, n.d.-b). I see its value in giving the team something specific to review and update as decisions develop.

For the college platform, I would start with a model of the main services and the information moving between them. It could show how the browser sends a login request, how the account service checks it, and how the learning platform receives an access decision. The connections would need meaningful labels. A page full of boxes and arrows would not help much if everyone interpreted those arrows differently. Clear labels would make it easier to discuss responsibilities and assumptions.

I would then model a few situations rather than only the successful login. One situation could involve an incorrect password. Another could involve a verification service that stops responding. A third could involve account recovery after a lost phone. For each situation, the team could follow the steps and check what the student sees. This would be a useful way to notice missing messages, unclear recovery options, or responsibilities that have not been assigned.

Models could also help connect requirements with implementation and testing. Suppose a requirement says that account recovery must preserve access to existing courses. I would connect that requirement to the account record, the recovery steps, and the test that checks course access afterward. If the design changes, those links would help the team identify what needs another review. The benefit is keeping the purpose of a feature visible throughout the project instead of losing it once development starts.

Trying an idea before students depend on it

A model-based approach would be especially helpful when a design has several possible outcomes. For example, the college could compare a recovery process that always requires staff approval with one that allows some approved cases to be completed automatically. Before choosing, the team could estimate how many requests arrive and how much staff time each process needs. This would help reveal workload concerns, although it would not settle every question about security or fairness.

A simulation could explore a busy deadline period using those estimates. If requests arrive faster than staff can review them, the model might show a growing queue. The team could then consider changes to staffing, instructions, or the process itself. I would label the assumptions clearly and try more than one scenario. A quiet week and a deadline week could produce different results. Treating one estimate as certain would make the exercise much less useful.

The proposed design would still need checks against real conditions. NASA separates verification, which asks whether requirements are met, from validation, which asks whether the product serves its intended purpose in the intended environment (NASA, n.d.). In this example, verification could check that recovery follows the approved steps. Validation could involve seeing whether legitimate students can complete those steps and regain course access. Following a procedure correctly does not automatically mean the experience meets the user's need.

This distinction changes how I would judge success. I would not be satisfied only because a feature passed its individual checks. I would also want to know whether it works with the surrounding services and whether people can use it under realistic conditions. A recovery option that works during a quiet demonstration might be difficult to use when support demand rises. Testing those conditions would make the team's confidence more meaningful.

Keeping the approach useful

Although I see benefits in modeling, I would be careful about relying on it too much. A model only reflects the information and assumptions included in it. If the team forgets a dependency or leaves an outdated policy in place, its conclusions could be misleading. In the college example, changing the verification method would require reviewing the recovery model too. Someone would need responsibility for keeping those details current.

There is also a practical limit to how much detail a project needs. I would not create an elaborate simulation just to change the wording of a button. I would choose the model based on the decision being made and the consequences of getting it wrong. A change affecting account recovery would deserve closer examination because it could influence access, security, and support work. The time spent modeling should help the team make a better decision.

I would also invite people outside the technical team to review the parts relevant to them. Support workers could point out exceptions missing from the recovery process, while students could explain which messages are confusing. Their feedback would not replace technical analysis, but it could challenge assumptions that seemed reasonable during design. For me, this is a reminder that a model should encourage discussion rather than become something people hesitate to question.

What this means for my approach to IT

As an ITAM student interested in cybersecurity, I want to get better at connecting technical choices with their wider effects. This topic gives me a practical starting point. Before proposing a fix, I would identify the service's purpose, the people it supports, and its major dependencies. Then I would compare possible changes and decide what evidence would show that the chosen approach actually helps.

The imagined login problem shows how much can sit behind a simple screen. Systems thinking helps me look for relationships, feedback, and effects that appear later. A model-based approach helps make those ideas specific enough to examine with others. I would carry both habits into future projects: ask how the pieces work together, and use a clear representation to check the design before people have to depend on it.

Thinking in Life Cycles: What Happens After a Digital System Goes Live?

By Sakib Hasan

A launch date is only one part of the story

When I think about a digital system, my attention usually goes to what I can see: the login screen, the menus, and whether I can finish a task without getting confused. A college student portal is a useful example. Students need to register for classes, check information, and reach important services. However, a portal that looks good on its first day can still become difficult to maintain, expensive to operate, or unsafe to use. Thinking in life cycles changes the question from whether a system works today to whether it can continue meeting people's needs over time.

For me, the most useful lesson is that finishing development does not finish the responsibility. Someone must support users, update software, manage information, and eventually replace the system. This post uses a hypothetical college portal to explore those responsibilities. Comparing linear, iterative, and incremental models helps explain how different development choices affect the same system. My view is that choosing a model should depend on uncertainty, risk, and the usefulness of partial releases, rather than on which approach sounds the most modern.

Understanding the life cycle before choosing a model

Holt (2023, pp. 134–137) makes an important distinction between a life cycle and a life cycle model. The life cycle identifies the stages through which a system evolves. The model describes how those stages are executed, including their sequence. In other words, listing the stages does not automatically tell a team how it should organize the work. Two projects can involve similar stages while following different paths through them.

The textbook identifies conception, development, production, utilization, support, and retirement as system life cycle stages. Applied to a student portal, conception would involve understanding student and staff needs. Development would explore possible solutions, while production would create and test the chosen system. Utilization would involve everyday use, and support would keep the portal operating effectively alongside that use. Retirement would address its eventual removal and replacement. These examples are my application of Holt's framework, rather than a description of a particular university's internal practices.

This wider perspective matters because a development project and the system it produces do not necessarily have the same lifespan. Holt (2023) explains that several projects can exist within a system's life cycle. A portal might have separate projects for registration, authentication, and a later migration. Treating each project as isolated could leave gaps between teams. For example, a team might finish an upgrade without clearly assigning responsibility for the older service that still holds student records.

Linear models: clear steps with limited flexibility

In a linear model, stages follow a defined sequence, with each stage completed before the next begins. Holt (2023, pp. 145–146) describes this approach as suitable for smaller, well-understood projects whose needs are unlikely to change. Its strengths are easy to appreciate. A team can establish responsibilities, review deliverables, and understand what must happen next. For a narrow portal change, such as implementing a clearly specified downloadable form, this structure could provide enough control without unnecessary complexity.

The difficulty appears when the original understanding is incomplete. Imagine a team developing an entire registration portal from an initial requirements document. If students only try the completed system near the end, they might discover that the registration process is confusing on a phone or that error messages do not explain how to fix a problem. Those discoveries could affect decisions already built into several parts of the system. A clear schedule would not make those problems disappear.

I would therefore be cautious about choosing a strictly linear approach for a large portal replacement. College policies, user expectations, and connected services can change during development. The model's simplicity is valuable, but that value depends on having a problem stable enough to justify it. For me, the drawback is delayed learning: the team may make major commitments before it has enough evidence that the proposed experience works for its users.

Iterative models: improving through repeated learning

An iterative model uses repeated passes through stages rather than relying on one pass. Holt (2023, pp. 146–147) explains how shorter iterations make complex work more manageable. A portal team could develop an early version of a registration workflow, evaluate it with users, and revise it. The next iteration might improve navigation, clarify prerequisites, or make the confirmation screen easier to understand. The important feature is the opportunity to revisit decisions using what the team has learned.

NASA's systems engineering guidance also describes iteration as repeating a process on the same product to address discrepancies or variations from requirements (National Aeronautics and Space Administration [NASA], n.d.). This supports a practical interpretation: repeating work should have a purpose. In the portal example, changing a menu because students repeatedly choose the wrong option is a meaningful iteration. Redesigning that menu simply because someone prefers a different appearance would need a stronger justification.

Iteration also creates management challenges. Stakeholders may keep suggesting changes, and the team could spend too much time polishing one function while other necessary work remains unfinished. Feedback can conflict, too. Students might prefer fewer steps, while staff need additional information to process a request. I would address this by giving each iteration a specific question and measurable acceptance criteria. For example, can test users complete registration and correctly identify their final enrollment status? An iteration should produce evidence for a decision, not just another version.

Incremental models: delivering useful parts over time

An incremental model delivers a system through several releases. Holt (2023, pp. 148–149) describes an initial concept stage covering the overall needs, followed by development and production of subsets. For a portal, the first release might provide secure access and basic student information. Later releases could add registration, advising appointments, and additional services. Each increment should provide a usable capability while contributing to the broader system.

The main advantage is that people can receive useful functions before the complete system is finished. A college could introduce one service, provide focused training, and learn about operational problems before expanding. Smaller releases could also make it easier to identify which change caused a new issue. However, these benefits depend on whether the system can be divided into useful parts. Holt (2023) emphasizes that some systems cannot be separated into suitable subsets.

For the hypothetical portal, authentication and authorization would be essential foundations. Releasing access to student records before reliable permission checks were available would be an unacceptable choice. There would also be integration work between releases: a later registration feature would need to use the same student information correctly. My concern is that an attractive release schedule could hide architectural weaknesses. Incremental delivery works best when the team plans shared data, interfaces, and dependencies before promising individual features.

Choosing an approach for the portal

The difference between iteration and increments becomes clearer when I separate improvement from added capability. Revising an existing registration screen after testing is iterative. Introducing an advising appointment feature alongside registration is incremental. A team could do both by delivering the portal in useful increments and improving each increment through repeated evaluation. These approaches can work together, but the terms describe different aspects of the work.

For this portal, I would favor incremental delivery supported by iterative evaluation. Students could begin using a limited set of dependable services while the team developed additional capabilities. Within each release, testing would help refine usability and check assumptions. A linear sequence could still be appropriate for a small, stable task within that larger effort. This is a proposed approach for the example, rather than a claim that one model is universally superior.

I would also use review gates to decide whether a release was ready. Holt (2023, pp. 142–143) explains that gates can lead to moving forward, remaining in a stage, returning to an earlier stage, or cancellation. For the portal, passing a gate should require evidence such as successful permission testing, a workable support plan, and a recovery procedure. Reaching a planned date alone would not demonstrate readiness.

Design and security across the system's life

Life cycle thinking affects design because today's choices become tomorrow's maintenance responsibilities. Suppose the portal uses a custom component that only one developer understands. It might satisfy the immediate deadline, yet leave future staff struggling to diagnose errors. I would want the team to consider documentation, accessible interfaces, replaceable components, and clear ownership from the beginning. These choices might not be visible to students, but they would affect how reliably the portal serves them.

Security provides another reason to look beyond launch. Souppaya et al. (2022) explain that secure development practices need to be integrated into software development life cycle models. Their Secure Software Development Framework aims to reduce vulnerabilities, limit their potential impact, and address their underlying causes. The implication for my example is that choosing an iterative or incremental approach does not automatically make the portal secure. The team still needs deliberate security practices within its chosen model.

In my proposed portal, those practices would include reviewing access rules during design, testing them before release, and assigning responsibility for vulnerabilities discovered later. If a student changes programs or an employee leaves, permissions must continue matching legitimate needs. I would also want a clear process for reporting a suspected problem. A successful launch would be a starting point for these responsibilities, not evidence that the security work had ended.

Sustainability, evolution, and retirement

I understand sustainability here as the ability to keep providing value without creating avoidable environmental, financial, or human burdens. For the portal, that could mean reviewing unnecessary data storage, keeping supported equipment useful where practical, and avoiding features that create more maintenance than benefit. These are design questions to investigate, not automatic environmental savings. For example, keeping old equipment longer would need to be balanced against its reliability, support status, and energy use.

There is a human side as well. A portal that requires students to contact staff for routine tasks may transfer its design problems into support workloads. An update that works only on newer devices could make access harder for some users. I would assess sustainability partly by asking whether people can continue using and supporting the system with the resources actually available. The cheapest initial solution might become expensive if it repeatedly interrupts essential services.

Evolution also requires attention to the relationships between systems. NASA (n.d.) emphasizes that systems include people, procedures, hardware, and software, with value arising from how elements work together. Applied to the portal, replacing one component could affect staff workflows and connected services. I would therefore assess changes across those relationships instead of judging a new component only by its individual features.

Finally, retirement deserves planning before it becomes urgent. Holt (2023) includes safe and secure decommissioning in the life cycle. For the portal, I would plan how to migrate necessary records, verify their accuracy, close obsolete accounts, and remove services that should no longer be accessible. Simply switching off the old website would not resolve every dependency. A retirement plan should also identify who confirms that essential functions have transferred successfully and who handles any remaining records.

What I would take into future IT work

My main takeaway is that a digital system should be judged across its whole period of usefulness. Linear models offer clarity when needs are stable. Iterative models create opportunities to learn and refine. Incremental models deliver useful capabilities over time, provided those capabilities can be separated responsibly. Each approach has tradeoffs, and the team must make those tradeoffs visible.

As I prepare for work in IT and cybersecurity, I want to ask questions that reach beyond immediate functionality: Who will maintain this? How will users get help? What happens when a dependency changes? How will the system be retired? Those questions make life cycle thinking practical. A system succeeds when it can serve people reliably, adapt with purpose, and eventually be replaced without leaving unnecessary problems behind.
