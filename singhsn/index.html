Model-Based Systems Thinking

Systems thinking means looking at a whole system instead of only looking at one small part. In my own words, it means understanding how different parts connect, affect each other, and work together to create an outcome. A system can be a business, a website, a school, a computer network, a transportation company, or even a team of people. The important idea is that the parts do not work alone. They influence each other.

Traditional thinking is often more linear. Linear thinking usually looks at problems as a straight line: one cause leads to one effect. This can be useful for simple problems. For example, if a computer is not turning on because the battery is dead, the solution may be simple. Charge the battery or replace it. But many real-world problems are not that simple. In complex systems, one change can create many effects. Sometimes those effects are not obvious at first.

Systems thinking is different because it asks bigger questions. Instead of only asking, What is broken? it also asks, What is connected to this? and What else will change if we fix this one part?” This matters because many problems in technology, business, and management are connected to more than one cause. A website problem may not only be a design issue. It may also involve the user experience, database, server, security settings, business process, and customer expectations.

This idea connects closely to systems engineering. NASA describes systems engineering as a methodical and multidisciplinary approach used for the design, realization, technical management, operation, and retirement of a system (NASA, 2023). I think this definition is helpful because it shows that systems engineering is not only about building something. It is about managing the entire life of a system. A system has to be planned, designed, tested, used, improved, and eventually replaced.

Model-Based Systems Engineering, also called MBSE, is useful because it gives teams a way to represent complex systems through models. SEBoK explains that MBSE uses formal models to support systems engineering tasks throughout the system life cycle (SEBoK, 2025). Instead of depending only on documents, teams can use models to show the system structure, relationships, requirements, and possible risks. This makes it easier to understand how the parts work together.

A model-based approach is important in complex digital systems because digital systems have many moving parts. A modern digital system might include users, software, hardware, cloud services, databases, cybersecurity rules, networks, and business goals. If a team only focuses on one piece, they might miss how that piece affects the rest of the system. MBSE helps teams see the bigger picture before major problems happen.

For example, think about an online college registration system. A student may only see the website where they search for classes and register. But behind that page, many systems are connected. There may be student records, class capacity, prerequisites, financial aid, advisor holds, payment systems, email notifications, and security permissions. A linear way of thinking might only ask whether the registration button works. A systems thinking approach asks how the entire registration process affects students, advisors, instructors, and the school.

This example shows why models are helpful. A model could show how the registration page connects to the student database, payment system, advisor approval process, and class schedule. If one part changes, the model can help the team understand what else may be affected. For example, changing prerequisite rules may affect registration access, advising, graduation planning, and student support. Without a systems view, a small change could create a bigger issue somewhere else.

Another benefit of model-based systems thinking is communication. Complex projects usually involve people from different backgrounds. A developer, project manager, security analyst, business leader, and user may all think about the system differently. If they only use long documents, they may misunderstand each other. A model gives everyone a shared view of the system. It can help people ask better questions and make better decisions.

This is also important for cybersecurity. Security cannot be added only at the end of a digital project. It has to be included in the full system design. A login screen, password policy, database, network connection, user permissions, employee training, and backup system can all affect security. Carnegie Mellon University’s Software Engineering Institute explains that MBSE can support design assurance for software-intensive systems, including performance, correctness, safety, and cybersecurity concerns (Carnegie Mellon University Software Engineering Institute, n.d.). This shows why MBSE is useful for modern digital systems where security and reliability matter.

A model-based approach can also reduce risk. In many projects, problems are discovered too late. A team may build a system and then realize that a requirement was missing, a process was unclear, or two parts of the system do not work together. With MBSE, teams can find some of these problems earlier because they can see the relationships between requirements, design, and behavior. This can save time and reduce confusion.

However, model-based systems thinking also has challenges. A model is not the same as reality. It is a simplified version of the system. If the model is wrong, outdated, or missing important details, it can mislead the team. A model only helps if people maintain it and use it correctly. If no one updates the model, it can become just as confusing as an old document.

Another challenge is that MBSE can take time to learn. Teams need the right tools, training, and discipline. People also need to agree on how the model will be used. If each person models the system differently, the model may become confusing. This means MBSE is not only a technical tool. It also requires teamwork, communication, and good management.

For me, the biggest value of systems thinking is that it helps prevent narrow thinking. It reminds managers, IT workers, and designers to slow down and look at the whole situation. In business, one decision can affect employees, customers, costs, schedules, and long-term goals. In IT, one technical change can affect performance, security, usability, and support. Systems thinking helps people avoid fixing one issue while creating another problem.

I also think systems thinking is useful in logistics and operations. For example, if a transportation company wants to improve delivery speed, it cannot only look at truck routes. It also has to consider drivers, dispatching, fuel costs, customer deadlines, maintenance, weather, traffic, and communication. A model-based approach could help show how those parts connect. This can lead to better decisions than simply changing one part without understanding the full system.

In conclusion, systems thinking is a way to understand the whole picture. It differs from linear thinking because it focuses on relationships, feedback, and long-term effects instead of only one cause and one result. Model-Based Systems Engineering is useful because it helps teams represent complex systems, communicate clearly, manage requirements, reduce risk, and design better digital systems. Modern digital systems are too connected to understand one piece at a time. A good system is not just a group of working parts. It is a connected environment where the parts support each other and serve the larger purpose.

Module 1.2 Blog Post

A Digital System Is Never Truly Finished: Understanding the Systems Development Life Cycle

When thinking about creating a digital system, the first things that come to mind are coding, designing and testing. However, there is significantly more involved in creating a successful system. After deployment, a system will need to be maintained, updated, made secure and eventually replaced.

This is where the Systems Development Life Cycle (SDLC) comes into play. The SDLC helps us to think about the whole system Life Cycle. By doing this, we have a structured approach to designing, developing, deploying, maintaining and replacing our systems. In ‘Systems Engineering Demystified’, Holt (2023) discusses the importance of understanding the Life Cycle of a system and the different models that can be used to determine how each stage can be performed. There are 3 models; the linear, iterative and incremental Life Cycle Models. Each has its own pros and cons.

What I found interesting was how none of these models are necessarily the best to use. It will depend on what you are creating, how well understood the requirements are and how much change is expected.

Understanding the Systems Development Life Cycle

The Systems Development Life Cycle is how a system evolves throughout its life. It helps engineers and developers to organise their work, set goals, manage resources and ensure the system is achieving its purpose.

There are 6 main stages in the systems Life Cycle (Holt, 2023). These include conception, development, production, use, support and replacement. Conception involves identifying the problem that needs solving, and setting the requirements. Development involves researching solutions and deciding which is the best option. Production involves creating and testing the system to ensure it meets its requirements.

Once deployed, the system will be used. This will involve people using the system for its intended purpose. Support will involve maintaining the system, fixing any issues and ensuring it is running properly. Finally, the system will be replaced.

Something I noticed from the reading was how there is a difference between a Life Cycle and Life Cycle Model. A Life Cycle is the stages that will be involved in evolving the system, whereas a Life Cycle Model will determine how each stage will be performed (Holt, 2023).

Linear Life Cycle Model

The linear model is likely the easiest to understand, often referred to as the Waterfall Model. The development will follow a set order of stages.

For example, if a company wanted to create an internal application to automate a repetitive task, and the requirements were well understood and unlikely to change, the developers would identify the requirements, design, implement and test the application. The biggest pro of this model is how simple it is. Everyone knows what needs to be done, when and what needs to be completed before moving on.

Linear models are great for smaller projects where the requirements are well understood and using established technologies (Holt, 2023). As each stage is well defined and decisions are made, it can be easy to track progress. This can be useful if you have a set budget, limited resources and lots of documentation. It can also make it easier to assign responsibilities as you know what is expected.

However, this can also be the biggest con. The model assumes that the requirements will be well understood at the start of development. This may not always be the case when creating a complex digital system. If users realise halfway through that an important feature is missing, or the technology chosen isn’t suitable, it can be expensive to go back and change previous decisions. Issues may not be identified until testing or deployment. Overall, I feel this model would make the most sense if there was little uncertainty. There is little reason to make the project more complex if the requirements are stable and the solution is well understood.

However, I would be wary of using a purely linear model for a large application that is expected to evolve. If you assume that very little will change during development it doesn’t seem like the best approach.

Iterative Life Cycle Model

Unlike the linear model, the iterative model involves going through the development stages multiple times to improve the system.

Rather than trying to create the perfect solution in one go, the developers will create an initial version of the system. They will then assess how it is performing, receive feedback and use this to improve future versions. Iterations are smaller life cycles that will create a working version of the system (Holt, 2023). Generally each version will be improved from the previous. This means development can continue. For example, if a university wanted to create a new online student portal. The first version would contain the core functionality that students need to sign up to classes.

After students have started using the system, the developers may realise that the interface is confusing or some pages aren’t performing well during sign ups. This can be used to improve the next development cycle. This can be a good pro as the developers can learn how the system is being used. This can be really useful as often users won’t know exactly what they want until they have something to interact with. Something that looks intuitive to the developers may not be to someone using it for the first time.

Another pro is the risk can be managed by releasing smaller versions. If there are major issues with a new update, the developers may be able to roll back to a previous stable version.

However, this can cause issues, as there will be frequent changes to the project it can be difficult to manage. Stakeholders may also keep changing their expectations.

There is also a temptation to rush the updates. It can be risky to focus on getting the updates out quickly rather than ensuring they are working (Holt, 2023). Managing changing stakeholder needs is also important. This makes me think, how much flexibility is too much? Whilst it can be useful to change the requirements, if the team are constantly changing their goals it can be difficult to make any real progress. Whilst using an iterative approach, there should still be a clear overall goal. I feel this would work best if the team use the feedback as evidence to make decisions.

Incremental Life Cycle Model

This model is similar to the iterative model as there are multiple development cycles and releases. However there is a key difference. Iterative development focuses on improving the working system, whereas incremental development focuses on adding further functionality.

Incremental development starts with an understanding of what the system needs (Holt, 2023). Development and production will focus on different parts of the requirements meaning sections of the final system can be released. For example, if a company wanted to create a new package tracking platform. The first increment may allow users to enter a tracking number and see the current status of their package. The second increment may include notifications and the third estimated arrival times and interactive maps. Further increments may include account management, reporting and more advanced features. Each release will add something new to the system.

The biggest pro of this model is that the system can start providing value. This means that users don’t have to wait months/years for all of the features to be completed. This can also help organisations prioritise what they want to develop. Core features can be released first and less important features can come later. However, this model can be tricky to plan. If the different components are dependent on each other.

In the package tracking example, the notifications depend on the system having reliable data on the packages. If the components are developed in the wrong order there can be issues integrating them or lots of changes needed. Another con is that the system may not provide a satisfactory experience to all users. Some systems can’t really be split into independent parts.

For example, if the system is safety critical it may need several integrated components to function safely. Only releasing a small section may not be practical or safe.

Overall I feel the main pro of this model is that useful functionality can be released early. However this can only be done if the increments are carefully chosen. If the system is incomplete it may not provide any real value.

Choosing a Model

After looking at the different models, I don’t feel that they are 3 different philosophies and one will always be better. In my opinion, each model will help with a different issue. The linear model will focus on predictability. The iterative will focus on learning and the incremental will focus on providing useful functionality.

In practice, a development team may want to use a mixture of the models. For example, using incremental development to add new features, but using iterative development to improve them. Meanwhile, some components may need to be more planned and verified.

The Guide to the Systems Engineering Body of Knowledge recognise that stages of the Life Cycle can be repeated or run in parallel (Yokell et al, 2026). This shows that development can often be a lot more complex than shown in a diagram.

Another useful concept from Holt (2023) was decision gates between each stage of the Life Cycle. This will help decide whether to continue with the project, stay in the current stage, go back to a previous stage or potentially cancel the project.

I feel this is a useful concept to use regardless of the model. Even if you are using an iterative model with frequent releases, there should be clear criteria to decide whether the update is ready.

A flexible development process shouldn’t mean there isn’t any accountability.

Why Thinking About the Whole Life Cycle Matters

Perhaps the most important thing I have learnt from looking at Life Cycle Models is that developing a digital system isn’t the same as maintaining it.

A system may work perfectly when it is first deployed, however this doesn’t mean it will still be useful, secure or efficient 5 years later.

Technology changes, organisations change and users will have new expectations. Developers will need to consider these changes.

Designing with the Future in Mind

One of the biggest pros of considering the Life Cycle is that it will encourage developers to think about long term requirements. If the system is expected to grow, it should be designed to be maintainable and scalable. Decisions on the architecture, data storage, documentation and interfaces can have long term impacts.

A quick and cheap design may become extremely expensive to maintain.

This ties into technical debt. The Guide to the Systems Engineering Body of Knowledge recognise that technical debt can build up throughout the Life Cycle of a system and can have a negative impact on the suitability of the design (Yokell et al, 2026).

This is a useful way to look at engineering decisions. Rather than just thinking about whether it will work, you should think about how easy it will be to maintain, alter or replace.

Security and Reliability

Another reason development can’t be finished once deployed is due to security. A digital system may be secure when first deployed, but as new threats are discovered it may become vulnerable. Dependencies in the software may have vulnerabilities, standards may change and attackers may find weaknesses.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology recognise this issue. They mention that security practices should be incorporated into software development Life Cycle Models as they may not consider security (Souppaya et al, 2022).

This is an important point to consider. Just because you are using an iterative or incremental model doesn’t mean your system will be secure. You need to actively include security.

The same can be said for reliability. Whilst testing before deployment is important, it is also important to monitor and maintain the system. A system that is working correctly today may stop working tomorrow due to changes to external services, increased demand or unexpected interactions with other components.

Sustainability

Another thing to consider when thinking about the Life Cycle is sustainability. Whilst digital systems may not seem to have a large impact, they do require servers, networking, storage and more. All of these require electricity and physical resources.

A system that isn’t designed efficiently may use more computing resources throughout its life. This can be reduced by optimising the software, reducing unnecessary data storage and choosing scalable infrastructure.

From an economic perspective, an inefficient system can become expensive to maintain. If there are too many infrastructure requirements, frequent failures and difficult maintenance processes.

To me, sustainability means designing a system that will continue to achieve its purpose without costing unreasonable amounts of money, energy or maintenance. This doesn’t necessarily mean designing it to be the cheapest option. Sometimes investing more effort into a well designed architecture can reduce costs throughout the life of the system.

Planning for Replacement

One thing I feel can be easily overlooked is replacement. When developing software we want it to be useful for as long as possible. However no system can be expected to run forever. Eventually it will become outdated and need to be replaced.

Holt (2023) discusses how the Life Cycle of a system will interact with the Life Cycle of the technologies it relies on. Even though the system may be running for many years, the underlying technologies may become outdated.

This can cause an issue. How can we maintain the system if the technologies aren’t widely used anymore?

Developers should think about whether the data can be moved to a new system, whether components can be upgraded individually and how users can move onto new technologies.

Replacement can also cause issues with security and privacy. Sensitive information can’t just be left behind. The organisation may need to securely delete information, keep the required information and ensure users don’t lose important services.

Whilst this may seem like an early consideration to make when designing, I feel it is an important part of responsible engineering. If a system is easy to create but almost impossible to replace it can become a long term liability.

Final Thoughts

Overall, after looking at the Systems Development Life Cycle and models mentioned in ‘Systems Engineering Demystified’, the main thing I have learnt is that successful systems engineering requires looking beyond just the development. Each of the models can be useful for organising your work, however none will guarantee success. The requirements, complexity, uncertainty and long term goals of your project should influence which model you choose.

I also feel it is important to remember that flexibility and structure don’t have to be mutually exclusive. You can be flexible with your requirements but still have clear goals, documentation, testing and quality expectations.

Most importantly, the success of your system shouldn’t just be based on whether it works when first deployed. It should also be based on whether it will continue to be useful, secure, maintainable and sustainable.

Ultimately, a digital system isn’t a finished product. It will continue to evolve as the technology, users and environment change. By understanding the full Life Cycle, engineers can make better decisions not just for the system they are creating but also for those who will rely on it.
