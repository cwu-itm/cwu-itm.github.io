Leaked from KarinaOS™: The System Is Working Exactly as Designed. Unfortunately...

KARINAOS™ INTERNAL REVIEW

Subject: Getting coursework done

Status: Leaked

First recommendation: "Try harder."

Engineering response: "We already tried that update. What, exactly, are we changing?"

Being a student sounds easy when you leave out the student.

Receive assignment. Do assignment. Submit assignment.

Three steps. Beautiful. Practically a brochure.

Then I enter the process.

I need to understand the instructions, find the reading, connect the ideas, decide where to start, and remember which website wants which submission. Other responsibilities compete for the same hours. My energy does not arrive in equal amounts every day. Somewhere in there, I also need to eat.

So I build systems: schedules, assignment maps, checklists, and places to store information. There is even a Karina Workstation user manual. Apparently, once you give enough technology a personality, you eventually become the technology.

I call this KarinaOS™.

Underneath the dramatic branding is a real design question: how can I spend less time figuring out how to do the work and more time actually doing it?

A Pile of Useful Things Still Needs a Plan

Systems thinking means looking at how connected parts affect each other and create a result. It also means asking how that result changes what happens next.

Instead of blaming the first thing that looks broken, I can ask: What is this system supposed to do? What parts contribute to that outcome? How do they interact? What else affects them?

Holt (2023) explains that understanding the relationships between system elements matters as much as understanding the elements themselves. An interface is where information or material passes between those elements (p. 6).

That sounds technical, but the idea becomes easier to see when I treat KarinaOS™ as a model.

The manual represents processes such as reasoning, curiosity, sensory processing, physical demands, background thoughts, and executive resources as competing processes inside a very questionable Task Manager. Obviously, my brain does not literally run CuriosityService.exe. The joke works because the model represents something real: several conditions can interact while I am trying to accomplish one apparently simple task.

For this example, the system I am examining is my coursework process. Its purpose includes learning and submitting appropriate work. Canvas, GitHub, course materials, schedules, and other tools interact with that process, even when I draw some of them outside the system boundary.

A system's boundary defines what is included in the study. Holt (2023) notes that different people may draw that boundary differently (pp. 8–10). That choice matters. If I model coursework as nothing more than “student receives assignment and student completes assignment,” I have created a very tidy model.

When the Simple Model Stops Working

At the beginning of this post, I reduced coursework to three steps: receive assignment, do assignment, submit assignment. Linear thinking follows that same kind of straight path: A leads to B, then B leads to C.

That is not inherently bad. Clear procedures often need a linear sequence. Fetch the repository, edit the file, preview it, commit it, and push it. Those steps have an order for a reason.

The problem comes when I assume that the ordered steps explain the whole system.

The KarinaOS manual contains an excellent example.

According to the incident report, the system possesses:

  • a laptop,
  • the assignment,
  • the required reading,
  • knowledge of the deadline,
  • sufficient intelligence,
  • and a genuine desire to complete the task.

And yet TaskInitiation.exe may still fail.

Cause: ¯\_(ツ)_/¯

The joke exposes a problem with the simple model. If possessing the necessary resources and wanting to complete the assignment were enough to reliably produce task initiation, the operation should succeed.

It does not always succeed.

That means the model needs more information.

Suppose I put off an assignment because I have not found a direction yet. Waiting leaves less time to explore the topic. Less exploration produces less clarity. Less clarity makes starting feel harder, so I wait longer.

The result has now fed back into the original problem.

Holt (2023) identifies unmanaged complexity, failed communication, and poor understanding as connected causes of system failure. Each can contribute to the others (p. 14). “Started late” describes an outcome. Systems thinking asks what interactions helped produce it.

That question is much more useful than “Why can't I just do the thing?”

Change the System, Not Just the Lecture

Once I look at the problem as a system, I can experiment with changing its conditions. That is where my Seed → Anchor → Burst strategy comes from.

The Seed is a short first visit to the assignment. I might create the document, copy the prompt, write three messy thoughts, or highlight something confusing. It takes about ten to fifteen minutes.

The Anchor is a midweek check. Does the document exist? Does it contain an outline, headings, sources, or some visible direction? If not, I create something tangible.

The Burst is where I follow the momentum when the ideas begin connecting.

The goal is not to force myself to produce a polished introduction on command. The goal is to change the starting conditions. Instead of expecting “begin assignment” to mean “understand everything and start writing the final product,” I give the thinking somewhere to land.

STARTUP INSTRUCTIONS:
Open document. Add one useful thing.
Complete understanding may install later.

Linear thinking still has a role here. Seed, Anchor, and Burst provide a path through the work. Systems thinking helps me understand why changing that path might change the larger outcome.

The Same System Does Not Always Need the Same Interface

KarinaOS also changes state.

The manual documents one failure mode as EXECUTIVE_BUFFER_OVERFLOW.

When resources are available, I might ask:

“Explain the architecture.”

As those resources disappear:

“Okay, what does that mean?”

“Which one?”

“Where?”

Eventually:

“Can you just tell me?”

The underlying person has not been replaced halfway through the conversation. The state of the system has changed.

That matters when designing an interface.

A detailed explanation can be useful when I have the resources to process it. During overload, that same explanation can add another problem to solve. The better interface might provide one concrete next action.

That idea scales beyond KarinaOS. A digital environment should not merely contain all necessary information. Designers also need to consider how people encounter it and what they must do with it.

This is one reason I appreciated seeing the design of this course itself. In the course walkthrough, Bjorge (n.d.) explains that information in a previous version of the course interface could become lost during scrolling. The revised design breaks material into shorter pages, tabs, and smaller assignment sections. She even encounters and fixes a navigation issue during the walkthrough. Holt (2023) distinguishes essential complexity, which belongs to the system, from accidental complexity, which can result from inefficient people, processes, or tools (p. 15). Understanding systems engineering requires effort. Hunting around an interface for the assignment instructions adds a different kind of effort.

A designer can study a complicated system and still give the user a simple path through it.

KarinaOS Needs an Environment

This is also how I am approaching The Cybernetic Desk Workspace, my SharePoint environment for organizing coursework.

The design question is not simply, “Where can I store my syllabi?”

It is: How does information become useful work?

How does a syllabus become deadlines? How does a deadline become preparation? How does an assignment become a Seed task, an Anchor checkpoint, and eventually a submission?

Lifestack gives me another view of that environment by moving assignments into actual blocks of time.

Giving this blog post three hours means those hours are no longer available for something else. The calendar makes that relationship visible so I can inspect and adjust it.

More features, however, do not automatically make the system better.

CHANGE REQUEST: Add more reminders.
Expected result: Nothing gets forgotten.
Possible result: User now needs a reminder to check the reminders.

NASA's systems engineering guidance emphasizes evaluating the whole design and balancing competing needs rather than optimizing one part at the expense of another (National Aeronautics and Space Administration [NASA], 2016). A reminder can function perfectly while making the overall experience worse. A dashboard can display every assignment while still failing to answer, “What should I do next?”

For my workspace, better questions are: Does this reduce repeated planning? Can I find the next action? Can I trust the deadline? How much effort does maintaining this feature require?

The icons may be beautiful. They still need to earn their place.

KarinaOS Is Not Karina

This is where model-based systems engineering, or MBSE, becomes especially interesting.

The International Council on Systems Engineering (INCOSE, n.d.) describes MBSE as the formalized application of modeling to support activities such as requirements, design, analysis, verification, and validation throughout a system's life cycle. Holt (2023) explains that in a traditional document-based approach, documents contain information about the system. In MBSE, a model holds relevant information and represents how it fits together (p. 33). KarinaOS gives me an unexpectedly useful way to think about this distinction because KarinaOS is not actually me.

It is a model.

The manual selects observable behaviors, recurring interactions, resources, constraints, and failure states and represents them using a ridiculous computer metaphor. It deliberately leaves things out.

That does not automatically make KarinaOS an MBSE model. My personal documentation does not suddenly become a formal engineering methodology because I named something TaskInitiation.exe. Holt (2023) also emphasizes that SysML and MBSE are not the same thing (p. 55). A model needs a defined method for creating, checking, changing, and using it.

But the manual helps me understand why models are useful.

A useful model does not need to reproduce every detail of reality. Holt (2023) argues that a model should contain enough useful information to help develop a successful system rather than simply collecting everything possible (p. 33).

The KarinaOS manual even contains a section called Things This Manual Does Not Establish. It cannot prove whether every behavior comes from autism, ADHD, physical state, stress, environment, personality, or some interaction among them.

The model is incomplete.

So is every useful model.

Its purpose is not to become the human. Its purpose is to give me something I can inspect.

The System Is Working Exactly as Designed

Systems thinking has changed the question I ask.

Instead of:

“Why can't I just do the thing?”

I can ask:

“What relationships are producing this outcome?”

Where is information getting lost? Where is unnecessary friction being introduced? What changes when resources decrease? Which parts actually need to be connected? Where would a small intervention change what happens next?

That does not mean every difficulty can be engineered away. It means I have a better framework for deciding what is worth redesigning.

The final observation in the Karina Workstation manual says:

“When the default interface doesn't work reliably, Karina builds another interface.”

That may be the most useful thing I have learned from accidentally becoming the operating system.

Schedules, assignment maps, Seed checkpoints, external storage, Lifestack, and The Cybernetic Desk are not attempts to create the world's most complicated planner. At least, they are not supposed to be.

They are attempts to design an environment that interacts better with the system using it.

The goal is not:

Try harder.

The better engineering question is:

What, exactly, are we changing?

KARINAOS™ REVIEW CLOSED

Approved goal: Less repeated planning. More actual work.

Next action: Seed the assignment.

Unnecessary new features: Under observation.

Module 1.2 · Thinking in Life Cycles

KarinaOS™ 2.0: You Can't Patch Your Way Out of a Bad Life Cycle

KARINAOS™ INTERNAL REVIEW

Subject: Automating assignment management

Goal: Spend less time managing homework.

Current activity: Troubleshooting the system that manages homework.

Recommendation: Review the development process before installing another motivational update.

My first post introduced KarinaOS™, the unofficial operating system I use to organize coursework, responsibilities, and the limited resources of being a person. One of its projects is the Cybernetic Desk, a digital workspace for school, projects, and resources. Within that workspace, the Signal Board is a SharePoint list where I track assignments, deadlines, priorities, and the support I need to get started.

The Signal Board provided a place to manage assignments, but entering them individually created another administrative task. I already had an Excel workbook called Class Assignment WBS, so I decided to use it for bulk intake. A Power Automate flow would read the spreadsheet, create the appropriate SharePoint items, and record which assignments had been imported.

Failed tests, date errors, empty rows, and partially completed operations turned a convenience project into a practical lesson in systems development. They also revealed why a useful system needs more than successful patches.

A system’s life extends beyond its first successful run

The systems development life cycle, or SDLC, organizes how a system moves from an identified need through development, use, support, and eventual replacement or retirement. Common activities include planning, requirements analysis, design, implementation, testing, deployment, and maintenance. Different frameworks organize these activities differently, but each addresses an important part of development.

Planning establishes purpose and feasibility. Requirements identify stakeholder needs and constraints. Design determines how components will interact, while implementation creates or configures them. Testing evaluates the result, deployment introduces it into use, and maintenance addresses defects and changing conditions. Retirement may involve migrating information, preserving records, or securely disposing of obsolete components.

Holt (2023) distinguishes a life cycle, which identifies stages in an entity’s evolution, from a life cycle model, which describes how those stages are executed (p. 144). Identifying development activities does not establish how work will move between them. Linear, iterative, and incremental models provide different ways to organize that progression.

For my workflow, success means transferring assignment information accurately, handling ordinary inputs, avoiding unnecessary duplication, and remaining manageable. A green checkmark provides evidence about execution. It does not establish that the system fulfills its purpose.

Linear development: the limits of an orderly plan

A linear model executes stages in a defined sequence, with each stage following completion of the previous one. Holt (2023) identifies its simplicity, clear stage gates, and suitability for small projects with stable needs and well-understood technologies. Its limitations become more significant when needs change or functionality cannot wait until the final release (pp. 145–146).

My initial plan followed a clear sequence: read the Excel rows, determine whether each assignment had already been imported, create a SharePoint item when appropriate, and update the original Excel row. The SharePoint item had to exist before its ID could be recorded in Excel.

However, an orderly plan can contain untested assumptions. I treated the spreadsheet as a collection of assignments ready for processing. Its table contained eighteen rows, most of which were empty placeholders, and the automation attempted to process them.

KARINAOS™ DATA QUALITY REPORT:

Available rows: Eighteen.

Available assignments: Fewer than advertised.

Original assumption: Withdrawn.

Other failures involved SharePoint field values and date conversion. The sequence made sense, but the information passed between components did not always satisfy the receiving component’s expectations.

This experience illustrates a risk of delaying feedback: later activities depend on earlier assumptions. Small tests could expose those assumptions before additional work accumulated around them. Linear planning remained useful, but its foundations needed checking.

Iterative development: learning through revision

An iterative model makes repeated passes through the life cycle stages. Holt (2023) describes each iteration as producing a working version that can be deployed, with later versions becoming more complete or improved. Benefits include progressive refinement and the possibility of returning to a previous working release. Drawbacks include changing stakeholder needs and pressure to release before a version works adequately (pp. 146–147).

My troubleshooting used iterative refinement within implementation and testing. Initially, I had difficulty configuring Excel’s “Update a row” action. The automation needed to identify the correct source row after creating its corresponding SharePoint item. Introducing a unique Import Key and mapping the SharePoint ID back into Excel addressed that problem, but others remained.

These debugging cycles illustrate iterative thinking, although they were not all complete, deployable iterations of the entire life cycle. NASA’s handbook similarly describes iteration at the process level as applying processes again to the same product to address discrepancies or variations from requirements (National Aeronautics and Space Administration [NASA], 2016).

Iteration made actual behavior available for evaluation. However, repeated changes became easier to manage when each had a specific objective: skip an empty row, identify the correct source record, or transfer a valid assignment. Without those questions, troubleshooting risked becoming a sequence of changes whose effects were difficult to distinguish.

Empty rows exposed incomplete requirements

The placeholder rows required a more precise definition of an eligible assignment. Before importing a row, the flow needed to establish that the assignment had not already been imported and that its Task Name contained meaningful content.

An apparently empty cell could contain a null value, an empty string, or whitespace. The condition needed to account for those possibilities instead of relying on how the spreadsheet looked.

KARINAOS™ REQUIREMENTS UPDATE:

An assignment must exist before it can be imported.

Implementation requests that this clarification be recorded without commentary.

I also reorganized the flow so that actions were placed in the correct conditional branch. Building the workflow revealed cases that the original description overlooked.

“Import assignments” became a more useful requirement when it included valid inputs, eligibility rules, and expected outcomes.

Incremental development: expanding useful capability

In Holt’s incremental model, the initial concept stage considers the overall needs, while subsequent development delivers subsets as usable partial solutions. Some functionality can therefore become available before the whole system is complete. However, the approach is unsuitable when a system cannot be divided into useful subsets (Holt, 2023, pp. 148–149).

The Cybernetic Desk illustrates the value of delivering capabilities in portions. The Signal Board provides assignment tracking, its existing connection to Google Tasks supports downstream planning in Lifestack, and bulk intake from Excel adds another capability. This resembles incremental development, although my workspace has evolved through discovery rather than from a completely specified initial plan.

A practical distinction is that iteration revisits and improves a version, while an increment delivers another portion of the intended capability. The approaches overlap: an increment can undergo several iterations, and an iterative release can add functionality.

Incremental development lets me improve intake without rebuilding the entire workspace. However, each addition still requires coordination. New capabilities can conflict with existing assumptions, duplicate information, or increase maintenance demands.

Partial success revealed an interface and recovery problem

One failed run created an assignment in SharePoint even though the overall flow reported failure.

Holt (2023) explains that system elements interact through interfaces supporting the exchange of flows or services (pp. 106–108). In my workflow, field mappings, identifiers, and date representations determine whether information moves meaningfully between Excel and SharePoint. Problems at those connections could affect the overall result even when individual actions succeeded.

Creating the destination item and updating the source row were separate operations. If SharePoint contained the assignment but Excel had not recorded that outcome, rerunning the flow could risk creating a duplicate.

KARINAOS™ INCIDENT REPORT:

Failure reported.

Assignment created.

Recovery procedure requires further development.

Recording the SharePoint ID helps connect the records, but does not prove that every partial failure is handled safely. A future improvement would check whether the destination item already exists before creating another after an interrupted run. This remains an identified limitation rather than a capability I can claim is fully proven.

Verification and validation require different evidence

The breakthrough came through a controlled test. I entered a new assignment into Excel, left its Imported field blank, and ran the automation. It read the table, identified the eligible assignment, created the SharePoint item, marked the source row as imported, and recorded the SharePoint ID.

NASA (2016) distinguishes verification, which checks compliance with requirements, from validation, which checks whether a product serves its intended purpose in its intended environment. My test demonstrated the expected sequence for one input. Broader validation asks whether normal use reduces duplicate entry and administrative effort.

Holt (2023) also identifies repeatability, measurability, and demonstrability as desirable process properties (p. 164).

Further checks should examine previously imported records, blank rows, and recovery after partial failure. Repeated testing and normal use would provide better evidence of reliability and time savings, without imposing procedures disproportionate to a personal assignment tracker.

Sustainability includes the person maintaining the system

Sustainability concerns whether a system and its effects can endure responsibly. Becker et al. (2015) identify environmental, social, economic, technical, and individual dimensions of sustainability and emphasize their interdependence.

Applied to my workspace, these dimensions raise questions about device requirements, accessibility, time costs, information longevity, and well-being. These are applications of the researchers’ framework to my experience, rather than findings they measured about my workflow.

An automation that reduces data entry but requires constant troubleshooting may transfer effort rather than reduce it. Documentation, clear field meanings, stable identifiers, and understandable recovery procedures could make maintenance easier. Export and replacement plans also matter if my access or tools change.

Holt (2023) warns that processes can become unrealistic, irrelevant, or outdated and require periodic review (p. 165). A workflow that helps this quarter may need revision as coursework or technology changes.

Each model offers sustainability opportunities and risks. Linear planning can establish requirements early but overlook changing conditions. Iteration can reveal burdens but consume resources through repeated rebuilding. Incremental delivery can avoid unnecessary features while accumulating integration complexity. Tomorrow’s Karina should inherit a useful workflow, not an archaeological investigation into today’s conditions.

GitHub provides another example of staged development

My planned blog submission combines these approaches. The complete academic article comes first, while optional diagrams and visual improvements can follow through further commits to the same pull request.

Adding a diagram is an increment; correcting an explanation is iteration. Following the required submission steps provides an ordered sequence. The initial version must still satisfy the assignment, since required analysis cannot be deferred as an optional enhancement.

My earlier submission was awaiting merging and grading when I discussed this plan. That distinguishes making work available from receiving review.

The development process is part of the upgrade

No single life cycle model is appropriate everywhere. My workflow benefits from ordered dependencies, iterative learning, and incremental capability.

This experience showed me that a patch can be necessary without addressing the underlying problem. An incomplete requirement, unchecked assumption, or missing recovery procedure may require a change in how the system is developed and evaluated.

The Cybernetic Desk now has a bulk intake workflow that passed a controlled test, along with a developer who understands its limitations more clearly. Its next improvements can build on evidence rather than optimism alone.

KARINAOS™ 2.0 RELEASE NOTES:

Improved assignment intake.

Added respect for empty cells.

Revised acceptance criteria.

User maintenance continues to include dinner.

KarinaOS™ 3.0: The Interface Is Now Under Review

By Karina Taylor | Module 2.1: Human First | A continuing KarinaOS™ investigation

Previously on KarinaOS™: The workflow passed a test. The human has questions.

KarinaOS™ is my playful model of how I manage coursework, responsibilities, and limited energy. The Cybernetic Desk is the actual digital workspace I am building to support that process. It began in SharePoint, Microsoft's platform for shared sites and information, and has since grown into my own web app.

The first post and KarinaOS™ 2.0 explored how my tools connect and how to build and test improvements. One lesson was that an assignment needs to exist before I can import it. That clarification has been entered into the historical record.

Now I have more freedom to change the workspace.

It also gives me fewer opportunities to blame the person who arranged the dashboard.

My next question is more specific: when I open this workspace, what does its interface ask my brain to do? All the information can be present while the layout still sends my attention somewhere unhelpful. I know this because I have already asked for one of my own useful features to move out of the way.

KARINAOS™ INTERFACE REVIEW

Previous finding: A green checkmark does not establish that the system fulfills its purpose.
Current investigation: What does using the system demand from the person?
Designer: Increasingly implicated.

The context for that question is still real: four classes, a commute between Yakima and Ellensburg, physical health complications, and a partner who is also a full-time student managing health issues. I am also learning a new job that I love, including its routines, communication styles, and social expectations. I can be excited about work and still come home with the processing capacity of a laptop at 3 percent battery. Enjoying the applications does not make them stop using RAM.

This installment examines how perception, attention, and memory should shape the interface, and what I need to learn before assuming it helps anyone besides me.

Read the human requirements before installing features

Human-centered design begins by understanding people, their tasks, and the circumstances in which they use a system. Franz and Kauer-Franz (2024) explain that users must be involved directly, with their feedback shaping revisions. Their discussion of ISO 9241-210, an international standard for human-centered design, emphasizes this context of use.

For my workspace, that means asking: What must remain visible when I return to a task? Which details can wait? What should I recognize without reconstructing the previous session? Somewhere there may be a user sitting peacefully at a clean desk with eight uninterrupted hours. I wish her well. We should probably also design for me.

Empathy means taking those circumstances seriously. IDEO.org (2015) emphasizes learning from the people experiencing a problem and involving them in developing solutions. My own frustration gives me a starting point, but it does not tell me what every student needs. Someone else may find my labels confusing or prefer a different amount of information. Designing for people requires listening when their experience challenges my assumptions.

The Observatory makes that context visible through planning areas for a small Seed task on the bus, an Anchor task on campus, and an optional Burst when momentum is available. A capacity selector lets me record how much I feel able to take on. These labels connect the workspace to the day I am actually navigating.

I enjoyed learning SharePoint, but restrictions in my school environment blocked external links and outside automations I wanted to use. My complaints belong to that setup; I am not filing a universal bug report against Microsoft. Canvas, our course platform, knows what I owe. Google knows where I am supposed to be. My school Microsoft environment has administrative concerns about them speaking to each other. My web app gives me more control over these connections and responsibility for how clearly I present them.

ISO and IDEO: Two ways to guide the next update

ISO 9241-210 provides a framework for managing human-centered design as a system is developed and used (International Organization for Standardization [ISO], 2019). Its core activities involve understanding users' circumstances, defining what they need to accomplish, developing possible solutions, and evaluating those solutions. The process repeats as new findings emerge (Franz & Kauer-Franz, 2024).

For my workspace, I could define a requirement that a student can find an unfinished assignment, open its instructions, and identify the next step. Watching someone attempt those tasks would help me evaluate the design. A correctly stored assignment can still be hard to find, understand, or resume.

IDEO.org's (2015) Field Guide offers three phases. In Inspiration, designers learn from people's lives and needs. In Ideation, they develop ideas and test possible solutions. Implementation brings the solution into use. Empathy, experimentation, and learning from failure support the process.

For me, this could mean testing a simple sketch of a new drawer arrangement before building it. “But I already made it pretty” is an understandable feeling and a terrible evaluation method.

The approaches complement one another: ISO structures the connection between user needs and evaluation, while IDEO provides practical encouragement and methods for exploring possibilities. Both require learning with people. I need evidence about what users understand, where they hesitate, and whether the workspace supports their tasks.

Attention management: Everything cannot be the main character

Visual hierarchy is how a layout signals what deserves attention first. One small revision made that idea concrete for me. My workspace displayed a “Private database connected” card near the top of the page. That information was useful, but it distracted me from the assignments and planning tools I had opened the workspace to use. Thank you for connecting to the database. Unfortunately, that was not the assignment I was avoiding. I requested that the card move to the bottom.

The card remained available, but my assignments received more priority. A feature can deserve a place in the system without deserving the first place my eyes land.

Perception also depends on how elements relate visually. A heading establishes a section; spacing separates groups; a boundary can make several details read as one task. W3C's guidance recommends clear regions, meaningful headings, and visual grouping to help users understand page structure (World Wide Web Consortium [W3C], 2021). For my dashboard, those choices can distinguish task information from navigation and secondary system details.

The color palette is also a deliberate choice. I find jewel tones against a cream background easier to process than stark black text on white. Darker jewel tones anchor the layout, while the richer accents give my ADHD brain enough visual interest to stay engaged. Apparently, the interface needs to provide both executive support and a reasonable level of ambiance.

That balance matters: I want enough visual interest to stay engaged without making every element compete for my attention. These choices reflect what works for me. Testing with other people would help me understand whether the same palette supports their reading and navigation, too.

I want everything accessible at a glance in a pretty package. However, that does not mean displaying every available detail with equal emphasis. I need a useful overview, with more information easy to reach. A task's title, progress, and next-step note should form a coherent group. If everything competes for attention, the dashboard simply gives me a more attractive place to feel overwhelmed. I already know how to be overwhelmed for free. The interface needs to contribute something else.

Four user experience laws meet KarinaOS™

User experience, or UX, includes how people experience using a product. These four principles give me specific ways to examine my interface choices.

Jakob's law: Please do not make me learn how doors work again

Jakob's law describes how experience with other products shapes users' expectations of a new interface (Yablonski, n.d.-c). In my workspace, familiar controls and understandable progress labels can help someone transfer knowledge they already have. A distinctive personality does not require inventing a new interaction for every action.

I organize the workspace into sections called “drawers,” but I am still evaluating their names, grouping, and order. I know where things are partly because I arranged them. A new user would not have that advantage. A charming digital desk loses some appeal when finding an assignment requires opening every drawer like I have misplaced my car keys. Testing could help me keep the character while making the navigation clearer.

Fitts's law: The assignment link should not be a boss fight

Fitts's law describes how a target's size and distance affect the time needed to move to and select it (Yablonski, n.d.-a). For my app, that means checking whether frequently used buttons and links are large enough and easy to reach.

Keeping an assignment link beside its task helps, but a tiny icon could still be difficult to select. I would want comfortably sized controls with enough separation to avoid mistakes, especially on a phone. This remains something to evaluate. Opening the instructions should not require the precision of defusing a tiny digital bomb.

Hick's law: I came here to do homework, not configure a spaceship

Hick's law connects decision time with the number and complexity of available choices (Yablonski, n.d.-b). For The Cybernetic Desk, this suggests examining how many decisions a person faces when importing or organizing assignments.

I value categories that fit how my brain works, but showing every customization option immediately could make starting harder. A default view could present essential choices first, with other settings available when needed. The labels still need to be clear; fewer vague options would not solve the problem. If I need a tutorial, a personality assessment, and a small committee before adding an essay, we have introduced a new assignment.

Miller's law: Yesterday Karina needs to leave a handoff document

Miller's law draws attention to limits in immediate memory and the value of meaningful chunks. It should not become a universal rule that an interface may contain only seven items; capacity varies with the person, knowledge, and situation (Yablonski, n.d.-d).

My practical application is keeping related task details together. I use “buckets,” or task-status groups, to show how far each assignment has progressed. Notes preserve where I left off. A note such as “outline finished; find second source next” would help me resume without remembering every previous step. Yesterday Karina understood this assignment. Today Karina would appreciate a handoff document. I do not need the dashboard to test my memory. Grouping information around recognizable tasks gives future me a place to recover context after the rest of life has interrupted.

Importing assignments without importing all the friction

The Signal Board is the assignment tracker inside The Cybernetic Desk. Its “Import Grade PDF” option addresses the work of getting assignment information into my planner. I can print my Canvas grades and assignment list to a PDF, then import it into my planner. The feature filters out completed assignments, flags possible duplicates, and pulls direct assignment links from the PDF. I can then sort tasks into categories that make sense to me.

Together, these steps reduce manual entry and keep the instructions within reach. The goal is to stop sending KarinaOS™ on a scavenger hunt whose prize is discovering how much homework I have. Canvas's calendar and dashboard have improved, but I still find adding my own planning details clunky. I need to move personal work sessions while preserving the instructor's official due date. I am trying to schedule the essay, not negotiate an extension with the calendar.

This supports Nielsen's (1994) principle of recognition rather than recall: make needed information available so people do not have to remember it between interactions. My notes preserve context, and the assignment link lets me check the original instructions.

My earlier Excel-import project exposed the risks of assumptions about incoming information. The PDF importer also needs a clear way to review results. A blank grade does not necessarily mean an assignment is unsubmitted, and a PDF is a snapshot that can become outdated. Duplicate flags and source links help me check the information. They do not prove every possible error is handled.

Updates require feedback, and scheduling requires permission

I would love to see another student move through my system without me explaining every step. I could ask them to import assignments, locate the original instructions, and leave a note for their next work session. I would watch where they hesitate, which drawer they open first, and whether the categories mean what they expect. I would also ask whether they enjoy using it and find it helpful. A successful click sequence alone would not answer those questions.

I have not run those tests yet. The drawer arrangement gives me a specific question to investigate, consistent with IDEO.org's (2015) emphasis on learning from people and revising solutions. I already know how to make another version. The challenge here is deciding what evidence should guide it. If I have to hover over someone's shoulder saying, “Oh, that is actually in the other drawer,” the hierarchy has provided some fairly direct feedback.

My future goal is to bring AI-assisted scheduling into The Cybernetic Desk itself, inspired by the support I want from tools such as Lifestack. The system could propose work sessions for my approval, with automatic scheduling turned on or off for each assignment. Some tasks need help finding a place in my week; others already have a plan I want to keep. The scheduler may suggest a study session. It may not declare that 8:00 p.m. Karina has the same capabilities as the ambitious woman who made the schedule at noon.

This is a future capability. It would need to show proposed changes clearly and let me reject, revise, or undo them. That aligns with Nielsen's (1994) principle of user control and freedom. Approval and assignment-level controls would be requirements to design and test.

Review status: The user gets a vote

Across these posts, the questions have become more specific. First, I examined the relationships producing an outcome. Then I examined how I develop and test changes to that system. Now I am examining what those changes require from the person encountering them.

ISO and IDEO help me plan that investigation, while the UX laws help me inspect specific choices. I can examine a button's size, a category's meaning, or the information needed to resume a task. “Intuitive” needs to become something I investigate rather than a compliment I award my own dashboard.

I still like my pretty workspace. I would also like it to survive contact with a person who did not help design it.
