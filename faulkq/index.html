Module 1.1 Blog Post

Introduction

When I think about designing a digital system, it is easy to focus on the individual pieces that make it work. For example, a computer network needs routers, switches, firewalls, servers, and computers. Each device has its own purpose, and making sure every device works correctly seems like a reasonable way to approach the design. However, a system can contain perfectly functional components and still fail if those components do not work together as intended. A firewall might be configured correctly, but it could block legitimate traffic needed by another service. A server might perform well on its own, but it could become a bottleneck when hundreds of users access it simultaneously. These examples show why understanding individual components is not enough to understand an entire system. This is where systems thinking becomes important. Systems thinking encourages us to look at the relationships between components, the environment in which a system operates, and the ways changes in one area can affect other areas. Instead of treating a problem as an isolated issue, it encourages us to investigate the larger structure that produces the problem. I think this perspective is especially important in information technology and cybersecurity because modern digital systems depend on numerous interconnected devices, applications, users, and security controls. In my view, systems thinking also changes the way we define success in system design. Success is not simply making each component function correctly. It is making sure the complete system achieves its intended purpose while remaining reliable, secure, maintainable, and adaptable. A model based approach supports this goal by allowing engineers to represent a system, examine how its parts interact, and evaluate design decisions before committing to a real world implementation. Together, systems thinking and model based systems engineering provide a practical foundation for designing complex digital systems.

Understanding Systems Thinking

I would define systems thinking as the ability to understand a problem by examining the entire system, including its components, relationships, dependencies, and environment, rather than focusing only on individual parts. It involves recognizing that the behavior of a system is influenced by how its components interact. A system is more than a collection of separate pieces because the relationships between those pieces can create outcomes that would not be obvious when examining each component individually. For example, consider a university's information technology environment. Students and employees depend on account authentication, campus networks, servers, cloud applications, endpoint devices, and technical support services. Each of these areas has a specific function, but they also depend on one another. If an authentication service becomes unavailable, users might lose access to several applications even though those applications and the network itself are still functioning. Looking at the issue as an isolated application failure would overlook the underlying dependency. A systems thinking approach would examine the authentication service, its connections to other services, its redundancy, and the potential impact of an outage across the university. Another important part of systems thinking is recognizing that problems can have multiple contributing causes. In IT, a slow application might initially appear to be a software issue. However, the actual cause could involve network congestion, insufficient server resources, inefficient database queries, or a combination of these factors. Changing the application without investigating the other possibilities could waste time and money while failing to solve the original problem. Systems thinking also requires considering how a system changes over time. A design that works well today might become unreliable as the number of users increases, software is updated, or new security requirements are introduced. A decision that seems beneficial in the short term could create maintenance problems later. For that reason, I believe systems thinking requires engineers to consider not only how a system works at the moment but also how it might behave under different conditions in the future. An important insight here is that the location where a problem appears is not necessarily the location where its cause originates. A user may experience a login failure on a laptop, but the underlying problem could involve a centralized identity service or a network dependency. This distinction matters because fixing the visible symptom does not always correct the underlying problem. Systems thinking encourages engineers to investigate the relationships that produce an outcome instead of immediately changing the component where the problem was noticed.

Systems Thinking Compared With Traditional Linear Thinking

Traditional linear thinking generally approaches a problem by identifying a cause, determining its effect, and applying a solution to that specific problem. This approach can be useful when a problem is well understood and has a relatively direct cause. For example, if a computer will not turn on because its power cable is disconnected, reconnecting the cable is an appropriate solution. There is no need to analyze the entire university network to fix that issue. However, linear thinking becomes less effective when a problem involves multiple interconnected components. In complex systems, several factors can influence the same outcome, and one change can produce consequences in unexpected places. Systems thinking expands the investigation by examining these connections and considering how different parts influence one another. Holt (2021) discusses the challenges of complexity in systems engineering and introduces model based methods for understanding and communicating system structure and behavior. This supports the idea that engineers need approaches that account for more than individual components. One difference between these approaches is how they define the boundaries of a problem. Linear thinking often narrows the scope to make a problem easier to solve. Systems thinking considers whether the problem is connected to a larger process or environment. Another difference is how each approach handles feedback. In a linear explanation, an action produces an effect. In a real system, that effect might influence future actions, creating a feedback loop that changes the original situation. For example, imagine a university IT department responding to repeated malware infections on campus computers. A linear approach might focus on cleaning each infected device and returning it to service. That response could be necessary, but it may not prevent the same issue from happening again. A systems thinking approach would also investigate how the malware entered the environment, whether endpoint protections were configured properly, whether devices were missing updates, how users were trained, and whether network segmentation limited the spread of infections. The larger investigation could reveal that the infections share a common cause, such as outdated software or inadequate endpoint monitoring. Addressing that cause could reduce future incidents across many devices rather than requiring technicians to repeatedly repair individual computers. In this situation, systems thinking helps the organization move from reacting to symptoms toward reducing the likelihood of the underlying problem. I do not think this means linear thinking is wrong or should be abandoned. Instead, I see the two approaches as complementary. Linear reasoning is useful for analyzing specific tasks, while systems thinking helps determine how those tasks fit into a larger environment. The important skill is knowing when a problem is simple enough for a direct solution and when the relationships between components require a broader investigation. Another important distinction is that systems thinking does not mean trying to analyze every possible factor in unlimited detail. That would be unrealistic and could make decision making slower. Instead, it means identifying the relationships that matter most to the system's purpose, risks, and performance. Engineers must still prioritize, establish boundaries, and make decisions with limited time and resources. To me, the strongest approach combines detailed analysis of individual components with an understanding of how the complete system behaves.

Why a Model Based Approach Is Important

A model based approach is applicable to complex digital systems because it provides a structured way to represent a system before it is fully implemented. A model can describe components, interfaces, requirements, dependencies, and expected behavior. Instead of relying entirely on separate documents or people's interpretations, engineers can use a shared representation to understand how the design is supposed to work. In Systems Engineering Demystified, Holt (2021) explains model based systems engineering as an approach for developing complex systems through modeling, visualization, and structured engineering practices. Modeling allows engineers to represent important aspects of a system at different levels of detail. For example, a high level model might show how users, applications, servers, and networks interact, while a more detailed model might describe individual interfaces or the behavior of a particular subsystem. This is valuable because complex digital systems can be difficult to understand through written descriptions alone. A written specification might explain that a firewall protects an internal network, but a diagram can show where that firewall sits, which networks it connects, what services communicate across it, and how traffic reaches critical servers. These representations make dependencies easier to identify and provide a common reference for the people responsible for designing, implementing, testing, and maintaining the system. NASA's Systems Modeling Handbook for Systems Engineering describes how modeling can support systems engineering activities, including defining stakeholder expectations, developing technical requirements, and planning verification and validation (National Aeronautics and Space Administration [NASA], 2025). Although NASA develops systems that differ significantly from university IT environments, the underlying principles are applicable to digital infrastructure. In both cases, engineers must translate needs into requirements, coordinate connected components, and determine whether the finished system satisfies its intended purpose. Consider the design of a secure university network with two internet connections, firewalls, intrusion prevention systems, switches, separate subnets, computers, and servers. It would be possible to purchase and configure each component individually, but that would not guarantee the network would operate securely or reliably. Engineers would first need to understand the intended traffic flows, security boundaries, access requirements, and potential points of failure. A model could illustrate how each subnet connects to the rest of the network, which systems are permitted to communicate, and how traffic is expected to move between internal resources and the internet. It could also help identify whether the network has a single point of failure or whether a security control creates an unintended interruption. These questions are much easier to investigate before the physical network is deployed than after users depend on it. A model based approach also supports traceability. Traceability means being able to connect a requirement to the design decisions and tests intended to satisfy it. For example, if a requirement states that student computers must not directly access a protected administrative server, the design should identify the network controls that enforce that restriction. Testing should then confirm that unauthorized connections are blocked while approved communication continues to function. Without traceability, a team might implement a security control but fail to verify that it addresses the original requirement. A model can help connect requirements, components, interfaces, and verification activities so that the team can better understand whether the system meets its objectives. NASA's systems engineering guidance emphasizes evaluating the complete system against its requirements rather than optimizing one subsystem at the expense of the overall design (NASA, 2017). Another benefit is the ability to evaluate changes before implementation. Suppose a university wants to add a new cloud based application that requires access to student records. That change could affect authentication, network traffic, data protection, access permissions, logging, and incident response. A model helps engineers identify the affected areas and consider the consequences before making changes to production systems. In my opinion, this is one of the most important reasons to use a model based approach: it makes the consequences of design decisions easier to examine before those decisions become expensive or difficult to reverse. Modeling does not eliminate mistakes, but it can reveal inconsistencies and missing requirements earlier in the development process. This can reduce rework and improve communication among technical teams.

The Limitations of Modeling and the Importance of Critical Thinking

Although model based systems engineering offers many advantages, I do not think creating a model automatically guarantees a successful system. A model is only as useful as the information, assumptions, and decisions that go into it. If engineers overlook an important requirement or misunderstand how users interact with a system, the model might look complete while still failing to represent real world conditions accurately. For example, a university network model might show that every firewall rule is configured correctly, but it might not fully account for employees using personal devices, students connecting from different locations, or a new application introduced after the design is completed. The model could be technically accurate for the original design while becoming outdated as the environment changes. This demonstrates why modeling must be combined with testing, monitoring, stakeholder feedback, and regular updates. I also believe there is a risk of becoming too focused on the model itself. Engineers could spend significant time creating detailed diagrams without asking whether those diagrams help solve the actual problem. The purpose of modeling should not be to make a system appear more organized on paper. It should be to improve understanding, support better decisions, and help ensure the finished system meets its requirements. This is why I see systems thinking and model based systems engineering as complementary rather than interchangeable. Systems thinking provides the mindset for recognizing relationships, dependencies, and unintended consequences. Model based systems engineering provides methods for representing those relationships and evaluating the design. Neither approach replaces professional judgment. Instead, they give engineers better information to use when making decisions. I think this lesson is particularly relevant to cybersecurity. Security controls rarely operate independently, and improving one area can introduce problems elsewhere. For instance, requiring more restrictive authentication could improve account security while making access more difficult for legitimate users. Allowing broader access might improve convenience but increase the risk of unauthorized activity. Engineers must consider these competing needs instead of optimizing one security measure without examining its effects on the rest of the system. A model can help make these trade offs visible, but people still need to decide which risks are acceptable and how the system should balance security, usability, cost, and reliability. Ultimately, the objective is not to create a perfect model. It is to develop a system that performs effectively in the environment where it will actually be used.

Conclusion

Systems thinking has changed how I understand the process of designing complex digital systems. Instead of viewing technology as a collection of independent devices and applications, I see it as an interconnected environment in which components influence one another. A problem in one area can originate elsewhere, and a solution that benefits one component can create unexpected consequences for the overall system. Traditional linear thinking remains useful for straightforward problems, but systems thinking becomes especially important when dealing with complex dependencies, feedback, and changing requirements. A model based approach builds on this perspective by giving engineers a structured way to visualize system relationships, evaluate requirements, communicate design decisions, and identify potential problems before implementation. My biggest takeaway is that a system should be designed around how all its parts work together, not simply how well each part works individually. This is particularly important in IT and cybersecurity, where reliability and security depend on interactions among technology, users, policies, and processes. By combining systems thinking with effective modeling, engineers can make more informed decisions, anticipate unintended consequences, and build digital systems that are better prepared to meet both current and future needs.

Module 1.2 Blog Post

Introduction

Technology is constantly changing, but developing a successful digital system requires more than creating something that works today. Systems engineers must consider how a system will be planned, designed, tested, implemented, maintained, and eventually replaced or retired. These stages are connected and influence the system’s long term performance, cost, reliability, and sustainability. Understanding the systems engineering life cycle helps organizations make informed decisions throughout a system’s existence rather than focusing only on its initial development. In Systems Engineering Demystified, Jon Holt explains systems engineering concepts that help teams manage complex systems and understand how different parts of a project relate to one another. Life cycle models provide a structured way to organize this work. Linear, iterative, and incremental models each offer different advantages depending on the project’s goals, complexity, and uncertainty. Understanding these approaches is important because the wrong development process can lead to wasted resources, missed requirements, and systems that are difficult to maintain. This is especially relevant to digital systems, where technology and user needs can change rapidly.

What Is the Systems Development Life Cycle?

The systems development life cycle (SDLC) is a structured process used to plan, create, test, implement, maintain, and eventually retire a system. Although it is commonly associated with software development, the same general idea applies to larger systems involving hardware, networks, people, processes, and supporting technology. The purpose of the life cycle is to make sure a system meets its intended requirements while remaining useful and manageable over time. The life cycle usually begins by identifying a problem or need. Engineers and stakeholders then develop requirements, evaluate possible solutions, design the system, and build or configure the selected solution. Testing helps determine whether the system meets its requirements before implementation. After deployment, the system still needs monitoring, maintenance, security updates, troubleshooting, and improvements. Eventually, it may be replaced or retired when it no longer meets organizational needs. The Federal Highway Administration (FHWA, n.d.) describes systems engineering life cycles as processes that can include planning, concept development, implementation, operations and support, and retirement. While the details vary by project, the central idea is that development is only one part of a system’s overall existence. A system can be successfully deployed and still fail in the long term if maintenance, support, or retirement planning is ignored. For example, a university purchasing new laptops should consider more than the purchase and setup process. The IT department must also plan for device imaging, software deployment, security updates, repairs, user support, inventory tracking, and eventual replacement or recycling. Thinking through these stages early can help prevent avoidable expenses and operational problems.

The Linear Life Cycle Model

The linear life cycle model organizes development into a sequence of stages. A team generally completes one stage before moving to the next. This approach is often associated with the waterfall model, in which requirements are established early, followed by design, implementation, testing, deployment, and maintenance. The model emphasizes planning, documentation, and defined checkpoints. One of the main advantages of the linear model is that it provides a clear structure. Team members can understand what needs to be completed, what deliverables are expected, and when each stage should be finished. This can make it easier to estimate budgets, assign responsibilities, and document decisions. It can be particularly useful when requirements are stable, the technology is well understood, and changes are expected to be limited. The FHWA (n.d.) notes that sequential approaches are most suitable when requirements are well understood and the risk associated with change is low. However, the linear model can become difficult to manage when requirements change after development has started. If testing reveals a major design problem, the team may need to revisit earlier decisions, creating delays and additional costs. Users may also have limited opportunities to provide meaningful feedback until later stages. This can result in a system that technically follows the original requirements but does not fully meet the users’ actual needs. Consider an organization installing a new network infrastructure. If the organization has well-documented requirements, known hardware specifications, and a stable environment, a linear process may be effective. However, if the organization discovers new security requirements or unexpected compatibility problems during implementation, the original plan may need significant changes. This demonstrates why a linear approach works best when uncertainty is relatively low.

The Iterative Life Cycle Model

The iterative life cycle model develops and improves a system through repeated cycles. Instead of expecting the initial design to be correct in every detail, the team creates a version, evaluates its performance, collects feedback, and makes improvements. This process continues until the system meets the necessary requirements or reaches an acceptable level of maturity. Holt (2023) discusses life cycle approaches as ways to structure system development and manage complexity, including the need to revisit and refine decisions as understanding improves. A major advantage of iterative development is that it gives teams opportunities to identify problems earlier. Testing and feedback occur throughout development rather than being postponed until the end. This can reduce the risk of building a system around incorrect assumptions. It also allows stakeholders to help shape the final product based on what they learn while using or evaluating earlier versions. For example, an IT department developing a new support ticket system might begin with a basic design and ask technicians to test it. Their feedback could reveal that ticket categories are confusing, important information is missing, or the workflow takes too many steps. The team can then adjust the design and test it again. This is more likely to produce a useful system than waiting until the entire platform is finished before collecting feedback. However, iterative development also has drawbacks. Repeated testing and revisions require time, coordination, and resources. If the team does not establish clear goals or manage changes carefully, the project can continue expanding without reaching completion. Stakeholders may also disagree about which improvements are most important. Iteration therefore requires discipline: feedback must be evaluated, priorities must be established, and each cycle should move the system toward a defined objective.

The Incremental Life Cycle Model

The incremental life cycle model builds and delivers a system in smaller, functional portions. Rather than waiting until the entire system is complete, a team releases a useful set of capabilities and then adds more features over time. Each increment contributes to the overall system and is intended to provide value on its own or alongside previous increments. One important advantage of this approach is that users can begin benefiting from the system before every feature is finished. It can also help organizations distribute costs and development effort over time. Teams can prioritize the most important capabilities first and use experience from earlier releases to guide later work. This is particularly useful for digital systems because organizations may need to respond to changing requirements, security risks, and user expectations. Imagine a university creating a new IT asset management system. The first increment might track device inventory and ownership. A later increment could add maintenance history, followed by warranty information, software inventories, and security compliance reporting. The organization gains useful functionality early while allowing the system to develop according to operational needs. Incremental development is not without challenges. Each new component must work with the existing system, so integration and compatibility need careful attention. If the initial architecture is poorly designed, later increments may become difficult to add. Teams must also plan how the different parts will share data, maintain security, and work together. Delivering features in stages does not eliminate the need for an overall design; it makes that design especially important.

Comparing the Three Life Cycle Models

The main difference between the three models is how they manage sequence, feedback, and delivery. The linear model follows a planned sequence and is generally strongest when requirements are stable. The iterative model emphasizes repeated evaluation and refinement, making it useful when the team expects to learn more as the project develops. The incremental model focuses on delivering the system in functional portions, allowing users to receive value before the entire solution is complete. These approaches are not necessarily mutually exclusive. A project may use a linear process for a well defined hardware installation while applying iterative methods to test and refine its software configuration. It may also deliver capabilities incrementally while using feedback from each release to guide the next. NASA (2017) describes systems engineering as a process involving technical planning, evaluation, verification, and management across the system life cycle. This supports the idea that teams should select development practices according to the system’s needs rather than automatically following one model for every project. In my view, choosing a life cycle model should begin with an honest assessment of uncertainty. If the requirements are clear and changes are unlikely, a linear approach may provide the structure needed to complete the work efficiently. If the team is still learning what users need, iteration may be more appropriate. If the organization needs a working solution quickly and can expand it over time, incremental development may offer the greatest benefit. The important point is to choose a process that manages the project's actual risks instead of choosing a model simply because it is familiar.

Why Life Cycle Thinking Matters for Sustainability

Life cycle thinking is important because a system’s environmental and financial impacts extend beyond its initial purchase or development. Decisions made during planning can affect energy use, maintenance requirements, replacement frequency, and disposal costs for years. A system that is inexpensive to acquire may become expensive to operate if it requires constant repairs, consumes excessive energy, or becomes difficult to secure and maintain. Digital systems also depend on physical resources. Computers, networking equipment, storage devices, and data centers require materials, manufacturing, electricity, cooling, transportation, and eventual disposal. Extending the useful life of equipment through appropriate repairs and upgrades can reduce unnecessary replacement and electronic waste. However, extending a device’s life is not always the best choice if the device consumes excessive power, cannot support necessary security updates, or no longer meets operational requirements. Life cycle decisions require balancing environmental impact, cost, security, and performance. For example, a university IT department deciding whether to replace laptops after three years or keep them for six years should evaluate more than the original purchase price. It should consider repair costs, battery replacement, performance, energy consumption, software support, security risks, and recycling options. A longer replacement cycle may reduce purchasing costs and electronic waste, but only if the devices remain reliable and secure enough to support students and employees. NASA’s systems engineering guidance emphasizes considering a system across its life cycle rather than treating development as an isolated activity (NASA, 2017). Applying that perspective encourages teams to evaluate long term consequences before committing to a design or procurement decision. It also makes sustainability a practical engineering consideration instead of something added at the end of a project.

How Life Cycle Thinking Supports Digital Systems Over Time

Digital systems rarely remain unchanged after deployment. Organizations introduce new applications, expand networks, update security requirements, and change how employees and customers use technology. A system designed only for its initial requirements may become outdated quickly. Life cycle thinking helps teams plan for updates, future integration, capacity changes, and eventual replacement from the beginning. Security is another important reason to consider the full life cycle. Software vulnerabilities may be discovered after a system has been deployed, and hardware or applications may eventually stop receiving vendor support. Organizations need processes for monitoring systems, applying patches, reviewing access, documenting changes, and deciding when a system should be retired. These responsibilities do not disappear once the initial implementation is complete. From an IT support perspective, documentation and maintenance are essential parts of keeping systems dependable. Technicians may need to troubleshoot recurring problems, restore devices, install approved software, or identify equipment that is approaching the end of its useful life. Accurate records can help IT teams recognize patterns and make better decisions about repairs, upgrades, and replacements. Without these practices, organizations may continue investing in systems that no longer meet their needs or overlook issues that create operational and security risks. Life cycle thinking also supports better communication between technical teams and decision makers. When engineers explain the long term costs, maintenance needs, and risks of a proposed system, managers can make decisions based on more than its initial price or promised features. This helps align technical decisions with the organization’s budget, security expectations, and long term goals.

Conclusion

The systems development life cycle provides a framework for managing technology from initial planning through operation, maintenance, and retirement. Linear, iterative, and incremental models offer different ways to organize this work. Linear development provides structure for stable requirements, iterative development supports learning and refinement, and incremental development allows useful capabilities to be delivered in stages. Each model has advantages, but each also requires careful planning and management. Ultimately, no single model is the best choice for every project. Systems engineers must understand the problem, the stakeholders, the level of uncertainty, and the consequences of failure before deciding how to proceed. Life cycle thinking also encourages organizations to consider sustainability, security, maintenance, and future changes from the beginning. By looking beyond initial development, teams can create digital systems that are more dependable, adaptable, cost effective, and responsible over the long term.

Introduction

Think about the last time you used a website or application that made a simple task unnecessarily difficult. Maybe you had to search through several menus to find a setting or accidentally selected the wrong button because the interface was confusing. These experiences demonstrate an important problem in technology design: a product can function correctly from a technical perspective and still fail the people who use it. Human centered design (HCD) addresses this problem by placing users' needs, abilities, experiences, and goals at the center of the design process. Rather than assuming developers know what people need, HCD encourages designers to investigate real user experiences and continuously improve their solutions. ISO 9241-210 emphasizes understanding users, their tasks, and their environments throughout the development of interactive systems (International Organization for Standardization [ISO], 2019). In my opinion, the most important idea behind HCD is that people should not have to work around a design when the design could have been created to work better for them. Understanding perception, attention, and empathy helps designers recognize why users behave differently and how interface decisions influence their experiences. By combining the structured framework of ISO 9241-210 with the practical, empathetic approach in IDEO's Field Guide to Human Centered Design, designers can create technology that is more intuitive, accessible, and effective.

ISO 9241-210 and IDEO's Field Guide

ISO 9241-210 and IDEO's Field Guide to Human Centered Design share the goal of designing around people's needs, but they offer complementary perspectives. ISO 9241-210 provides a framework for incorporating human centered activities throughout a system's life cycle. Its principles include understanding the context of use, identifying user requirements, developing design solutions, and evaluating those solutions against users' needs (ISO, 2019). This process is iterative, meaning designers should refine their work as they learn more. IDEO.org's (2015) Field Guide to Human Centered Design emphasizes empathy, collaboration, experimentation, and creativity. Its inspiration, ideation, and implementation phases encourage designers to learn directly from people, explore potential solutions, build prototypes, and improve their ideas through feedback. I see ISO's main strength as its structured approach to making user needs part of the entire development process. IDEO's strength is helping designers discover needs they might otherwise overlook. For example, imagine a university developing a new student portal. The ISO framework could help establish requirements for viewing grades, registering for classes, and paying tuition. An IDEO inspired approach could involve interviewing students and observing how they currently complete these tasks. That research might reveal that students frequently miss registration deadlines because important information is hidden among unrelated notifications. Designers could develop a prototype with clearer headings, more visible deadlines, and simplified navigation, then ask students to test it. Neither approach is sufficient by itself. A structured process can still produce a poor design if developers misunderstand users, while empathy alone cannot guarantee that a solution is functional or reliable. Combining the two creates a stronger process in which user research informs design decisions and testing determines whether those decisions actually work.

Perception, Attention, and Visual Hierarchy

Human perception influences how people interpret information and recognize important elements on a screen. Users do not examine every part of an interface equally. Instead, size, contrast, spacing, position, and familiarity influence what attracts their attention. Visual hierarchy uses these characteristics to communicate which information matters most. Consider a university registration page containing deadlines, course listings, tuition information, and several action buttons. If every element uses the same font size and visual emphasis, students must work harder to determine where to look. A better design could place the registration deadline near the top, separate information into clearly labeled sections, and make the primary registration button easy to identify. This improves more than appearance. A clear visual hierarchy helps users locate information quickly and understand what to do next. However, designers must avoid assuming everyone perceives information identically. Users with color vision deficiencies might struggle with color-coded information, while users with low vision may have difficulty reading small text. The World Wide Web Consortium (W3C, n.d.) emphasizes the importance of accessible visual presentation, including readable content and distinguishable information. For example, a security dashboard might use green for safe systems and red for compromised systems. Although this provides a quick visual signal for some users, color alone does not communicate the status reliably to everyone. Adding descriptive labels, recognizable icons, and clear messages makes the information easier to understand. This illustrates why designing for people requires more than attractive colors and modern layouts. Designers must consider how information will be perceived in different situations and by people with different abilities. Good visual design directs attention without creating unnecessary barriers.

Four UX Laws That Explain Human Behavior

Jakob's Law: Familiarity Reduces Learning

Jakob's Law explains that users spend much of their time using other products and bring those experiences and expectations to new interfaces (Yablonski, 2020). People generally expect familiar conventions, such as a magnifying glass representing search or a shopping cart leading to selected products. A university portal can use this principle by placing navigation in recognizable locations and using straightforward labels such as "Grades," "Courses," and "Account Settings." Students can then focus on completing their tasks instead of learning an unfamiliar interface. Unusual icons and inconsistent navigation may make a product look different, but they can also create confusion. I believe Jakob's Law demonstrates that innovation should solve a problem rather than introduce unnecessary differences. Designers should preserve familiar patterns when they help users and change them when there is a clear benefit.

Fitts's Law: Make Important Actions Easy to Select

Fitts's Law describes how target size and distance influence the time required to reach and select an interactive target. Larger targets that are closer to the user's current position are generally faster and easier to select (Yablonski, 2020). This principle matters when designing buttons, links, and touchscreen controls. For instance, a small "Submit" button placed far from the final fields of a long form can make completing the task inconvenient. A clearly labeled button with an adequate target size, positioned near the relevant fields, makes the interaction easier. Designers should also consider the consequences of mistakes. A button that permanently deletes an account should not be activated accidentally just because it is large and easy to reach. Clear labels and appropriate confirmation steps can protect users. Therefore, Fitts's Law is not simply about making every action faster; it is about making intended actions easier while reducing errors.

Hick's Law: Simplify Decisions Without Removing Control

Hick's Law explains that decision time generally increases as the number and complexity of choices increase (Yablonski, 2020). When users encounter too many options simultaneously, determining what to do can become difficult. Imagine a university IT support page containing 30 equally prominent links for password resets, networking, software installation, hardware problems, and account access. Even if every link is useful, users must first figure out which option applies to their situation. Grouping services into clear categories, highlighting common tasks, and providing a search function can reduce this burden. A guided troubleshooting process could also ask a few relevant questions before displaying specific solutions. However, simplifying choices does not mean hiding useful features. Experienced users may prefer direct access to advanced settings, while beginners might need additional guidance. Effective design organizes options around user goals while preserving appropriate flexibility. More choices do not automatically provide more control; understandable choices often provide a better experience.

Miller's Law: Respect the Limits of Working Memory

Miller's Law is commonly associated with the limited capacity of working memory. Miller (1956) discussed immediate memory capacity and how grouping information into meaningful chunks can help people process it. In UX design, this principle encourages designers to avoid overwhelming users with too much information at once. For example, a password reset page containing lengthy instructions, unrelated links, and several warning messages may make the next step difficult to identify. Breaking the process into manageable stages and grouping related information can help users complete the task. The same idea applies to navigation menus and technical instructions. Organizing settings into categories such as security, privacy, and notifications makes a complex interface easier to understand. However, Miller's Law should not be interpreted as a strict requirement to limit every interface to seven items. Memory depends on context, familiarity, and the complexity of the information. The goal is to make information easier to process, not simply to count the number of visible options. Together, these four laws explain how familiarity, target size, decision complexity, and memory influence usability. They provide useful guidance, but designers should still test their decisions with real users rather than assuming a principle guarantees success.

Empathy and Real World User Needs

UX laws help explain human behavior, but they cannot replace empathy. Empathy involves understanding people's experiences, challenges, motivations, and environments. IDEO.org (2015) emphasizes learning directly from users instead of relying exclusively on assumptions. Consider a student struggling to use an online learning platform. A developer might assume the student needs a more attractive dashboard. However, an interview or usability test could reveal that assignment deadlines appear differently across courses, the platform is difficult to use on a phone, or its controls do not work well with assistive technology. These students use the same product but experience different challenges. Empathy helps designers recognize those differences and develop solutions based on actual needs. It also changes how designers interpret mistakes. If users repeatedly click the wrong button, the problem might be unclear labeling, similar looking controls, or insufficient feedback. Instead of blaming users, designers can investigate why the interface encourages mistakes. Of course, empathy does not mean implementing every request. Users may have conflicting preferences, and security, technical, or financial constraints may limit available solutions. Its value is that it helps designers understand the reasons behind users' behavior and make more informed decisions.

Applying Human Centered Design Early

Usability should be considered before a product is fully developed. Waiting until the end to evaluate the user experience can allow expensive design problems to become embedded in a system. A stronger process begins by identifying users and their goals, investigating their environment, and developing an understanding of their needs. Designers can then build simple prototypes and ask representative users to complete realistic tasks. Observing hesitation, confusion, and mistakes can reveal issues that technical testing alone might miss. For example, a university IT team designing a support portal could discover that students do not know which support category to select. A prototype with familiar labels, clearly organized categories, and an understandable confirmation message could address that problem. Jakob's Law would encourage familiar navigation, Fitts's Law would inform button design, Hick's Law would guide category organization, and Miller's Law would support clear instructions. Empathy and usability testing would determine whether these decisions actually help students. The team could revise the design based on feedback and repeat the evaluation, reflecting ISO 9241-210's emphasis on iterative development (ISO, 2019). Accessibility must also remain part of the process. Testing across devices, assistive technologies, and different levels of technical experience helps identify barriers before they become permanent features of the product.

Conclusion

Human centered design changes how we evaluate technology. Instead of asking only whether a system works, designers should ask whether people can understand it, navigate it, and use it to accomplish their goals. ISO 9241-210 provides a structured development framework, while IDEO's Field Guide emphasizes empathy, exploration, and experimentation. Perception and visual hierarchy influence what users notice, while Jakob's, Fitts's, Hick's, and Miller's laws explain how familiarity, target size, choices, and memory affect interactions. My biggest takeaway is that design principles should support an understanding of people rather than replace it. No single law guarantees that an interface will work for everyone. Designers must observe real behavior, consider accessibility, and remain willing to revise their assumptions. Technology should make people's lives easier instead of requiring them to become experts at navigating poorly designed systems. When designers begin with human needs and continuously evaluate their solutions, they can create products that are functional, intuitive, inclusive, and genuinely useful.
