Understanding Systems Thinking

When we think about engineering, we often imagine designing a mechine, developing software or building a product. Successful engineering is not only about making individual components work. It is about ensuring that all components,people,processes and technologies work together as one complete system.

A system can appear successful at the individual component level but still fail as a whole.This is why systems thinking and MBSE are important. In my understanding, Syatems thinking teaches us to look beyond individual parts and understand their relationships while MBSE provides a structured way to represent, communicate and manage those relationships throughout a system's lifecycle

What is Model Based Systems Engineering ?

A system is a collection of connected elements that work together to achieve a particular purpose. These elements may include people hardware, software, processes and information. Understanding a system requires examining its elements, stakeholders, attributes boundaries, needs and constraints (Holt, 2019).

For example, consider a hospital's information technology system. It is not simply a computer network or electronic health record software. It includes patient databases, medical devices, healthcare professionals, cybersecurity controls, network infrastructure and administrative processes.

If one part fails, the impact may spread throughout the organization. A network outage could prevent healthcare professionals from accessing patient records, delay communication and affect daily operations. This example demonstrates that systems thinking is about recognizing relationships and consequences rather than focusing only on isolated problems.

My key insight is that solving one problem does not always mean improving the entire system. Sometimes, improving one component creates new problems elsewhere. Therefore, engineers must consider the broader environment, stakeholder needs, and possible unintended consequences before making decisions.

Managing Complexity, Communication and Context

There are three major challenges of systems engineering: complexity, communication and context. These challenges explain why engineering a large system is more difficult than simply connecting its individual parts (Holt, 2019).

Complexity: Modern systems contain many interconnected components. A small change in one component can affect other parts of the system.

Communication: Different stakeholders may have different technical knowledge and priorities. Without clear communication, a team may develop something that does not fully meet the user's needs.

Context: A system does not operate in isolation. It interacts with users, organizations, regulations and other systems. Engineers must understand these external relationships before making decisions.

From Documents to Connected Models

Traditional systems engineering often relies on separate documents, spreadsheets and diagrams. When a requirement changes, several documents may need to be updated manually. This can create confusion, outdated information and communication gaps.

Model Based Systems Engineering addresses this challenge by using a connected system model as a central source of information. MBSE uses models to represent system structure, behavior, requirements and relationships. A model is not the actual system. Instead, it is a simplified representation that helps people understand and analyze the real system (Holt, 2019).

For example, when designing a hospital cybersecurity system, engineers could use Systems Modeling Language diagrams to represent the network structure, connected devices, security requirements, access controls and responses to security incidents.

Understanding Models, Views and Ontology

Another concept I found interesting is the relationship between models, views and ontology. A model represents the overall system, while a view presents specific information for a particular stakeholder.

All these views should represent the same underlying system without contradicting each other. Ontology is important because it establishes shared concepts and relationships. A common vocabulary helps ensure that everyone understands what the model represents.

Why MBSE Matters in Modern Engineering

As systems become more interconnected, managing requirements and relationships through separate documents becomes increasingly challenging. The International Council on Systems Engineering describes MBSE as the formal application of modeling to support requirements, design, analysis, verification and validation throughout a system's lifecycle.

How MBSE Improves Communication and Decision-Making

One of the most useful benefits of Model-Based Systems Engineering is that it creates a shared understanding among different stakeholders. In a large project, software developers, network engineers, managers, security analysts and end users may all have different expectations. When information is shared only through long documents or emails, misunderstandings can occur. MBSE uses models and diagrams to represent system components, relationships, requirements and behavior in a visual format. For example, a hospital cybersecurity team could use a system model to show how patient records move between a medical workstation, an application server and a database. This model could help technical staff understand where security controls should be applied and help managers understand why certain investments are necessary. However, a model is only useful when it is accurate, updated and understandable to the people using it.

Systems Thinking in Healthcare Cybersecurity

Systems thinking is especially important in healthcare because hospitals depend on many connected systems to provide safe and effective patient care. For example, an electronic health record system is not just a software application. It connects doctors, nurses, patients, medical devices, databases, networks and administrative departments. If one part of this system fails, it can affect the entire hospital. A network outage may prevent medical staff from accessing patient records, while a cybersecurity attack could expose sensitive patient information or interrupt medical services. Systems thinking helps security professionals understand these relationships instead of focusing only on individual computers or applications. By examining the entire environment, organizations can identify risks, understand how one failure may affect other components and develop better security plans. This approach also reminds us that technology, people and procedures must work together to protect healthcare information.

Is MBSE Always the Solution?

Although MBSE provides important advantages, I do not believe that simply adopting modeling software guarantees a successful project. A model is only as reliable as the information, assumptions and people behind it.

If a model is outdated, incomplete or too complicated, it may create additional confusion instead of solving communication problems. Teams also need training, agreed modeling practices and suitable tools.

Therefore, I believe engineers should focus on creating models that answer real questions rather than creating diagrams just to satisfy a process. The purpose of MBSE should be better understanding and decision-making not simply replacing documents with software.

Challenges and Limitations of Model-Based Systems Engineering

Although MBSE offers many advantages, it is not a perfect solution for every project. Creating detailed models requires time, technical knowledge, appropriate tools and cooperation among stakeholders. Smaller organizations may not have enough resources or trained employees to implement a complete MBSE process. Another challenge is that models can become outdated when system requirements or technologies change. If team members continue using an old model, it may create confusion rather than improve communication. In cybersecurity, this could be especially dangerous because networks, applications and threats change frequently. I believe organizations should begin with the most important parts of a system and gradually expand their models as needed. The goal should not be to create the largest or most complicated model. Instead, the goal should be to create useful models that support understanding, decision-making and system improvement.

My Perspective on Systems Thinking and MBSE

As an IT Management student focusing on cybersecurity, I believe systems thinking and MBSE can help me prepare for future responsibilities in information security. Cybersecurity is not limited to installing antivirus software, configuring firewalls or monitoring suspicious network activity. Security professionals must understand how business operations, users, networks, applications and security policies depend on one another. For example, when designing security for a hospital, protecting patient data is important but maintaining access to critical medical systems is equally important. A security control that is too restrictive could interrupt the work of healthcare professionals. This demonstrates why security decisions must consider the entire system and its stakeholders. In my opinion, the biggest lesson from this module is that effective engineering requires more than technical knowledge. It requires understanding relationships, communicating clearly and considering the consequences of decisions before implementing them.

Understanding Systems life Cycle Models

Systems engineering is not simply about designing and building a product. It also involves understanding how a system develops, operates, changes and eventually reaches retirement. Three commonly discussed models are the linear, iterative and incremental models. Each provides a different approach to organizing development activities and meeting stakeholder needs.

Linear Model: Structure and Predictability

linear model commonly associated with the waterfall approach, organizes development into sequential stages. A project generally moves from requirements and design to implementation, testing and delivery. One advantage of this model is that each stage has clear objectives and deliverables. This structure makes planning, budgeting, documentation and progress tracking easier. For example, an organization replacing well-understood equipment may benefit from a linear approach when its requirements are stable. However, this model becomes less effective when stakeholder needs change frequently. If a major problem is discovered during testing, returning to an earlier stage can require significant time and money. Therefore, a linear model is most appropriate when requirements and technology are sufficiently understood before development begins.

Iterative Model: Learning Through Repetition

Iterative model allows engineers to revisit and improve a system through repeated development cycles. Instead of assuming that the first design will be correct, engineers use feedback, testing and evaluation to identify areas for improvement. For example, a cybersecurity team developing a threat-monitoring dashboard could begin with a basic version, evaluate how analysts use it and improve its alerts and reporting features in later iterations. This approach helps teams discover problems earlier and respond to changing needs. The Agile approach also emphasizes feedback, adaptation and frequent delivery of useful results. In my view, iteration is especially valuable when a project involves uncertainty because engineers can learn from evidence instead of relying entirely on their original assumptions.

Incremental Model: Delivering Value in Parts

Incremental model focuses on delivering a system in separate, usable portions. The overall requirements guide development, while individual increments provide additional capabilities over time. For example, a hospital information system might first introduce secure user authentication then add access control and audit logging and later integrate security alerts and reporting. Each increment adds functionality toward the larger system. This approach helps organizations obtain useful capabilities before the entire system is complete. However, engineers must plan carefully to ensure that individual increments integrate properly and support the system's overall requirements. Systems engineering standards emphasize considering system needs and activities throughout the system life cycle.

Comparing the Models and Applying Systems Thinking

Although these models have different characteristics, none is automatically the best choice for every project.linear model emphasizes sequential progress, Iterative model emphasizes repeated learning and refinement and the incremental model emphasizes delivering capabilities in manageable portions. These approaches can also be combined when project conditions require it. For instance, a healthcare cybersecurity project might use a defined overall architecture, develop monitoring capabilities incrementally and improve detection rules iteratively based on testing results. This combination provides structure without preventing adaptation. Engineers must also consider maintenance, technology changes, stakeholder expectations and retirement instead of focusing only on initial delivery (Holt, 2019).

Choosing the Right Life Cycle Model

My main takeaway is that choosing a life cycle model is a strategic engineering decision not just a project management preference. A model influences how teams manage risk, communicate with stakeholders respond to unexpected problems and allocate resources. As someone interested in cybersecurity, I see particular value in combining structured planning with continuous improvement. Security threats evolve, software changes and new vulnerabilities emerge after a system enters operation. A team that never revisits its original design may miss important risks. Frequent changes without a clear architecture can create inconsistencies and new weaknesses. Systems engineers should choose a model based on project needs, evidence and risk while considering the system's entire life cycle.

Introducing the Concept of Human Centered Design

Human-centered design is an approach to problem-solving that begins with understanding people and their needs before deciding what solution to create. Instead of assuming that designers already know what people need, this approach encourages them to listen, observe, ask questions, and learn directly from the people who will use or be affected by a solution. Human-centered design can be used to develop products, digital applications, public services, healthcare systems and solutions to social problems. Its central idea is that a successful solution should not only work technically but should also make sense for the people who will use it

Designers should therefore work with communities and learn from their experiences rather than developing solutions based only on their own assumptions. The guide presents three main phases of human-centered design: Inspiration, Ideation and Implementation. It also introduces seven mindsets: empathy, optimism, iteration, creative confidence, making, embracing ambiguity and learning from failure.

What I find most interesting about human-centered design is that it changes the way a problem is understood. A designer might initially believe that a complicated application needs a more attractive interface. After speaking with users, however, the designer might discover that the real problem is confusing instructions, limited internet access or a lack of trust in the service. This shows that solving a problem effectively requires understanding the people experiencing it before selecting a solution.

My main argument is that human-centered design improves problem-solving by connecting creativity with real human experiences. However, it should not be treated as a guarantee of success. Designers must also consider accessibility, privacy, cost, technical limitation, and the experiences of people who are not always represented in research. Human-centered design is most valuable when listening to people leads to meaningful action, careful testing and continuous improvement.

What Is Human Centered Design?

Human-centered design places people at the center of the design process. It considers what people need, what they value, the challenges they face and how they interact with products or services in their everyday lives. According to IDEO.org (2015), designers should understand the people they are designing for, generate possible solutions and test their ideas before implementing them on a larger scale. This process helps designers move beyond assumptions and develop solutions grounded in actual experiences.

Human-centered design is not limited to professional designers. Students, engineers, business owners, software developers and community organizations can use it to solve problems. For example, a university developing a student portal should consider more than the technical features of the website. It should understand how students register for classes, find assignments, contact instructors and access important academic information. Students may have different levels of technical experience, different accessibility needs and different devices or internet connections. Understanding these differences can help the university design a service that works for a wider range of people.

IDEO describes human-centered design as an approach that starts with people's needs and experiences and connects them with technological possibilities and organizational requirements. This is important because a solution must balance several considerations. It should be desirable for the people using it, feasible to develop and maintain and viable within the organization's resources and responsibilities.

For example, a mobile application might be useful to students but too expensive for a university to maintain. Another application might be affordable and technically advanced but difficult for students with disabilities to use. Neither solution fully addresses the problem. Human-centered design encourages teams to consider these concerns together rather than focusing on one measure of success.

An important distinction is that human-centered design does not mean giving every user exactly what they request. Users may suggest different or conflicting features and some requests may not address the underlying problem. Instead, designers should investigate why people want a particular feature, identify common needs, and evaluate possible solutions. The goal is to understand the problem deeply enough to create an appropriate response.

Phase One: Inspiration

The Inspiration phase focuses on understanding people and the challenges they experience. Describes this phase as an opportunity to observe people's lives, listen to their hopes and desires and learn more about the design challenge. Designers should remain curious instead of immediately deciding what the solution must be.

Several activities can support this phase, including interviews, observation, conversations and research into people's everyday experiences. Interviews allow designers to ask questions about people's needs and frustrations. Observation can reveal behaviors that people may forget to mention or may not recognize themselves. Combining these approaches helps designers develop a more complete understanding of the problem.

Imagine that a college notices that many students miss assignment deadlines. An initial assumption might be that students need more reminders. However, interviews and observation could reveal several different causes. Some students might struggle to find assignment instructions, others may not understand how the learning platform is organized and some may have difficulty accessing the platform using a mobile device. Additional reminders would not necessarily solve these problems.

This example demonstrates why empathy matters. Describes empathy as the ability to understand other people's lives and consider problems from their perspectives. Empathy does not mean simply feeling sorry for someone. It involves making a genuine effort to understand their circumstances without assuming that their experiences are the same as the designer's.

My reflection is that the Inspiration phase may be the most important part of the process because it determines how the problem is framed. If designers misunderstand the problem, even an excellent technical solution may produce disappointing results. Spending time learning about users may initially appear slower than immediately building a product but it can prevent a team from investing resources in a solution that does not address the real need.

Ideation

After gathering information, designers move into Ideation. During this phase, the team organizes its observations, identifies patterns and develops different possible solutions.Emphasizes generating many ideas before deciding which ones deserve further attention. This approach encourages teams to explore possibilities rather than accepting the first idea that seems reasonable.

For example, if students struggle to locate assignments in a learning platform, a team might consider several solutions. It could simplify the navigation menu, create a weekly assignment summary, introduce clearer notifications or develop a dashboard showing upcoming deadlines. The team could also consider improving the instructions provided by instructors. These ideas address different aspects of the problem and should be evaluated according to user needs and practical limitations.

Brainstorming can be useful because it allows team members to contribute different perspectives. However, generating many ideas is not enough. The team must examine which ideas are likely to solve the problem which are technically possible and which can be supported with available resources. Feedback from users can help the team identify weaknesses and decide which concepts should be developed further.

Implementation

Implementation focuses on turning ideas into solutions that can be tested, delivered and maintained. This phase involves bringing a solution to life and considering how to maximize its impact. A promising idea must move beyond discussion and become something people can experience and evaluate.

Prototyping is an important part of this work. A prototype is an early representation of a proposed solution. It might be a paper drawing, a clickable interface, a basic website, or a small-scale version of a service. A prototype does not need to be perfect. Its purpose is to make an idea tangible so that designers and users can examine it, discover problems, and suggest improvements.

Returning to the student portal example, a development team could create a simple dashboard that displays upcoming assignments and their deadlines. Before building a complete application, the team could ask students to use the prototype and explain what they understand, where they become confused and which information they cannot find. The team could then revise the design based on the results.

The Seven Mindsets of Human-Centered Design

In addition to the three phases, identifies seven mindsets that guide human-centered designers. These mindsets help explain how designers should approach uncertainty, collaboration, creativity and learning.

  1. Empathy: Designers work to understand people's experiences, needs, and perspectives. Empathy helps prevent teams from relying entirely on personal assumptions.
  2. Optimism: Designers approach challenges with the belief that improvement is possible. Optimism encourages exploration, although it should be balanced with realistic evaluation of risks and constraints.
  3. Iteration: Designers repeatedly improve ideas based on feedback and new information. The process does not assume that the first solution will be the best one.
  4. Creative confidence: Designers develop confidence in their ability to explore and contribute ideas. Creativity is treated as a skill that can grow through practice.
  5. Making: Designers turn abstract ideas into something tangible. Building a prototype helps people discuss a real example rather than an idea that exists only in conversation.
  6. Embracing ambiguity: Designers accept that they may not know the correct answer at the beginning. Remaining open to uncertainty creates room for discovery and alternative approaches.
  7. Learning from failure: Designers treat unsuccessful attempts as opportunities to learn. Testing a weak idea can reveal important information that leads to a better solution.

These mindsets are connected rather than separate rules. Empathy helps designers understand the problem, while embracing ambiguity allows them to explore possible explanations. Creative confidence encourages them to develop ideas, making allows those ideas to be examined and iteration helps refine them. Learning from failure supports the process by showing what does not work. Optimism encourages the team to continue learning instead of giving up when an early attempt is unsuccessful.

I believe iteration and learning from failure are especially important because people often become attached to their first ideas. A team may spend considerable time developing a feature and then hesitate to change it when testing reveals a problem. However, the purpose of user testing is not simply to confirm that the design is correct. It is to discover whether the design actually meets people's needs. A team that listens honestly to criticism has a better opportunity to improve its work.

Challenges and Limitations

Human-centered design offers a valuable way to understand problems but it also has limitations. One challenge is that designers cannot always speak with every person affected by a solution. A small group of interview participants may not represent the experiences of the wider community. If a team mainly interviews people who are comfortable with technology, it might overlook people who have limited digital experience, disabilities, language barriers or unreliable internet access.

This limitation suggests that empathy must be supported by thoughtful research. Designers should consider who is participating, whose perspectives are missing, and whether the findings reflect different situations. They should avoid assuming that one person's experience represents everyone. When possible, teams should involve a diverse range of users and revisit their conclusions as new evidence becomes available.

Applying Human-Centered Design to Cybersecurity

Human-centered design is especially relevant to cybersecurity because security technologies depend on human behavior. An organization may introduce strong password requirements, multifactor authentication and security training, but these measures may not work as intended if employees find them confusing or difficult to use.

Imagine a hospital introducing a new multifactor authentication system. A technology team might focus on preventing unauthorized access. However, nurses and doctors may need to access patient records quickly while working under pressure. If the authentication process is difficult to understand or unreliable, staff may become frustrated or seek unsafe workarounds. A human-centered approach would involve healthcare workers early, observe how authentication fits into their daily work and identify both security requirements and usability concerns.

Conclusion

Human-centered design is a problem-solving approach that begins with people and develops solutions through research, creativity, prototyping and learning. The three phases of Inspiration, Ideation and Implementation provide a useful structure for moving from understanding a challenge to developing and testing a solution. The seven mindsets described by IDEO.org (2015) encourage designers to remain empathetic, optimistic, creative, open to uncertainty, willing to experiment, and ready to learn from failure.

My main takeaway is that the quality of a solution depends partly on how well the problem is understood. A technically impressive product may still fail if it ignores people's real needs or the conditions in which they use it. At the same time, listening to users is not enough on its own. Designers must evaluate evidence, consider people whose voices are missing, respect privacy, and account for practical limitations.

Designing for the Human Mind

How Memory, Habits, and Mental Models Shape Usable Digital Systems

Usability Is About People, Not Just Technology

Have you ever opened a website and immediately understood where to click, even though you had never used it before? On the other hand, have you ever struggled to find a simple button, understand an error message or complete a form because the interface was confusing? These experiences demonstrate an important idea: a digital system can have advanced technology and attractive visuals but still be difficult to use.

Usability is not simply about making a website look professional. It is about helping people accomplish their goals without unnecessary effort, confusion or frustration. Every day, people interact with learning management systems, banking websites, mobile applications, online shopping platforms and other digital tools. Whether these interactions are successful often depends on how well the systems support human memory, habits, attention and expectations.

As an Information Technology Management student specializing in cybersecurity, I am particularly interested in how people interact with digital systems. Security and usability are closely connected. When users cannot understand a system, they may make mistakes, ignore important warnings, or choose unsafe shortcuts.

Instead of asking only whether a feature works technically, we should ask whether people can understand it, learn it, remember how to use it and recognize the results of their actions.

My central argument is that usable digital systems reduce the amount of unnecessary thinking users must do. Designers cannot eliminate every difficult task, but they can make interfaces more predictable, provide useful feedback and build on what people already know.

1. How Memory Influences User Interaction

Human memory plays an important role in everyday interactions with technology. However, people cannot remember every instruction, menu location, password and step in a complicated process. When a digital system requires users to remember too much information, completing even a simple task can become difficult.

One important distinction is between recall and recognition. Recall means retrieving information from memory without much help. Recognition means identifying something familiar when it is presented as an option.

For example, remembering the exact name of a setting in an unfamiliar application requires recall. Seeing a clearly labeled setting and recognizing that it is the correct choice requires recognition. Recognition is often easier because the interface provides helpful clues.

Consider an online university portal. A poorly designed portal might require students to remember course codes, navigate through several unrelated pages and manually re-enter information. A more usable system could display relevant course information, provide searchable options, preserve previously entered information and show the remaining steps.

Visible menu labels, familiar icons accompanied by text, saved preferences and clear instructions help users identify what they need without memorizing unnecessary details.

This principle is especially relevant to university learning platforms such as Canvas. Students regularly switch between course modules, assignments, announcements, grades and discussion boards. If these features are consistently labeled and organized, students can focus on learning rather than repeatedly figuring out how to navigate the platform.

2. Mental Models: Why Familiar Designs Feel Easier

A mental model is a person's internal understanding of how something works. People develop mental models through experience with physical objects, software, websites and other systems. These models influence what users expect to happen when they perform an action.

For example, many people understand a shopping cart because it resembles the idea of placing items in a physical cart before paying. A trash-bin icon suggests deleting something, while a magnifying-glass icon suggests searching. Designers use these familiar patterns because people can transfer existing knowledge to new situations.

My experience with online learning systems provides a useful example. When I open a course website, I expect assignments to appear under an assignment section, grades to be accessible through a grades section, and course materials to be grouped into modules or lessons. These expectations form a mental model of how a learning platform should work.

When the actual interface follows this model, navigation feels natural. When an assignment is hidden under an unexpected menu or a submission button appears in an unfamiliar location, I must stop and reconsider how the system works.

The problem is not necessarily that the user lacks technical knowledge. The problem may be that the system violates a reasonable expectation. Good design builds on familiar expectations while making unfamiliar or important actions clear.

3. Interface Consistency and the Power of Habits

Habits make repeated interactions faster because people gradually learn where controls are located and what actions produce particular results. Interface consistency means that similar actions, visual elements, labels, and navigation patterns behave similarly throughout a system.

Imagine using a website where the navigation menu appears at the top of every page, the primary action buttons have consistent labels and forms follow a predictable structure. After a few visits, users begin to anticipate where they should go next. They do not need to relearn the interface each time.

Now imagine the opposite situation. The navigation menu moves between pages, the same action is labeled "Submit" in one section and "Finish" in another and the system uses different colors for identical functions. Users must repeatedly stop to interpret the interface. This creates unnecessary cognitive load.

Emphasizes understanding people's experiences and developing solutions through learning, testing and iteration. Designers should observe how people actually use a system rather than designing only around their own preferences.

4. System Feedback: Helping Users Understand What Is Happening

A usable interface should communicate what happens after a user takes an action. This communication is called system feedback. Feedback can take many forms, including confirmation messages, loading indicators, progress bars, highlighted selections and error explanations.

Systems should keep users informed about what is happening through appropriate feedback within a reasonable time.

Consider submitting an assignment through an online learning platform. After clicking "Submit," a student might see a message confirming that the file was uploaded successfully, along with the submission time and the name of the uploaded document.

Without confirmation, the student may wonder whether the file was uploaded, whether the button registered the click, or whether the process failed. They might click the button repeatedly or upload duplicate files.

Good feedback should answer three basic questions:

  • What happened? Did the system accept the action?
  • What is happening now? Is the process still running?
  • What should I do next? Is another step required?

Effective feedback is also a trust mechanism. Users are more likely to trust a system when it clearly communicates its status instead of leaving them to guess.

5. Tesler's Law: Complexity Does Not Simply Disappear

Tesler's Law, also known as the Law of Conservation of Complexity, states that every system has some complexity that cannot be eliminated entirely. Instead, the complexity must be handled by the user, the software, or the people developing the system

This principle raises an important design question: Who should have to deal with the complexity?

Consider completing a university application. The process may require personal information, educational history, supporting documents and several confirmations. Some of this complexity is unavoidable because the university needs accurate information.

However, the interface determines how difficult it is to provide that information. A poorly designed application might ask for the same details several times, provide unclear instructions, reject a document without explaining why, and erase the form when the user navigates backward.

A better system can preserve entered information, validate fields as users complete them, explain document requirements, and display progress through the application. These improvements do not eliminate the university's need for accurate information. They move avoidable complexity away from the user and into the system's design.

This principle is also important in cybersecurity. Consider multifactor authentication. Requiring a second verification factor introduces an additional step but that step serves a security purpose. Designers can make the process more understandable through clear instructions, accessible authentication options, helpful recovery procedures and accurate feedback.

6. Two Principles from Designing with the Mind in Mind

Principle One: Recognition Is Easier Than Recall

The first principle is that recognition is generally easier than recalling information from memory. An interface that displays available actions reduces the need to remember commands or instructions. For example, a form that labels a field "University Student ID" is more helpful than one that displays an unexplained abbreviation.

Similarly, a password manager can help users access unique passwords without requiring them to memorize every password. This supports human memory limitations while encouraging stronger security practices.

Principle Two: Human Attention and Memory Are Limited

The second principle is that attention and memory are limited resources. A screen filled with competing colors, unnecessary notifications, unexplained icons and multiple equally prominent buttons forces users to decide what deserves attention before they can complete their task.

For example, imagine a university portal displaying registration deadlines, course announcements, financial information, technical alerts and promotional messages in one crowded area. Users may struggle to identify the information that matters most.

A more effective design groups related information, establishes a clear visual hierarchy, and emphasizes important actions without making everything appear urgent.

7. How Poor Design Creates Unnecessary Cognitive Load

Poor design becomes particularly noticeable when users must concentrate on the interface instead of the task they intended to complete.

One example is an online form that provides vague instructions. If a date field rejects a value without explaining the required format, users must guess whether the system expects month/day/year or day/month/year. A simple instruction and a clear example could prevent repeated errors.

Another example is an application that uses icons without labels. Experienced users may recognize the symbols but new users might have to click several of them to discover their purpose. Adding short text labels can reduce uncertainty and help people learn the system.

A third example is a website that loses entered information when users return to a previous page. This forces users to remember and re-enter details they have already provided.

I can relate these examples to academic work, where students frequently move between course materials, assignment instructions, uploaded files, and submission pages. If instructions are difficult to locate or the submission status is unclear, students must spend additional time managing the technology instead of focusing on the assignment.

These problems also affect accessibility. Users with limited experience with technology or who are working under time pressure may face greater difficulties when an interface relies on hidden controls, unexplained symbols, or memory-intensive workflows.

In my view, one of the most important questions in usability testing is not simply, "Did the user finish the task?" It is also, "How much unnecessary effort did the user need to finish it?"

8. Designing Digital Systems That Respect Users

Usable systems should support human abilities instead of expecting people to compensate for poor design. Designers should make important information visible when users need it, maintain consistent interfaces, provide timely feedback and reduce avoidable complexity without removing necessary safeguards.

Usability should also be evaluated through actual interaction. Designers should observe whether people can complete realistic tasks, understand what the interface communicates, recover from errors and recognize when an action has succeeded.

These strategies are not merely about convenience. They can improve reliability, reduce mistakes and make digital services more inclusive. In cybersecurity, clear warnings and understandable authentication workflows can help people make better decisions without weakening essential protections.

Conclusion: Good Design Makes Technology Feel Understandable

Usability depends on more than technical functionality. It depends on how people remember information, develop expectations, form habits and interpret feedback.

These ideas have changed how I evaluate digital systems. Instead of judging an application only by its appearance or whether its features work, I can also consider whether it matches users' mental models, supports familiar habits, communicates clearly and allows people to recover from mistakes.

Ultimately, the best digital systems do not require users to become experts just to accomplish ordinary tasks. They make important information recognizable, interactions predictable and outcomes understandable. Good design may require additional effort from developers but that effort can save users time, reduce frustration and improve trust.

A usable system does not eliminate the need for human thinking. It helps people spend their thinking on the things that matter.

How Visual Design and UX Laws Shape the Way We Use Digital Interfaces

Why layout, interaction and human psychology matter when designing digital experiences.

Design Is More Than Appearance

When people interact with a website, mobile application or other digital platform, they usually do not think about the design decisions happening behind the screen. They simply expect to find information, understand available options and complete their tasks without unnecessary difficulty. However, the way an interface is organized can significantly influence what people notice, where they click, how they make decisions and whether they continue using a product.

User experience (UX) design is not just about making a website look attractive. It involves understanding how people perceive information, use memory, direct attention and interact with technology. Elements such as navigation menus, buttons, colors, spacing, typography and feedback messages all contribute to the overall experience. A well-designed interface guides users toward their goals, while a poorly designed one can create confusion, frustration and mistakes.

While studying interface design, I realized that many design decisions are connected to human psychology. Jeff Johnson's Designing with the Mind in Mind explains how perception, attention, memory and decision making influence interface use (Johnson, 2020). Jon Yablonski's Laws of UX connects familiar design principles to psychological concepts and shows how they can be applied to digital products (Yablonski, 2024).

In this post, I explore how visual hierarchy and interaction design influence user behavior, discuss five UX laws and reflect on a personal experience using GitHub Pages while building my professional portfolio.

1. How Interface Layout Influences User Behavior

Interface layout determines how information is presented and how users move through a digital product. When a website has a clear structure, users can quickly understand where they are, what information is available and what actions they can take. When the structure is inconsistent, users may spend more time searching for basic information instead of completing their original task.

One important concept is visual hierarchy . Visual hierarchy refers to arranging interface elements according to their importance. Designers use heading size, color, contrast, spacing, alignment and position to communicate which elements deserve attention first. For example, on a university website, course registration, financial aid, academic schedules and student resources should be easy to locate.

Johnson (2020) explains that people naturally perceive structure and patterns. Grouping related information, using consistent headings and separating unrelated sections can make an interface easier to understand. This is not merely a visual preference, it helps users form a mental model of how the website is organized.

Another important factor is cognitive load or the mental effort required to process information. Users have limited attention and working memory. When a page presents too many choices, instructions or fields at once, users must spend additional effort understanding the interface.

For example, a long registration form with unnecessary questions may discourage someone from completing it. A better design divides the form into logical sections, explains unfamiliar terms and asks only for information that is needed.

In my view, a good layout should make the next step reasonably obvious without forcing users to study the page. Good visual hierarchy guides attention; it should not hide important information or pressure users into taking an action they did not intend.

2. How Control Design Affects Interaction

Controls are the interactive elements that allow users to communicate with a digital system. Examples include buttons, dropdown menus, search fields, checkboxes, sliders and navigation links. Their labels, size, placement and feedback can affect whether people understand what will happen when they interact with them.

For instance, a button labeled "Submit Application" communicates a specific action. A button labeled only "Continue" may be less clear if the user does not know what comes next. Good control design should communicate what action is available, what result the user can expect and how the system will respond afterward.

Feedback is especially important. Imagine submitting an online payment without receiving a confirmation message. Even if the payment succeeds, the user may click the button again because there is no visible indication of what happened.

A confirmation message, progress indicator or clear error notification helps users understand the system's status. Nielsen's usability heuristics identify visibility of system status as a core usability principle: the interface should keep users informed through timely and understandable feedback (Nielsen, 2024).

Controls should also be consistent. If a blue button means "Save" on one page but "Delete" on another, users have to stop and reconsider their assumptions. Clear labels, predictable placement, and consistent interaction patterns reduce uncertainty.

3. Five UX Laws and Their Influence on Design

UX laws are principles that help explain common patterns in human perception and behavior. They are not rigid rules that guarantee success in every situation. Instead, they provide useful starting points for making design decisions and evaluating whether an interface supports its users.

A. Aesthetic Usability Effect

The Aesthetic Usability Effect describes how people often perceive visually attractive interfaces as easier to use, even when the actual usability may not be better.

Imagine two websites offering the same service. The first uses consistent colors, readable typography, organized sections and appropriate images. The second has crowded content, inconsistent spacing and outdated visual elements. Many users will probably feel more comfortable with the first website before they have tested either one.

This effect matters because first impressions influence trust and willingness to explore. However, attractive design does not automatically mean effective design. A beautiful website with confusing navigation, inaccessible text or unclear buttons can still be frustrating.

B. Von Restorff Effect

The Von Restorff Effect, also called the isolation effect, suggests that an item that differs from surrounding items is more likely to attract attention and be remembered.

For example, on a page with several neutral-colored controls, a single contrasting "Register" button may stand out. This can help users notice an important action.

The principle is useful for university portals, banking applications and online forms. However, highlighting everything defeats the purpose. If every button is bright, animated or oversized, no single action stands out.

I think the best use of this effect is selective emphasis: highlight actions that are genuinely important, while keeping secondary actions visually quieter

C. Postel's Law

Postel's Law is commonly summarized as be conservative in what you send, be liberal in what you accept. In interface design, this encourages systems to handle reasonable variations in the way people enter information.

For example, a form asking for a phone number might receive "(206) 555-1234," "206-555-1234," or "2065551234." If these represent the same valid number, the interface should not reject an entry merely because the punctuation differs.

The same idea applies to search. A helpful search feature can ignore unnecessary capitalization or extra spaces and still return useful results. Flexibility should not mean accepting unsafe or invalid data, it means distinguishing meaningful errors from harmless differences in formatting.

D. Hick's Law

Hick's Law explains that the time needed to make a decision generally increases as the number and complexity of available choices increase.

A navigation menu with five clearly grouped categories may be easier to scan than a menu with twenty-five unrelated links. When users face too many choices at once, they may take longer to decide or abandon the task.

This does not mean designers should remove useful options. Instead, they can group choices into meaningful categories, provide search and reveal advanced options when they are needed. For example, a course registration system could organize courses by department, subject or academic level rather than showing every course in one long list.

E. Fitts's Law

Fitts's Law describes the relationship between the size and distance of a target and the time needed to reach it. In interface design, important buttons should be large enough to select comfortably and positioned where users can find and reach them.

A mobile banking application should make frequent actions such as checking balances or transferring money, easy to locate. Tiny buttons placed close together can lead to accidental taps, especially on a phone.

This principle also connects to accessibility. The World Wide Web Consortium's Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.2 include criteria addressing target size, keyboard operation, focus visibility and other barriers

First, the experience raised a system-status and feedback issue. The message made me uncertain about what was required to continue. A more helpful interface would distinguish clearly between features that require a paid plan and features available on the free plan, then explain the exact next step.

Second, the experience connects to recognition rather than recall. Johnson (2020) explains that recognizing visible information is generally easier than remembering information or instructions from elsewhere. Rather than expecting new users to understand hosting terminology, an interface should provide contextual explanations beside the relevant setting.

Third, the experience demonstrates the value of error prevention and recovery. When users encounter a restriction or configuration problem, the interface should explain why it happened and offer a practical way forward. Nielsen (2024) emphasizes helping users recognize, diagnose and recover from errors.

After reviewing the available documentation and checking the repository configuration, I learned that GitHub Pages can be used with public repositories on supported plans, provided the repository and deployment settings meet the requirements. The important lesson was not that the platform was unusable; it was that the message and my understanding of the workflow did not match.

This experience taught me that even a technically capable platform can create a frustrating experience when it does not communicate clearly. People who are new to a platform should not have to know its internal structure before they can complete a basic task. Good interfaces make the next step discoverable and explain problems in language users can understand.

5. Accessibility and Inclusive Design

A website may look attractive and work correctly for some users while creating barriers for others. People interact with technology using different devices, screen sizes, input methods and assistive technologies.

A person using a screen reader depends on meaningful labels and properly structured headings. Someone navigating with a keyboard needs visible focus indicators and controls that can be reached without a mouse. People with low vision may need sufficient text contrast and the ability to zoom.

Color is another important consideration. If a website communicates important information only through red or green, some users may not be able to distinguish the meaning. Designers can combine color with text labels, icons, or patterns.

The W3C's WCAG 2.2 provides recommendations for making web content more accessible, including guidance on text alternatives, keyboard access, contrast, focus and target size (W3C, 2024).

Accessibility should be considered throughout the design process rather than added after a website is complete. It also benefits users who do not identify as having a disability. Captions help people in noisy environments, clear headings help people scan long pages and larger touch targets help anyone using a phone while moving.

6. Applying UX Principles to Better Digital Experiences

Effective interface design requires a balance between appearance, functionality and human behavior. Designers should begin by understanding what users want to accomplish. They can then organize information logically, make important controls easy to identify, provide meaningful feedback and test the interface with people who represent its intended audience.

For a university student portal, I would apply these ideas in practical ways. I would use visual hierarchy to make course registration, financial information and academic resources easy to locate. I would apply the Aesthetic Usability Effect by using consistent typography, spacing, and colors, while checking that the design remains functional.

I would use the Von Restorff Effect to emphasize only the most important actions. I would apply Postel's Law by accepting reasonable variations in form input. Hick's Law would guide me to group navigation choices, and Fitts's Law would encourage sufficiently large controls in locations that are easy to reach.

I would also test the portal with students who have different levels of technical experience and use different devices. A designer may believe that a navigation menu is simple, but actual users may interpret its labels differently. Observing people complete common tasks can reveal where they hesitate, make mistakes, or need additional information.

Designing Around Human Behavior

Visual and interaction design have a significant influence on how people navigate digital interfaces. Layout determines how information is organized, while controls determine how users interact with available features. Together, these elements influence attention, decision-making, accessibility, and satisfaction.

The Aesthetic Usability Effect, Von Restorff Effect, Postel's Law, Hick's Law, and Fitts's Law offer useful ways to understand why particular design approaches may work better than others. Johnson's discussion of perception, attention, and memory also shows why interface decisions should reflect how people process information.

My experience with GitHub Pages helped me understand that even a technically capable platform can become frustrating when its interface does not communicate clearly. A good digital experience should reduce uncertainty, provide useful feedback, prevent avoidable errors and guide users toward their goals.

Ultimately, the best interfaces are not necessarily the most complicated or visually impressive. They are the ones that understand human behavior, respect users' needs, and make everyday tasks easier.

Designing Meaningful Digital Experiences

How timing, motion, feedback and ethical design influence the way people interact with technology

Creating Better User Experiences

Have you ever clicked a button on a website and wondered whether anything happened? Or waited for a page to load without knowing what was going on? These small situations may seem unimportant but they can significantly influence how people feel about a digital product.

User experience is more than creating attractive websites and applications. It involves understanding human behavior, expectations, emotions and the decisions people make while interacting with technology. A well-designed interface should help users complete tasks easily while making them feel comfortable, confident and in control.

Interaction patterns such as feedback timing, responsiveness, motion and error prevention play an important role in achieving this goal.I will explore these concepts, discuss important UX laws and explain why ethical design should always be part of the user experience.

The Importance of Feedback Timing

Feedback is one of the most important elements of interaction design. Whenever users perform an action, they expect the system to respond. For example, when someone submits an online form, uploads a document or makes a payment, they want to know whether the action was successful.

The timing of this feedback can make a significant difference. If a website takes too long to respond without showing any indication of activity, users may become confused or frustrated. They might click the same button multiple times because they are unsure whether their first action worked.

Consider an online payment system. After clicking the payment button, a user expects confirmation. If nothing appears for several seconds, the user may think the payment failed and submit it again. This could potentially create duplicate transactions. A simple loading indicator followed by a confirmation message can prevent this confusion.

Nielsen (1994) explains that visibility of system status is an important usability heuristic. A system should keep users informed about what is happening through appropriate feedback within a reasonable time.

I have experienced similar situations when using websites and online learning platforms. Sometimes, after submitting an assignment or completing an online form, the page takes time to respond. Without a confirmation message, I become uncertain whether my action was completed successfully. This experience shows how even a small feedback message can improve confidence and reduce unnecessary stress.

Good feedback should be immediate when possible, clear and relevant to the action. When an action requires more processing time, a progress indicator can help users understand that the system is still working.

Three Important UX Laws

UX laws are principles based on human psychology and behavior. They help designers understand how people interact with digital interfaces and make better design decisions. Three particularly useful principles are Fitts's Law, Hick's Law and the Doherty Threshold.

Fitts's Law: Making Interaction Easier

Fitts's Law explains that the time required to reach a target depends on its size and distance. Larger targets that are easier to reach generally require less effort to select. This principle is especially important when designing buttons, menus and interactive elements.

For example, a large checkout button on an online shopping website is easier to select than a tiny button hidden in a corner. This is particularly important for mobile users who interact with screens using their fingers.

Yablonski (2020) discusses Fitts's Law as an important principle for designing interfaces that reduce interaction effort. Designers should make important interactive elements easy to locate and select.

In my opinion, this principle is useful because users should not have to struggle to find basic functions. A well positioned button can make an application easier to use and reduce accidental selections.

Hick's Law: Reducing Decision Time

Hick's Law describes how the time required to make a decision increases as the number of available choices increases.

Imagine visiting a website with twenty-five different navigation options displayed at once. A new visitor may struggle to understand where to go. However, if those options are organized into meaningful categories, users can find information more easily.

I have noticed this when using websites with complicated navigation menus. When too many options appear together, finding a specific feature becomes difficult. A simpler menu makes the experience more comfortable.

This does not mean that designers should remove every additional option. Instead, they should organize choices logically and show users the most relevant information at the appropriate time.

Improving Responsiveness

The Doherty Threshold emphasizes the importance of responsive systems. It is commonly associated with a response time of approximately 400 milliseconds, beyond which users may begin to notice delays and experience interruptions in their workflow.

For example, when searching for information, users appreciate seeing results quickly. If a website takes too long to respond, they may lose interest or leave the page.

Designers can improve responsiveness by optimizing website performance, reducing unnecessary loading time and providing immediate visual feedback. Even when a process takes longer, a progress indicator can help users understand what is happening.

The Role of Motion and Animation

Motion is another important part of modern interaction design. Animations can guide attention, communicate changes and help users understand relationships between different interface elements.

For example, when a user clicks a menu icon and the navigation panel smoothly opens, the movement helps explain what happened. Similarly, a small animation when adding an item to a shopping cart confirms that the action was successful.

Motion can also help users understand transitions between different screens. Instead of suddenly displaying an entirely different interface, a smooth transition can communicate how one screen connects to another.

Google's Material Design motion guidance explains that meaningful motion can communicate relationships and provide continuity between interface states.

However, animation should always have a purpose. Excessive movement, unnecessary transitions and constantly changing elements can distract users. They can also create accessibility problems for people who experience motion sensitivity.

In my experience, I prefer websites with subtle animations rather than interfaces where every element moves. A smooth transition can make an application feel professional but excessive animation can make it difficult to focus on the actual task.

Therefore, designers should use motion carefully. Animations should support the user's goals rather than become an unnecessary source of distraction. Providing options to reduce motion can also improve accessibility.

Error Prevention and User Confidence

A good digital experience should not only help users complete tasks but also prevent avoidable mistakes. Error prevention is especially important when users interact with financial services, healthcare platforms, educational systems and applications involving sensitive information.

Consider an online registration form. If a user forgets to enter an email address, the system should clearly identify the missing field and explain how to correct it. This is better than allowing the user to submit the form and displaying a confusing error afterward.

Another example is a confirmation message before permanently deleting a file. Giving users an opportunity to review their actions can prevent accidental data loss.

Nielsen (1994) identifies error prevention as another important usability heuristic. Interfaces should be designed to prevent problems before they happen whenever possible.

Error messages should also be understandable. Instead of displaying technical error codes, a website should explain what went wrong and provide clear instructions for resolving the issue. Users should feel supported rather than blamed when something does not work.

I believe error prevention is particularly important because people use technology with different levels of experience. A beginner should be able to complete basic tasks without needing technical knowledge. Good design makes the process understandable for everyone.

Ethical UX Design and Persuasive Techniques

Designers have considerable influence over how people make decisions online. Colors, notifications, button placement, animations and recommendations can all influence user behavior.

Persuasive design is not necessarily unethical. For example, a reminder to save unfinished work can help users avoid losing important information. A progress indicator can encourage someone to complete an online learning activity.

However, persuasion becomes problematic when designers intentionally manipulate users. Examples include hiding subscription cancellation options, making advertisements look like system notifications, using misleading countdown timers or making it difficult to reject cookies.

These practices are often associated with dark patterns. They prioritize business interests over the user's ability to make informed decisions. Although they may increase short-term engagement or sales, they can damage trust and create negative experiences.

Ethical design should prioritize transparency, user control, accessibility, privacy and informed consent. Users should understand what they are agreeing to and should be able to change their decisions without unnecessary difficulty.

For example, a subscription website should make its cancellation process as understandable as its registration process. A privacy setting should provide meaningful choices rather than encouraging users to share more information than necessary.

I believe this is particularly important when designing applications that handle personal information. Users should not feel pressured to share information or accept unnecessary permissions simply because the interface makes other options difficult to find.

A successful digital product should earn users trust through honest communication and useful features, not through manipulation.

6. Connecting UX Design to My Own Experience

As an IT Management student specializing in cybersecurity, I have become increasingly interested in how technology affects people's decisions and behavior.

In cybersecurity, users are often considered an important part of an organization's security environment. Even when an organization has strong security tools, confusing interfaces can encourage users to make mistakes.

For example, consider a multi-factor authentication process. If the instructions are unclear or confirmation takes too long, users may become frustrated and attempt to bypass security steps. A better design should provide clear instructions, immediate feedback and understandable error messages.

Another example is a password reset process. Users should receive clear information about password requirements, successful changes and any security steps they need to complete. Without proper feedback, users may repeatedly attempt the same action or become confused.

This demonstrates that usability and security should work together. A secure application should not make users feel confused or powerless. Instead, good design should help users make safer decisions naturally.

I have learned that technology is most effective when people can understand and use it confidently. This is why UX principles are important not only for designers but also for IT professionals who develop, manage and secure digital systems.

Key Takeaways for Better UX Design

After exploring these interaction patterns and UX principles, I believe designers should focus on several important practices:

  • Provide timely feedback: Let users know when an action has been received, processed, or completed.
  • Make interactions simple: Use appropriately sized buttons and clear navigation to reduce user effort.
  • Reduce unnecessary choices: Organize information so users can make decisions without feeling overwhelmed.
  • Use motion meaningfully: Apply animations to communicate changes rather than distract users.
  • Prevent errors: Provide clear instructions, validation and confirmation before important actions.
  • Respect user autonomy: Avoid manipulative design and give users meaningful control over their decisions.

    • Designing for People, Not Just Screens

    Meaningful user experience design goes beyond visual appearance. Timing, responsiveness, motion and error prevention all influence how users feel when interacting with digital products.

    UX laws such as Fitts's Law, Hick's Law and the Doherty Threshold provide useful guidance for understanding human behavior and creating more effective interfaces. However, applying these principles should not be the only goal. Designers must also consider accessibility, privacy, transparency, and ethical responsibility.

    My biggest takeaway is that successful interaction design should make people feel confident, respected and in control. Technology should support human needs rather than manipulate human behavior.

    Ultimately, the best digital experiences are not simply those that encourage people to click more buttons. They are the ones that help people accomplish their goals safely, comfortably, and effectively.
