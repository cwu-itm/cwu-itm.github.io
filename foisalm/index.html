Understanding Systems Thinking

When we think about engineering, we often imagine designing a mechine, developing software or building a product. Successful engineering is not only about making individual components work. It is about ensuring that all components,people,processes and technologies work together as one complete system.

A system can appear successful at the individual component level but still fail as a whole.This is why systems thinking and MBSE are important. In my understanding, Syatems thinking teaches us to look beyond individual parts and understand their relationships while MBSE provides a structured way to represent, communicate and manage those relationships throughout a system's lifecycle

What is Model Based Systems Engineering ?

A system is a collection of connected elements that work together to achieve a particular purpose. These elements may include people hardware, software, processes and information. Understanding a system requires examining its elements, stakeholders, attributes boundaries, needs and constraints (Holt, 2019).

For example, consider a hospital's information technology system. It is not simply a computer network or electronic health record software. It includes patient databases, medical devices, healthcare professionals, cybersecurity controls, network infrastructure and administrative processes.

If one part fails, the impact may spread throughout the organization. A network outage could prevent healthcare professionals from accessing patient records, delay communication and affect daily operations. This example demonstrates that systems thinking is about recognizing relationships and consequences rather than focusing only on isolated problems.

My key insight is that solving one problem does not always mean improving the entire system. Sometimes, improving one component creates new problems elsewhere. Therefore, engineers must consider the broader environment, stakeholder needs, and possible unintended consequences before making decisions.

Managing Complexity, Communication and Context

There are three major challenges of systems engineering: complexity, communication and context. These challenges explain why engineering a large system is more difficult than simply connecting its individual parts (Holt, 2019).

Complexity: Modern systems contain many interconnected components. A small change in one component can affect other parts of the system.

Communication: Different stakeholders may have different technical knowledge and priorities. Without clear communication, a team may develop something that does not fully meet the user's needs.

Context: A system does not operate in isolation. It interacts with users, organizations, regulations and other systems. Engineers must understand these external relationships before making decisions.

From Documents to Connected Models

Traditional systems engineering often relies on separate documents, spreadsheets and diagrams. When a requirement changes, several documents may need to be updated manually. This can create confusion, outdated information and communication gaps.

Model Based Systems Engineering addresses this challenge by using a connected system model as a central source of information. MBSE uses models to represent system structure, behavior, requirements and relationships. A model is not the actual system. Instead, it is a simplified representation that helps people understand and analyze the real system (Holt, 2019).

For example, when designing a hospital cybersecurity system, engineers could use Systems Modeling Language diagrams to represent the network structure, connected devices, security requirements, access controls and responses to security incidents.

Understanding Models, Views and Ontology

Another concept I found interesting is the relationship between models, views and ontology. A model represents the overall system, while a view presents specific information for a particular stakeholder.

All these views should represent the same underlying system without contradicting each other. Ontology is important because it establishes shared concepts and relationships. A common vocabulary helps ensure that everyone understands what the model represents.

Why MBSE Matters in Modern Engineering

As systems become more interconnected, managing requirements and relationships through separate documents becomes increasingly challenging. The International Council on Systems Engineering describes MBSE as the formal application of modeling to support requirements, design, analysis, verification and validation throughout a system's lifecycle.

How MBSE Improves Communication and Decision-Making

One of the most useful benefits of Model-Based Systems Engineering is that it creates a shared understanding among different stakeholders. In a large project, software developers, network engineers, managers, security analysts and end users may all have different expectations. When information is shared only through long documents or emails, misunderstandings can occur. MBSE uses models and diagrams to represent system components, relationships, requirements and behavior in a visual format. For example, a hospital cybersecurity team could use a system model to show how patient records move between a medical workstation, an application server and a database. This model could help technical staff understand where security controls should be applied and help managers understand why certain investments are necessary. However, a model is only useful when it is accurate, updated and understandable to the people using it.

Systems Thinking in Healthcare Cybersecurity

Systems thinking is especially important in healthcare because hospitals depend on many connected systems to provide safe and effective patient care. For example, an electronic health record system is not just a software application. It connects doctors, nurses, patients, medical devices, databases, networks and administrative departments. If one part of this system fails, it can affect the entire hospital. A network outage may prevent medical staff from accessing patient records, while a cybersecurity attack could expose sensitive patient information or interrupt medical services. Systems thinking helps security professionals understand these relationships instead of focusing only on individual computers or applications. By examining the entire environment, organizations can identify risks, understand how one failure may affect other components and develop better security plans. This approach also reminds us that technology, people and procedures must work together to protect healthcare information.

Is MBSE Always the Solution?

Although MBSE provides important advantages, I do not believe that simply adopting modeling software guarantees a successful project. A model is only as reliable as the information, assumptions and people behind it.

If a model is outdated, incomplete or too complicated, it may create additional confusion instead of solving communication problems. Teams also need training, agreed modeling practices and suitable tools.

Therefore, I believe engineers should focus on creating models that answer real questions rather than creating diagrams just to satisfy a process. The purpose of MBSE should be better understanding and decision-making not simply replacing documents with software.

Challenges and Limitations of Model-Based Systems Engineering

Although MBSE offers many advantages, it is not a perfect solution for every project. Creating detailed models requires time, technical knowledge, appropriate tools and cooperation among stakeholders. Smaller organizations may not have enough resources or trained employees to implement a complete MBSE process. Another challenge is that models can become outdated when system requirements or technologies change. If team members continue using an old model, it may create confusion rather than improve communication. In cybersecurity, this could be especially dangerous because networks, applications and threats change frequently. I believe organizations should begin with the most important parts of a system and gradually expand their models as needed. The goal should not be to create the largest or most complicated model. Instead, the goal should be to create useful models that support understanding, decision-making and system improvement.

My Perspective on Systems Thinking and MBSE

As an IT Management student focusing on cybersecurity, I believe systems thinking and MBSE can help me prepare for future responsibilities in information security. Cybersecurity is not limited to installing antivirus software, configuring firewalls or monitoring suspicious network activity. Security professionals must understand how business operations, users, networks, applications and security policies depend on one another. For example, when designing security for a hospital, protecting patient data is important but maintaining access to critical medical systems is equally important. A security control that is too restrictive could interrupt the work of healthcare professionals. This demonstrates why security decisions must consider the entire system and its stakeholders. In my opinion, the biggest lesson from this module is that effective engineering requires more than technical knowledge. It requires understanding relationships, communicating clearly and considering the consequences of decisions before implementing them.

Understanding Systems life Cycle Models

Systems engineering is not simply about designing and building a product. It also involves understanding how a system develops, operates, changes and eventually reaches retirement. Three commonly discussed models are the linear, iterative and incremental models. Each provides a different approach to organizing development activities and meeting stakeholder needs.

Linear Model: Structure and Predictability

linear model commonly associated with the waterfall approach, organizes development into sequential stages. A project generally moves from requirements and design to implementation, testing and delivery. One advantage of this model is that each stage has clear objectives and deliverables. This structure makes planning, budgeting, documentation and progress tracking easier. For example, an organization replacing well-understood equipment may benefit from a linear approach when its requirements are stable. However, this model becomes less effective when stakeholder needs change frequently. If a major problem is discovered during testing, returning to an earlier stage can require significant time and money. Therefore, a linear model is most appropriate when requirements and technology are sufficiently understood before development begins.

Iterative Model: Learning Through Repetition

Iterative model allows engineers to revisit and improve a system through repeated development cycles. Instead of assuming that the first design will be correct, engineers use feedback, testing and evaluation to identify areas for improvement. For example, a cybersecurity team developing a threat-monitoring dashboard could begin with a basic version, evaluate how analysts use it and improve its alerts and reporting features in later iterations. This approach helps teams discover problems earlier and respond to changing needs. The Agile approach also emphasizes feedback, adaptation and frequent delivery of useful results. In my view, iteration is especially valuable when a project involves uncertainty because engineers can learn from evidence instead of relying entirely on their original assumptions.

Incremental Model: Delivering Value in Parts

Incremental model focuses on delivering a system in separate, usable portions. The overall requirements guide development, while individual increments provide additional capabilities over time. For example, a hospital information system might first introduce secure user authentication then add access control and audit logging and later integrate security alerts and reporting. Each increment adds functionality toward the larger system. This approach helps organizations obtain useful capabilities before the entire system is complete. However, engineers must plan carefully to ensure that individual increments integrate properly and support the system's overall requirements. Systems engineering standards emphasize considering system needs and activities throughout the system life cycle.

Comparing the Models and Applying Systems Thinking

Although these models have different characteristics, none is automatically the best choice for every project.linear model emphasizes sequential progress, Iterative model emphasizes repeated learning and refinement and the incremental model emphasizes delivering capabilities in manageable portions. These approaches can also be combined when project conditions require it. For instance, a healthcare cybersecurity project might use a defined overall architecture, develop monitoring capabilities incrementally and improve detection rules iteratively based on testing results. This combination provides structure without preventing adaptation. Engineers must also consider maintenance, technology changes, stakeholder expectations and retirement instead of focusing only on initial delivery (Holt, 2019).

Choosing the Right Life Cycle Model

My main takeaway is that choosing a life cycle model is a strategic engineering decision not just a project management preference. A model influences how teams manage risk, communicate with stakeholders respond to unexpected problems and allocate resources. As someone interested in cybersecurity, I see particular value in combining structured planning with continuous improvement. Security threats evolve, software changes and new vulnerabilities emerge after a system enters operation. A team that never revisits its original design may miss important risks. Frequent changes without a clear architecture can create inconsistencies and new weaknesses. Systems engineers should choose a model based on project needs, evidence and risk while considering the system's entire life cycle.

Introducing the Concept of Human Centered Design

Human-centered design is an approach to problem-solving that begins with understanding people and their needs before deciding what solution to create. Instead of assuming that designers already know what people need, this approach encourages them to listen, observe, ask questions, and learn directly from the people who will use or be affected by a solution. Human-centered design can be used to develop products, digital applications, public services, healthcare systems and solutions to social problems. Its central idea is that a successful solution should not only work technically but should also make sense for the people who will use it

Designers should therefore work with communities and learn from their experiences rather than developing solutions based only on their own assumptions. The guide presents three main phases of human-centered design: Inspiration, Ideation and Implementation. It also introduces seven mindsets: empathy, optimism, iteration, creative confidence, making, embracing ambiguity and learning from failure.

What I find most interesting about human-centered design is that it changes the way a problem is understood. A designer might initially believe that a complicated application needs a more attractive interface. After speaking with users, however, the designer might discover that the real problem is confusing instructions, limited internet access or a lack of trust in the service. This shows that solving a problem effectively requires understanding the people experiencing it before selecting a solution.

My main argument is that human-centered design improves problem-solving by connecting creativity with real human experiences. However, it should not be treated as a guarantee of success. Designers must also consider accessibility, privacy, cost, technical limitation, and the experiences of people who are not always represented in research. Human-centered design is most valuable when listening to people leads to meaningful action, careful testing and continuous improvement.

What Is Human Centered Design?

Human-centered design places people at the center of the design process. It considers what people need, what they value, the challenges they face and how they interact with products or services in their everyday lives. According to IDEO.org (2015), designers should understand the people they are designing for, generate possible solutions and test their ideas before implementing them on a larger scale. This process helps designers move beyond assumptions and develop solutions grounded in actual experiences.

Human-centered design is not limited to professional designers. Students, engineers, business owners, software developers and community organizations can use it to solve problems. For example, a university developing a student portal should consider more than the technical features of the website. It should understand how students register for classes, find assignments, contact instructors and access important academic information. Students may have different levels of technical experience, different accessibility needs and different devices or internet connections. Understanding these differences can help the university design a service that works for a wider range of people.

IDEO describes human-centered design as an approach that starts with people's needs and experiences and connects them with technological possibilities and organizational requirements. This is important because a solution must balance several considerations. It should be desirable for the people using it, feasible to develop and maintain and viable within the organization's resources and responsibilities.

For example, a mobile application might be useful to students but too expensive for a university to maintain. Another application might be affordable and technically advanced but difficult for students with disabilities to use. Neither solution fully addresses the problem. Human-centered design encourages teams to consider these concerns together rather than focusing on one measure of success.

An important distinction is that human-centered design does not mean giving every user exactly what they request. Users may suggest different or conflicting features and some requests may not address the underlying problem. Instead, designers should investigate why people want a particular feature, identify common needs, and evaluate possible solutions. The goal is to understand the problem deeply enough to create an appropriate response.

Phase One: Inspiration

The Inspiration phase focuses on understanding people and the challenges they experience. Describes this phase as an opportunity to observe people's lives, listen to their hopes and desires and learn more about the design challenge. Designers should remain curious instead of immediately deciding what the solution must be.

Several activities can support this phase, including interviews, observation, conversations and research into people's everyday experiences. Interviews allow designers to ask questions about people's needs and frustrations. Observation can reveal behaviors that people may forget to mention or may not recognize themselves. Combining these approaches helps designers develop a more complete understanding of the problem.

Imagine that a college notices that many students miss assignment deadlines. An initial assumption might be that students need more reminders. However, interviews and observation could reveal several different causes. Some students might struggle to find assignment instructions, others may not understand how the learning platform is organized and some may have difficulty accessing the platform using a mobile device. Additional reminders would not necessarily solve these problems.

This example demonstrates why empathy matters. Describes empathy as the ability to understand other people's lives and consider problems from their perspectives. Empathy does not mean simply feeling sorry for someone. It involves making a genuine effort to understand their circumstances without assuming that their experiences are the same as the designer's.

My reflection is that the Inspiration phase may be the most important part of the process because it determines how the problem is framed. If designers misunderstand the problem, even an excellent technical solution may produce disappointing results. Spending time learning about users may initially appear slower than immediately building a product but it can prevent a team from investing resources in a solution that does not address the real need.

Ideation

After gathering information, designers move into Ideation. During this phase, the team organizes its observations, identifies patterns and develops different possible solutions.Emphasizes generating many ideas before deciding which ones deserve further attention. This approach encourages teams to explore possibilities rather than accepting the first idea that seems reasonable.

For example, if students struggle to locate assignments in a learning platform, a team might consider several solutions. It could simplify the navigation menu, create a weekly assignment summary, introduce clearer notifications or develop a dashboard showing upcoming deadlines. The team could also consider improving the instructions provided by instructors. These ideas address different aspects of the problem and should be evaluated according to user needs and practical limitations.

Brainstorming can be useful because it allows team members to contribute different perspectives. However, generating many ideas is not enough. The team must examine which ideas are likely to solve the problem which are technically possible and which can be supported with available resources. Feedback from users can help the team identify weaknesses and decide which concepts should be developed further.

Implementation

Implementation focuses on turning ideas into solutions that can be tested, delivered and maintained. This phase involves bringing a solution to life and considering how to maximize its impact. A promising idea must move beyond discussion and become something people can experience and evaluate.

Prototyping is an important part of this work. A prototype is an early representation of a proposed solution. It might be a paper drawing, a clickable interface, a basic website, or a small-scale version of a service. A prototype does not need to be perfect. Its purpose is to make an idea tangible so that designers and users can examine it, discover problems, and suggest improvements.

Returning to the student portal example, a development team could create a simple dashboard that displays upcoming assignments and their deadlines. Before building a complete application, the team could ask students to use the prototype and explain what they understand, where they become confused and which information they cannot find. The team could then revise the design based on the results.

The Seven Mindsets of Human-Centered Design

In addition to the three phases, identifies seven mindsets that guide human-centered designers. These mindsets help explain how designers should approach uncertainty, collaboration, creativity and learning.

  1. Empathy: Designers work to understand people's experiences, needs, and perspectives. Empathy helps prevent teams from relying entirely on personal assumptions.
  2. Optimism: Designers approach challenges with the belief that improvement is possible. Optimism encourages exploration, although it should be balanced with realistic evaluation of risks and constraints.
  3. Iteration: Designers repeatedly improve ideas based on feedback and new information. The process does not assume that the first solution will be the best one.
  4. Creative confidence: Designers develop confidence in their ability to explore and contribute ideas. Creativity is treated as a skill that can grow through practice.
  5. Making: Designers turn abstract ideas into something tangible. Building a prototype helps people discuss a real example rather than an idea that exists only in conversation.
  6. Embracing ambiguity: Designers accept that they may not know the correct answer at the beginning. Remaining open to uncertainty creates room for discovery and alternative approaches.
  7. Learning from failure: Designers treat unsuccessful attempts as opportunities to learn. Testing a weak idea can reveal important information that leads to a better solution.

These mindsets are connected rather than separate rules. Empathy helps designers understand the problem, while embracing ambiguity allows them to explore possible explanations. Creative confidence encourages them to develop ideas, making allows those ideas to be examined and iteration helps refine them. Learning from failure supports the process by showing what does not work. Optimism encourages the team to continue learning instead of giving up when an early attempt is unsuccessful.

I believe iteration and learning from failure are especially important because people often become attached to their first ideas. A team may spend considerable time developing a feature and then hesitate to change it when testing reveals a problem. However, the purpose of user testing is not simply to confirm that the design is correct. It is to discover whether the design actually meets people's needs. A team that listens honestly to criticism has a better opportunity to improve its work.

Challenges and Limitations

Human-centered design offers a valuable way to understand problems but it also has limitations. One challenge is that designers cannot always speak with every person affected by a solution. A small group of interview participants may not represent the experiences of the wider community. If a team mainly interviews people who are comfortable with technology, it might overlook people who have limited digital experience, disabilities, language barriers or unreliable internet access.

This limitation suggests that empathy must be supported by thoughtful research. Designers should consider who is participating, whose perspectives are missing, and whether the findings reflect different situations. They should avoid assuming that one person's experience represents everyone. When possible, teams should involve a diverse range of users and revisit their conclusions as new evidence becomes available.

Applying Human-Centered Design to Cybersecurity

Human-centered design is especially relevant to cybersecurity because security technologies depend on human behavior. An organization may introduce strong password requirements, multifactor authentication and security training, but these measures may not work as intended if employees find them confusing or difficult to use.

Imagine a hospital introducing a new multifactor authentication system. A technology team might focus on preventing unauthorized access. However, nurses and doctors may need to access patient records quickly while working under pressure. If the authentication process is difficult to understand or unreliable, staff may become frustrated or seek unsafe workarounds. A human-centered approach would involve healthcare workers early, observe how authentication fits into their daily work and identify both security requirements and usability concerns.

Conclusion

Human-centered design is a problem-solving approach that begins with people and develops solutions through research, creativity, prototyping and learning. The three phases of Inspiration, Ideation and Implementation provide a useful structure for moving from understanding a challenge to developing and testing a solution. The seven mindsets described by IDEO.org (2015) encourage designers to remain empathetic, optimistic, creative, open to uncertainty, willing to experiment, and ready to learn from failure.

My main takeaway is that the quality of a solution depends partly on how well the problem is understood. A technically impressive product may still fail if it ignores people's real needs or the conditions in which they use it. At the same time, listening to users is not enough on its own. Designers must evaluate evidence, consider people whose voices are missing, respect privacy, and account for practical limitations.
