Thinking Beyond the Parts: Systems Thinking and Model-Based Systems Engineering

Understanding Systems Thinking

Before learning more about systems engineering, I used to think of a system mostly as a collection of parts that work together. That definition is not completely wrong, but systems thinking goes much further. To me, systems thinking means looking at the complete environment around a problem and paying attention not only to the individual parts, but also to the relationships, dependencies, people, processes, and interactions that connect those parts. A system cannot always be understood by separating it into pieces because the behavior of one component may depend on what is happening somewhere else.

This idea became clearer to me while reading Systems Engineering Demystified. Holt explains that one of the main principles of systems theory is that components are better understood through their relationships with each other and with other systems instead of being viewed in isolation (Holt, 2023). That point changed how I think about technical problems. A computer, application, network, user, database, security policy, and cloud service can each work correctly by themselves while the overall digital system still fails because of the way those elements interact.

The International Council on Systems Engineering also describes systems engineering as a holistic approach to complex projects. Instead of concentrating only on one component, the approach considers how the different components work together and follows the system throughout its life cycle (International Council on Systems Engineering [INCOSE], n.d.). I think this is one reason systems thinking is increasingly important in information technology. Digital systems are no longer isolated computers performing one task. They are connected to networks, cloud services, users, databases, APIs, security controls, and other systems. The more connected they become, the more important the relationships become.

Systems Thinking Versus Linear Thinking

The biggest difference I see between systems thinking and traditional linear thinking is the way a problem is approached. Linear thinking often follows a simple path: something happened, there must be one cause, and correcting that cause should solve the problem. This approach can be useful when a problem really is simple. For example, if a monitor is unplugged, reconnecting its power cable may solve the issue immediately. There is no need to build a complex model for every basic problem.

However, many technology problems do not follow a straight line. A problem can have several causes, and one action can create consequences somewhere else. Systems thinking asks more questions. What other components does this depend on? What information moves between them? Who uses the system? What assumptions are being made? What happens if one element changes? What other system is connected to this one?

Holt describes this interconnectedness directly, explaining that system elements interact rather than exist independently and that understanding the relationships between the elements is just as important as understanding the elements themselves (Holt, 2023).

This is where linear thinking can become limiting. Suppose an employee cannot log in to an application. A linear response might be, "The password is wrong, so reset the password." Sometimes that works. But if it does not, the problem could involve the device, the network connection, the identity service, multifactor authentication, account permissions, group membership, security policies, or the application itself. The login screen is only where the failure becomes visible. It does not necessarily tell us where the failure began.

Systems thinking encourages a person to trace those relationships rather than repeatedly changing the most visible component. In that sense, I do not see systems thinking as replacing linear thinking. I see it as expanding it. Simple cause-and-effect reasoning still has a place, but it becomes less reliable as the number of components and interactions increases.

Complexity Is Often in the Connections

One part of the reading that stood out to me was Holt's discussion of complexity. He identifies complexity, communication, and understanding as three major causes of system failure. More importantly, these problems affect one another. Unmanaged complexity can create communication and understanding problems, while poor communication can create even more complexity (Holt, 2023).

I think this is especially relevant to digital systems because their complexity is not always visible. A user may see a very simple interface with only a few buttons, while hundreds of processes and services operate behind it. The simplicity of the screen does not mean that the underlying system is simple.

Holt also makes an important distinction between essential and accidental complexity. Essential complexity naturally belongs to the system, while accidental complexity can be introduced by inefficient people, processes, or tools. He notes that complexity often appears in the relationships between system elements rather than only in the elements themselves (Holt, 2023). This distinction is useful because not every kind of complexity can simply be removed. Some complexity has to be understood and managed. Other complexity can be reduced by improving processes, communication, documentation, or tools.

That gives systems thinking a practical purpose. It is not simply about saying, "Everything is connected." It is about identifying which connections matter and understanding their effects well enough to make better decisions.

Why a Model-Based Approach Makes Sense

Systems thinking helps us recognize complexity, but recognizing complexity does not automatically make it manageable. This is where Model-Based Systems Engineering, or MBSE, becomes useful.

One of the most important ideas I took from Holt's explanation of MBSE is the difference between storing system knowledge in documents and storing it in a model. In traditional systems engineering, information about the system may be distributed across many documents. With MBSE, the model becomes the main representation of the system. Holt describes a model as an abstraction or simplified representation of the actual system (Holt, 2023).

The word simplified is important. At first, it may seem strange to use a simplified model to understand something complex. However, the purpose of a model is not to copy every detail of the real system. If it contained everything, it would become almost as difficult to understand as the system itself. The goal is to capture the information that matters for the particular problem, decision, or stakeholder.

I think of it like a map. A map does not show every tree, person, car, window, or object in a city. It removes information that is unnecessary for its purpose while keeping information such as roads, locations, and connections. Different maps can also show different information. A transportation map, for example, emphasizes something different from a population map even though both describe the same city. MBSE uses a similar idea by allowing different views of one system for different stakeholders.

Holt explains that the information in a model can be organized into views and that each view should exist for a reason and provide value to a stakeholder (Holt, 2023). This prevents modeling from becoming an exercise where engineers create diagrams simply because they can.

Why MBSE Is Useful for Complex Digital Systems

Digital systems are good candidates for MBSE because they usually combine many different types of components. A modern digital environment can contain hardware, software, networks, cloud infrastructure, data, users, policies, security controls, and third-party services. These components also change frequently.

NASA's Systems Engineering Handbook emphasizes the importance of understanding stakeholder expectations because those expectations become the foundation for later system design and requirements. It also discusses the use of model-based approaches within modern systems engineering practices (National Aeronautics and Space Administration [NASA], 2016).

This matters because technical success is not only about making components function. A system must function for the people and organizations that depend on it. A cybersecurity team may care about access control and monitoring. A user may care about speed and convenience. Management may care about cost and availability. An IT support team may care about troubleshooting and maintainability. All of them can be looking at the same system while having different needs.

A model-based approach can represent these different perspectives without treating them as unrelated problems. One view could show system components and their relationships. Another could show user interactions. Another could show requirements. Another could focus on data flows or security boundaries. Because the views belong to the same model, changes can be analyzed across the system instead of remaining trapped in separate documents.

Holt argues that consistency between views is essential. If the different views contradict each other, there is not really a coherent model, only a collection of diagrams (Holt, 2023). I think this is one of the strongest advantages of model-based thinking. A diagram alone is not enough. The real value comes from keeping the different representations connected.

An Example: Enterprise Authentication

An enterprise authentication system is a good example of why systems thinking and MBSE are useful. From the user's point of view, authentication may appear simple: enter a username and password, approve an MFA request, and access an application. But the system behind those few actions can be much more complex.

The user's device must have connectivity. The network must route the request correctly. An identity provider must recognize the account. Authentication policies must determine whether the login is allowed. MFA may depend on another device or service. Authorization rules must determine what resources the user can access. Applications may depend on groups or roles. Logging systems may record the event for security teams. Organizations may also have requirements related to privacy, auditing, and compliance.

If one component changes, other components can be affected. A security team might strengthen authentication requirements to reduce risk, but the change could also affect user experience or create more support requests. Removing an employee from one group might unintentionally remove access to several applications. A network configuration change could look to a user like an authentication problem even though the credentials are correct.

A linear view might focus only on the component where the error appeared. A systems view instead asks how the whole authentication path works and where dependencies exist. A model could help show the device, network, identity provider, MFA service, application, users, and policies as related elements instead of disconnected pieces.

This also relates to Holt's description of structure and behavior. The structure of a model describes what elements exist and how they are related, while behavior describes how events occur, in what order, and under what conditions (Holt, 2023). For authentication, a structural view could show the systems involved, while a behavioral view could show what happens from the moment a user submits credentials until access is granted or denied.

That combination would provide much more information than a list of individual technologies.

Models Should Help, Not Add More Complexity

Even though I see clear benefits in MBSE, I do not think modeling automatically makes a system better. A model can become another source of complexity if people create too much information, use terminology that stakeholders do not understand, or fail to keep the model updated.

This was another useful point in the reading. Holt states that modeling should be used when it adds value. He connects modeling to managing complexity, improving understanding, and improving communication, but he also argues that modeling activities that do not contribute value should not continue (Holt, 2023).

I think this is an important limitation to recognize. It would be easy to assume that "more modeling" means "better engineering." In reality, an extremely detailed model that nobody can understand or maintain may defeat the purpose of systems thinking. The model needs to simplify complexity enough to make relationships clearer without hiding information that is necessary for good decisions.

This is why the stakeholder perspective matters so much. The right model is not necessarily the model with the most information. It is the model that provides the right information to the right people while keeping the different views consistent.

Conclusion

Systems thinking has changed the way I understand technical systems. Instead of looking at a problem only through individual components, it encourages me to consider relationships, dependencies, boundaries, stakeholders, and the larger environment. Linear thinking can still be effective for simple problems, but it becomes less dependable when systems contain many interacting components.

MBSE provides a practical way to apply systems thinking to complex systems. By representing the system through a model and different connected views, teams can make relationships more visible, communicate across different stakeholder perspectives, and better understand how a change in one place could affect another.

For complex digital environments, I think this approach is particularly valuable. Technology continues to become more interconnected, and the most difficult problems are often not located inside a single component. They occur between components, people, processes, and systems. Systems thinking helps us recognize those connections, while MBSE gives us a way to represent and manage them. For me, that is the biggest lesson from this topic: understanding the pieces is necessary, but understanding how the pieces work together is what allows us to understand the system.

Human First: Designing for the Way People Actually See, Think, and Act

A digital interface can technically work and still fail the person using it. A button may perform the correct action, a form may successfully send information, and a website may contain everything a user needs, but none of that guarantees a good experience. If people cannot quickly understand where to look, what to do next, or whether an action is safe, the design has created work for the user. This is why human-centered design matters. To me, designing for people means starting with human abilities and limitations rather than expecting users to adapt to the system.

What Human-Centered Design Means

Human-centered design is an approach that makes the people affected by a system central to design decisions. ISO 9241-210 describes human-centered design as a set of principles and activities used throughout the life cycle of interactive systems (International Organization for Standardization [ISO], 2019). This is important because usability should not be treated as a final layer added after development. Decisions about requirements, navigation, terminology, layout, interaction, and feedback can affect usability long before the product is complete.

IDEO’s Field Guide to Human-Centered Design presents the idea from a more practical and empathetic perspective. It focuses on understanding the people being designed for, generating ideas, prototyping, and learning from real use (IDEO.org, 2015). The guide encourages designers to approach a problem with curiosity rather than assuming that they already know the answer. That mindset changes the role of the designer. Instead of designing for an imagined “average user,” the designer must pay attention to real behaviors, frustrations, environments, and goals.

ISO 9241-210 and IDEO: Two Perspectives on the Same Goal

ISO 9241-210 and IDEO therefore feel complementary to me. ISO provides a structured framework that can help an organization make human-centered design part of an entire development process. IDEO provides methods and mindsets that help a team get closer to the people behind the requirements. One is more formal and process-oriented, while the other feels more exploratory and empathy-driven.

I do not see them as competing approaches. A strong team could use ISO to make sure human-centered activities are planned and repeated, while using IDEO methods to actually understand users and test ideas with them. ISO helps answer the question, “How do we make human-centered design part of our process?” IDEO helps answer, “How do we learn what people actually need?”

Perception and Attention Are Part of the Interface

One of the biggest lessons I take from this module is that users do not experience a screen as a neutral collection of elements. They notice some things before others. They group objects, recognize patterns, ignore distractions, and use prior experience to decide what is probably clickable or important. This means that visual design is not only about making a page attractive. It influences how information is interpreted.

Visual hierarchy is a good example. Nielsen Norman Group explains that hierarchy can guide the eye toward the most important elements through scale, color, contrast, and grouping (Gordon, 2020). Imagine a Canvas assignment page where the assignment title, due date, instructions, rubric, and submission button all have the same size, color, and visual weight. All the information may be present, but the user has to work harder to figure out what matters first.

A clearer design might make the assignment title prominent, place the due date near it, group instructions together, and give the submission action enough contrast to be easy to locate. Nothing about the information itself has changed. What changed is the way the interface directs attention.

The same idea applies to a banking app. If “Transfer Money,” account balance, advertisements, settings, and promotional offers all compete equally for attention, a user trying to complete a transfer may hesitate or choose the wrong control. The early design decision about what receives the strongest visual emphasis directly affects usability.

The World Wide Web Consortium recommends clear page structure, recognizable regions, headings, boundaries, and visual cues so users can understand structure and find important information (World Wide Web Consortium [W3C], n.d.). I think this is where visual hierarchy and empathy connect. A designer who understands that users may be distracted, stressed, inexperienced, visually scanning, or working under time pressure will not assume that people will patiently read every element on a screen.

Attention is limited. A designer cannot force a user to notice everything. The better question is: what deserves attention at this moment? Good design reduces competition for attention so the next important action becomes easier to perceive.

Empathy Changes Early Design Decisions

Empathy is sometimes described as understanding the user, but I think it is more useful when it changes a decision. For example, imagine designing a password reset process. From a technical point of view, the requirement may simply be to verify identity and create a new password. From a human point of view, the user may already be frustrated, worried about being locked out, or in a hurry to access an important account.

That understanding could change the design. The interface might clearly explain what will happen next, show which email or phone method will be used without exposing sensitive information, avoid unnecessary fields, provide visible confirmation after each step, and explain what to do if the recovery method is unavailable. None of those choices change the basic technical purpose of the system, but they change the experience of the person using it.

This is why I think human-centered design is strongest when empathy appears before the interface is polished. If the team waits until the end to think about users, some of the hardest usability problems may already be built into the structure of the product.

Jakob’s Law: Familiarity Reduces Learning

Jakob’s Law says that users spend much of their time using other products, so they bring expectations from familiar interfaces into new ones (Yablonski, n.d.). In practical terms, people do not approach each website with a completely empty mental model. They expect a logo to often lead home, navigation to behave consistently, familiar icons to have familiar meanings, and common controls to work in recognizable ways.

This does not mean every interface should look identical. It means innovation should have a reason. If a designer changes a familiar pattern only to appear original, the user pays the cost of learning it.

For example, if a shopping app hides the cart behind an unfamiliar symbol simply because the design team wants something unique, users may have trouble locating a basic feature. The design may be visually creative but less usable. A more human-centered decision is to preserve familiar patterns when familiarity helps people complete their goals.

I also connect Jakob’s Law to empathy. Respecting a user’s existing mental model can be a form of respect for their time. The interface does not need to prove how clever the designer is. It needs to help the user move forward.

Fitts’s Law: Important Actions Need to Be Easy to Reach

Fitts’s Law connects the time required to reach a target with its size and distance. In interface design, this means touch and click targets should be large enough, spaced appropriately, and placed where users can acquire them easily (Yablonski, n.d.).

This is especially obvious on mobile devices. A tiny “Submit” button surrounded by other small controls creates unnecessary difficulty. The problem becomes worse for someone using one hand, moving, dealing with a small screen, or having reduced motor precision.

A human-centered design would make important actions easier to select and separate destructive actions from common actions. For example, “Save” and “Delete Account” should not be tiny neighboring controls. Fitts’s Law reminds us that physical interaction is part of usability. The user is not only thinking; the user is also moving a mouse, finger, hand, or assistive device.

This principle can be considered early in wireframes. Designers do not have to wait for final colors and typography to ask whether the main actions are large, reachable, and safely spaced.

Hick’s Law: More Choice Can Mean More Work

Hick’s Law states that decision time increases as the number and complexity of choices increase (Yablonski, n.d.). This does not mean every interface should contain only a few options. It means designers should be careful about presenting many equally important choices at the same moment.

Imagine opening an employee portal and seeing thirty actions on the home screen without categories: benefits, payroll, tax forms, scheduling, time off, training, profile settings, security, documents, and many more. Even if every option is useful, presenting all of them at once can create hesitation.

A better design might group related functions, prioritize common tasks, use progressive disclosure, or guide a new user through a smaller set of choices. The goal is not to remove useful capability. It is to organize complexity so that the user does not have to process all of it at once.

I think this law is especially important because feature-rich products often confuse “having more features” with “showing more features.” Human-centered design recognizes that the system can remain powerful without making every possibility visually compete for attention.

Miller’s Law: Do Not Make Memory Do the Interface’s Job

Miller’s Law is commonly associated with limits in working memory and the idea of organizing information into meaningful chunks (Yablonski, n.d.). The most useful design lesson for me is not that every screen must contain exactly seven items. In fact, the Laws of UX guidance warns against treating the “magical number seven” as a rigid design restriction.

The stronger lesson is that designers should not require people to remember unnecessary information when the interface can keep it visible or organize it better. Consider a multi-step form. If the first screen gives the user an account number and the fourth screen asks them to type that number again from memory, the interface is creating avoidable cognitive work. The system already knows the information. It could display it when needed.

Chunking also helps. A long settings page becomes easier to understand when options are grouped into sections such as Account, Privacy, Notifications, and Security. The information has not disappeared, but its structure gives the user smaller meaningful groups to process.

Miller’s Law therefore connects directly to visual hierarchy. Grouping, headings, whitespace, and labels are not merely decorative. They reduce the mental effort required to understand a screen.

The Four Laws Work Better Together

What I find most useful about these laws is that they overlap. Imagine designing a mobile food-ordering checkout. Jakob’s Law suggests using familiar checkout patterns so users know what to expect. Fitts’s Law suggests making the primary “Place Order” action large and easy to tap. Hick’s Law suggests not overwhelming the user with unnecessary decisions during the final step. Miller’s Law suggests grouping order details, delivery information, payment method, and total into clear sections rather than expecting the user to remember details from earlier screens.

Visual hierarchy then directs attention toward the total, delivery information, and final action. Empathy adds another question: what is the user feeling at checkout? They may be hungry, distracted, worried about the price, or trying to order quickly. That context should influence which information is prominent and which decisions can be postponed.

However, I do not think UX laws should become automatic rules. Human-centered design requires context. A larger button is not automatically better if it makes other necessary information impossible to see. Fewer choices are not automatically better if important options are hidden. Familiarity is useful, but sometimes a genuinely better interaction requires change. Chunking helps, but too many layers of navigation can also create friction.

The laws are most valuable as questions: Is this familiar enough to understand? Is the target easy to reach? Are there too many choices at once? Are we asking users to remember something the system could show them? Those questions help designers identify risks early.

Designing Human First

Early-stage design choices have lasting effects. Navigation structure, terminology, content grouping, target size, workflow order, and information hierarchy may be decided before an interface looks finished, but these decisions determine how much effort the user will need later.

For me, “human first” does not mean giving users every feature they request or making a product visually simple at any cost. It means recognizing that a digital system is being used by people with limited attention, limited working memory, existing expectations, different physical abilities, different levels of experience, and different emotional contexts.

ISO 9241-210 gives human-centered design a structured place across the development life cycle, while IDEO emphasizes learning directly from people and testing ideas rather than assuming what people need (ISO, 2019; IDEO.org, 2015). Perception and visual hierarchy remind designers that what exists on a screen is not necessarily what a user will notice. Jakob’s, Fitts’s, Hick’s, and Miller’s Laws provide practical ways to think about familiarity, physical interaction, choice, and cognitive load.

Conclusion

My main takeaway is that good design often looks obvious only after someone has done the difficult work of understanding people. The best interface is not the one that asks users to pay more attention, remember more, learn more, or click more carefully. It is the one that takes human behavior seriously enough to reduce those demands.

Designing for people means accepting that the user should not have to think like the system. The system should be designed to work with the way people actually perceive, decide, remember, and act. That is what makes human-centered design more than a visual design style. It is a way of making technology fit people instead of forcing people to fit technology.
