Understanding Systems Thinking and Model-Based Systems Engineering

Introduction

Technology has become a major part of almost everything we do today. We use computers, phones, transportation systems, websites, networks, and many other technologies every day. Most of these things may look simple when we use them, but behind them there are many different parts working together. Because modern systems are becoming more complicated, it is important to understand how all of their parts connect and affect one another. This is where systems thinking and systems engineering become useful.

Before learning about systems engineering, I mostly thought that solving a problem meant finding the part that was causing the problem and fixing it. After learning more about systems thinking, I realized that this approach does not always work. Sometimes changing one part of a system can create a new problem somewhere else. Systems thinking encourages us to look at the complete system instead of focusing only on one individual part.

Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE) takes this idea even further by using models to represent and understand complicated systems. Instead of keeping important information spread across many separate documents, models can help engineers understand requirements, relationships, behaviors, and other important parts of a system. Holt and Weilkiens (2023) explain that modern systems engineering must deal with complexity, communication, and understanding. I think these ideas are especially important today because technology is becoming more connected and systems often depend on many different people, processes, and technologies.

Understanding Systems Thinking

Systems thinking is a way of looking at a problem as part of a larger system. Instead of examining every component separately, we look at how different components interact with each other. A system can contain people, technology, processes, information, hardware, software, and many other elements.

NASA explains that a system is made up of elements that work together to provide a capability or satisfy a need. The important point is that the value of the entire system comes not only from the individual parts but also from the relationships between those parts (Hirshorn et al., 2017). This helped me understand why systems thinking is sometimes described as looking at the "big picture."

A simple example is a university. A university is not just a building where students attend classes. It includes students, professors, administrators, classrooms, computers, online systems, registration systems, financial services, transportation, security, and many other parts. If the online registration system stops working, the problem can affect students, instructors, advisers, and administrators. Looking only at the computer that failed would not necessarily explain the complete problem.

The same idea applies to technology. A website, for example, may depend on servers, databases, networks, software, security systems, developers, and users. If users complain that a website is slow, there can be many possible causes. The problem could be the server, database, network connection, poorly designed software, or even a combination of these factors. Systems thinking encourages us to understand these relationships before deciding on a solution.

Why Systems Thinking Matters

One thing I found interesting about systems thinking is that a solution to one problem can sometimes create another problem. This happens because parts of a system are connected.

Imagine a company has a website that customers say is too slow. The company might immediately decide to buy faster servers. That sounds like a reasonable solution, but what if the actual problem is inefficient software or a database that is poorly designed? The company could spend a large amount of money and still have the same problem. A systems-thinking approach would first examine the relationships among the website, software, database, network, server, and users.

NASA describes systems engineering as a multidisciplinary approach covering the design, realization, technical management, operation, and eventual retirement of a system (Hirshorn et al., 2017). This is important because it shows that engineers need to think beyond simply building something. They must consider the entire life of the system.

I also think systems thinking is useful outside engineering. Businesses are systems, schools are systems, transportation networks are systems, and even communities can be viewed as systems. In a business, for example, changing employee schedules might affect productivity, customer service, employee satisfaction, and costs. A manager should consider these relationships before making a major decision.

For me, this is one of the biggest lessons from systems thinking. Problems should not always be separated from everything around them. Understanding the connections can help us make better decisions and avoid unexpected consequences.

Complexity and Communication

Another important idea from Systems Engineering Demystified is complexity. Modern systems can contain many different elements and relationships. Holt and Weilkiens (2023) discuss complexity, communication, and understanding as major concerns in systems engineering. I found communication especially important because even a technically good system can have problems if the people developing it do not understand one another.

Consider a large engineering project. Software developers may think about software requirements, electrical engineers may focus on electrical systems, managers may focus on schedules and budgets, and customers may focus on what the final product should do. All these people can be talking about the same system but looking at it differently.

This can create misunderstandings. A requirement that seems clear to one person may mean something different to another person. If the misunderstanding is discovered late in a project, fixing it could take additional time and money.

Systems engineering provides ways for teams to organize and communicate information more clearly. Instead of allowing each group to work completely independently, the goal is to understand how their work connects. This is another reason models can become valuable.

What Is Model-Based Systems Engineering?

Model-Based Systems Engineering, usually called MBSE, is an approach where models become an important part of systems engineering activities.

The Systems Engineering Body of Knowledge explains MBSE as the use of formalized system models to support systems engineering work throughout a system's life cycle. These models can support requirements, design, analysis, verification, and validation (Singam & Carter, 2026).

At first, I thought a model was simply a diagram or picture. After looking more closely at MBSE, I realized that it is much more than that. A system model can organize information about requirements, components, behaviors, relationships, and interfaces. It can help people see how the entire system fits together.

This is different from a traditional document-based approach. In a large project, information might be stored in many Word documents, spreadsheets, diagrams, emails, and presentations. If one requirement changes, several documents may need to be updated. If someone forgets to update one of them, different team members might end up working with conflicting information.

With MBSE, models can provide a more connected representation of the system. According to Singam and Carter (2026), MBSE can consolidate system information into system design models and help provide a more consistent source of technical information. To me, this seems especially useful for large projects where hundreds or thousands of requirements and components may be involved.

How Models Help Us Understand Complex Systems

Visualization is one of the parts of MBSE that I find most useful. Reading many pages of technical information can make it difficult to understand how everything is connected. A model can make those relationships easier to see.

Systems Engineering Demystified discusses modeling and visualization as important parts of MBSE. It also discusses the Systems Modeling Language, or SysML, which can be used to represent information about systems (Holt & Weilkiens, 2023).

For example, imagine designing an airplane. An airplane contains engines, electrical systems, navigation systems, communication systems, software, controls, safety equipment, and many other components. Each component also interacts with other components. A change to one area can potentially affect several others.

Using models can help engineers understand these connections before the actual system is completed. This can make it easier to identify missing requirements, conflicts, or other problems earlier in the development process.

NASA has also incorporated model-based approaches into systems engineering. Its Systems Engineering Handbook notes that MBSE is being used to improve the development and delivery of products (Hirshorn et al., 2017). This shows that MBSE is not simply a classroom idea. It has practical applications in organizations working on extremely complicated projects.

Benefits of MBSE

One major benefit of MBSE is improved communication. Different people can use a common model to understand how a system works instead of depending entirely on separate documents.

Another benefit is consistency. When information is spread across many documents, it can become difficult to determine which document contains the latest information. A model-based approach can make relationships between different pieces of information clearer.

MBSE can also support verification and validation. Verification asks whether the system was built according to its requirements, while validation considers whether the system actually satisfies its intended purpose. Models can help connect requirements to design decisions and later testing activities.

Another important benefit is managing change. Systems rarely remain exactly the same throughout development. Customers may change requirements, new technology may become available, or engineers may discover problems. When information is connected through a model, teams can have a better chance of understanding what other areas could be affected by a change.

The Systems Engineering Body of Knowledge explains that system models can act as a centralized source of system information and can help integrate knowledge across engineering disciplines (Singam & Carter, 2026). This is important because complicated systems usually require many specialists working together.

Challenges of MBSE

Even though MBSE has many advantages, I do not think it automatically solves every problem. A model is useful only when the information inside it is accurate and when people know how to use it.

One challenge is learning. Engineers and organizations that are used to traditional documents may need training before they can use MBSE effectively. Modeling languages and software tools can also take time to learn.

Another challenge is deciding how much detail should be included. A model with too little information might not be useful, while a model with too much information could become difficult to understand. Teams need to determine which information is important for their particular system.

There can also be organizational challenges. Moving from document-based engineering to MBSE is not simply a matter of purchasing new software. People may need to change how they communicate, manage information, and make decisions.

This made me realize that MBSE should be viewed as an approach rather than just a technology. Having a modeling program on a computer does not automatically mean that an organization is practicing good systems engineering. People still need clear requirements, good communication, accurate information, and an understanding of the system.

My Reflection

The most important thing I learned from this topic is that understanding relationships is just as important as understanding individual components.

Before studying systems thinking, I would usually approach a complicated problem by dividing it into smaller pieces. I still think breaking problems into smaller parts can be useful, but now I understand that we also have to reconnect those parts and examine their relationships.

I can see this being especially useful in information technology and cybersecurity. A cybersecurity problem, for example, is rarely about only one computer. Security depends on hardware, software, networks, user behavior, passwords, company policies, training, updates, and many other factors. An organization could purchase expensive security software and still experience a security incident if employees are not trained properly. Systems thinking would encourage us to examine the entire environment rather than assuming one technology can solve every problem.

MBSE adds another useful idea because it provides a structured way of representing complex information. Instead of expecting everyone to understand a large collection of independent documents, models can make relationships more visible.

The idea from Holt and Weilkiens (2023) that systems engineering must deal with complexity, communication, and understanding stood out to me. These three issues are connected. As a system becomes more complex, communicating information becomes more difficult. If communication becomes unclear, people's understanding of the system can also become different. Modeling can help reduce some of these problems by giving people a structured way to represent and discuss the system.

At the same time, I learned that the human side of systems engineering is important. Models and tools can support people, but people still have to communicate, make decisions, understand stakeholder needs, and determine whether a system actually solves the intended problem.

Conclusion

Systems thinking and Model-Based Systems Engineering provide useful ways to understand today's complicated systems. Systems thinking teaches us to look beyond individual parts and examine relationships, interactions, and the complete system. This can help us understand why problems occur and avoid solutions that fix one issue while creating another.

MBSE builds on this idea by using models to represent system information. Models can help teams organize requirements, communicate designs, understand relationships, manage changes, and support verification and validation. This becomes especially valuable as systems grow larger and involve more people and technologies.

After learning about these concepts, I see systems engineering differently. It is not simply about designing technical components. It is about understanding how people, processes, technology, requirements, and other elements work together to achieve a goal. I think this way of thinking will become even more valuable as technology continues to become more connected and complex.

The biggest lesson I am taking away is that solving complex problems requires more than understanding individual pieces. We also need to understand how those pieces influence one another. Systems thinking provides the big-picture perspective, while MBSE provides tools and models that can make that perspective easier to communicate and manage.

Thinking in Life Cycles: How Digital Systems Grow and Change

Introduction

When I first heard the term systems engineering life cycle, I thought it was mainly about the steps needed to build a product. After learning more, I realized that a system does not stop being important when it is launched. It still needs maintenance, improvements, security updates, and eventually replacement. A website, mobile app, or online banking service may look finished to users, but people behind the scenes are constantly working to keep it useful. Thinking about the whole life cycle helps engineers make better decisions from the beginning instead of only reacting when something goes wrong.

In this post, I will explain the systems development life cycle and three common ways to organize development: linear, iterative, and incremental models. I will discuss what makes each approach useful, where it can create problems, and how it connects to the future of digital systems. My main takeaway is that there is no single model that works best for every project. The right choice depends on how clear the requirements are, how much change is expected, and how important it is to receive feedback early.

What Is the Systems Development Life Cycle?

The systems development life cycle is a structured way to plan, design, build, test, operate, maintain, and eventually retire a system. The purpose is to help a team think about more than the first version of a product. Before building anything, engineers need to understand the problem and the people who will use the system. During development, they must check that the design actually meets those needs. After release, they still need to monitor performance, correct errors, and respond to new requirements. NASA (2016) describes systems engineering as work that connects technical decisions and management across the life of a system.

For example, imagine a college creates an app where students can register for classes, view grades, and pay tuition. The first step is learning what students, professors, and administrators need. Next, the team designs and builds the app, tests important functions, and launches it. But the work continues after launch. New courses appear, payment methods change, and security risks develop. Eventually the college might replace the app with a newer system. Every one of those decisions is part of the life cycle.

I think this idea matters because it encourages long-term responsibility. A team might save time by skipping documentation or testing, but that choice can create bigger problems later. Planning for maintenance and replacement can seem less exciting than launching a new product, yet it is just as important. Holt and Weilkiens (2023) connect systems engineering to the challenge of organizing complex work so that many parts can function together. A life cycle gives that work a clearer structure.

The Linear Life Cycle Model

The linear model follows a planned sequence of stages. A team usually gathers requirements first, then moves into design, development, testing, deployment, and maintenance. It is often compared with the waterfall approach because work moves mainly in one direction. Each stage has a purpose, and the team normally completes major activities in one stage before moving to the next. IBM (n.d.) explains that software development life cycle models provide different ways to organize development activities, including sequential approaches.

One advantage of the linear model is that it is easy to understand and manage. Teams can prepare schedules, budgets, and documentation before major development begins. Everyone has a clearer idea of what should happen next. This approach can work well when the requirements are stable and the project must follow strict procedures. For example, a company updating a predictable internal reporting system might already know exactly which reports and controls it needs.

However, the biggest drawback is that changes can be expensive. If users discover a major problem after development is almost finished, the team may need to revisit earlier decisions. The model can also delay useful feedback because people might not see a working product until later. Imagine that a university spends months designing a student portal and then learns that students find the navigation confusing. Fixing the design at that point could require rewriting several connected parts.

My opinion is that the linear model is useful when predictability matters more than flexibility. It can keep a project organized, but teams should not assume that an early plan will always be correct. Even a well-planned project needs communication with users and careful testing. I would be cautious about using a strictly linear approach for a new consumer app because people's expectations can change quickly.

The Iterative Life Cycle Model

The iterative model develops a system through repeated cycles. Instead of trying to get everything perfect on the first attempt, a team creates a version, tests it, collects feedback, and improves it. Each cycle helps the team learn more about what works and what needs to change. The same feature may be redesigned several times. This approach treats learning as part of development rather than as a sign that the original plan failed.

A good example is a college website that helps students find academic advising appointments. The team could first create a basic booking page and ask a small group of students to try it. Students might say that the available appointment times are difficult to read. The team could improve the layout, test it again, and later adjust the reminders. The system becomes better through repeated evaluation. This fits the broader systems engineering goal of checking whether a solution actually meets stakeholder needs (NASA, 2016).

The main advantage of iteration is flexibility. Teams can discover problems early, respond to user feedback, and improve usability before investing too much in a weak design. This is especially helpful when the problem is complex or the users are not completely sure what they need. It also gives engineers opportunities to review performance and security as the design changes.

There are drawbacks, too. Repeated changes can make schedules difficult to predict. A team might keep adjusting small details and never agree that a feature is ready. Testing the same system again and again also requires time and money. If the team does not document decisions, people may forget why an earlier design was changed. I think iteration works best when each cycle has a clear goal and a reasonable stopping point.

What I like most about this model is that it accepts mistakes as opportunities to learn. In real life, people rarely understand every requirement at the beginning. Asking users for feedback and improving the system seems more realistic than expecting the first version to be perfect. At the same time, feedback should guide meaningful improvements rather than endless redesign.

The Incremental Life Cycle Model

The incremental model builds and delivers a system in smaller, usable pieces. Instead of waiting for every planned feature to be finished, a team releases one set of functions and adds more later. Each increment expands what the system can do. This is different from iteration: iteration mainly improves or revises something, while incremental development mainly adds new capabilities. In practice, a project can use both approaches at the same time.

For example, imagine a small business creating an online shopping website. Its first release might allow customers to browse products and place orders. A second release could add order tracking. A third might introduce customer reviews, and a fourth could include personalized recommendations. Customers get value from the earlier releases while the company continues building the rest. The development team can also learn which features customers actually use.

One advantage is that useful parts of the system can become available sooner. The team can divide a large project into smaller tasks and make progress easier to see. If funding or priorities change, the business may still have a working product rather than a half-finished project. This approach can also reduce some risks because the team does not need to deliver every feature at once.

However, incremental development requires careful planning of the overall system. Features released at different times still need to work together. If the first version is built without considering future additions, the team may face integration problems. Users might also become frustrated if an important feature is promised but not delivered until much later. A small initial release should still be reliable, secure, and useful.

I think the incremental model is especially practical for digital products because people often want something they can use now rather than a perfect product years later. Still, releasing early should not mean ignoring quality. The first increment needs a strong foundation so later features can be added without breaking what already works.

Comparing the Three Models

The easiest way for me to remember the difference is to think about what each model emphasizes. Linear development emphasizes completing planned stages in order. Iterative development emphasizes revisiting a solution and making it better. Incremental development emphasizes adding working pieces over time. These models are not automatically good or bad. Each one answers a different project need.

  • Linear: Helpful when requirements are clear, documentation is important, and changes are limited. Its main risk is difficulty adapting to late discoveries.
  • Iterative: Helpful when user feedback and experimentation are important. Its main risk is repeated rework without clear limits.
  • Incremental: Helpful when a system can provide value in smaller releases. Its main risk is poor integration between new and existing features.

Consider a banking application. Some parts, such as required financial controls, may benefit from careful planning and formal approval. The design of a customer dashboard may benefit from repeated usability testing. New features, such as budgeting tools or spending alerts, may be delivered in increments. This example shows why a team may combine ideas from different models instead of following one approach rigidly.

The choice also depends on risk. A small mistake in a casual entertainment app might be inconvenient, while a mistake in a medical or financial system could have serious consequences. Teams need to consider reliability, security, costs, user needs, and the difficulty of making changes. A life cycle model is a guide for organizing decisions, not a replacement for good judgment.

Why Life Cycle Thinking Matters for Digital Systems

Digital systems constantly change because technology, user behavior, and security threats change. A website may need to support new devices. A business may need to follow updated privacy requirements. A university system may need to serve more students than it did five years ago. Thinking in life cycles helps engineers prepare for these changes instead of treating launch day as the end of the project.

Security is one important example. If developers think only about getting a product released, they might overlook how updates will be delivered or how old accounts will be removed. Later, those weaknesses could create risks for users. Life cycle thinking encourages teams to plan for monitoring, patches, backups, and responsible retirement. In my view, maintaining a secure system is an ongoing responsibility, not a one-time checklist.

Another important issue is sustainability. Digital products may seem invisible, but they rely on physical devices, data centers, electricity, and networks. A poorly designed system can waste computing resources or require expensive replacements. When engineers plan for efficiency, repair, updates, and reuse, they can reduce unnecessary costs and potentially reduce environmental impacts. The complete life cycle includes what happens when technology is no longer supported.

For example, a company might replace computers every few years because its software no longer works on older hardware. If software is designed to remain compatible with reasonable hardware for longer, the company may be able to delay some replacements. That can save money and reduce electronic waste. Of course, older devices can also become unsafe when they stop receiving security updates. Sustainable decisions must balance efficiency, safety, and long-term support rather than focusing on only one factor.

NASA's systems engineering guidance emphasizes looking across a system's life rather than treating individual stages as unrelated activities (NASA, 2016). I think this is a useful reminder that early design choices can affect years of maintenance. Holt and Weilkiens (2023) also show why complex systems need structured thinking. If many components depend on each other, even a small change can affect the larger system. Understanding those connections makes long-term decisions more responsible.

My Personal Reflection

Before this assignment, I mostly judged technology by whether it worked when I opened it. If an app loaded quickly and did what I needed, I considered it successful. Now I understand that a successful system also needs to be reliable over time, adaptable to change, and manageable for the people maintaining it. Users usually do not see the planning, testing, or maintenance behind a digital service, but those activities strongly affect their experience.

If I were part of a team developing a new student app, I would probably choose an incremental approach combined with iteration. I would start with a small set of important features, such as viewing class schedules and deadlines. After students used those features, I would collect feedback and improve confusing parts before adding more tools. This would give students something useful early while still leaving room to learn. I would also make sure security and accessibility were considered from the beginning rather than added only at the end.

One challenge I would expect is deciding when to stop improving a feature and move on. It is easy to imagine a team spending too much time making a small detail perfect. I would want the team to agree on priorities, measure whether changes help users, and document major decisions. This balance between flexibility and discipline seems important no matter which model is used.

I also learned that sustainability is not only about saving energy. It can mean building systems that remain useful, understandable, and maintainable for a long time. Good documentation, updates, and thoughtful design can help future teams work with a system instead of starting over every time something changes. That is a practical form of sustainability that I had not considered much before.

Conclusion

The systems development life cycle gives engineers a way to think about a system from its first idea through operation and eventual retirement. The linear model provides a clear sequence, the iterative model supports repeated learning and improvement, and the incremental model delivers useful capabilities in stages. Each has advantages and limitations, so choosing the right approach requires understanding the project rather than following a model automatically.

My biggest lesson is that building a digital system is not just about making something work today. It is about making decisions that support users tomorrow, next year, and sometimes much longer. When engineers consider feedback, security, maintenance, sustainability, and future change, they can create systems that are more useful and responsible throughout their lives.
